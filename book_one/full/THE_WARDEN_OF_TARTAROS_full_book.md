## Chapter One: The Tram

The tram car smelled like rust and old blood, and Kester Vaile was handcuffed to the bench across from a man he used to call sir.

"You're quiet, Kester."

Warden Bastien Orrel sat with his legs crossed, one polished boot swinging lazily in the low gravity. He was sixty, silver-haired, and wore the black dress uniform of the Hegemony Corrections Authority like a priest wore his vestments — with reverence. Behind him, through the tram's reinforced window, the surface of Tartaros scrolled past: a rust-red moon pockmarked with mining pits and the grey concrete scars of Facility Nine. Somewhere up above, past the haze, the gas giant Moloch filled half the sky like a bruised eye.

"I've got nothing to say to you," Kester said.

"You had plenty to say three days ago. To the Federation inspector. On an encrypted channel. Which I owned." Orrel smiled. It was a warm smile, the kind he used at the annual officers' dinner when he handed out commendations. "I was disappointed, Kester. I thought we were family."

"You run a death camp."

"I run a *correctional facility*." The smile didn't move. "The distinction matters."

Kester looked away. He'd been Orrel's right hand for nine years. Logistics officer. The man who signed off on the ore quotas, who rerouted the oxygen rations, who filed the incident reports that said *prisoner expired due to pre-existing condition* when the truth was the prisoner had been worked to death in a sulfur vein with a cracked suit. He'd told himself, for nine years, that he was keeping it humane. That without him it would be worse.

Then the Federation inspector had come. A woman named Adeyemi, with tired eyes and a briefcase full of subpoenas, and she'd asked him one question in the commissary that he couldn't stop hearing:

*Do you sleep, Mr. Vaile?*

He didn't. He hadn't in months.

So he'd talked. He'd given her everything — the off-books prisoners, the medical trials, the children of political dissidents listed as *administrative wards*. And now here he was, in a tram car bound for Sub-Level Four, with the warden himself as his escort.

"I'm going to offer you something," Orrel said. "Because I loved you like a son. Are you listening?"

Kester said nothing.

"I'm going to let you walk out of this tram. I'm going to give you a suit, a sidearm, and a twelve-hour head start. You run. You hide. You find one of the old survey shelters in the Ridge, you live out your days eating reclaimed protein and drinking your own piss. And I never see you again."

"And the catch."

"You tell me where the inspector's data core is." Orrel leaned forward. "That's it. One location. One sentence. And this — " he gestured at the cuffs, the tram, the descending red planet below, " — all goes away."

Kester studied him. Nine years. He knew every tell the man had. The slight tightening at the jaw when he lied. The way his left hand drifted toward his cuff when he was about to have someone killed.

His left hand was resting on his cuff.

"Bastien," Kester said quietly. "You were going to kill me either way."

The smile finally moved. It widened.

"Well," Orrel said. "Yes."

---

The tram hit the charge.

Kester had no idea who planted it — later he'd learn it was a maintenance tech named Ivo who'd been funneling scrap to the Undersiders for two years — but the explosion peeled the tram car open like a ration tin, and suddenly there was red dust and screaming atmosphere and Orrel's guards tumbling into the thin cold air.

Kester's cuffs were magnetic. They released when the tram's power died.

He didn't think. He grabbed a guard's emergency mask, clamped it over his face, and ran.

Behind him, through the dust and the sirens and the high thin wail of decompression, he heard Orrel laughing. Actually laughing. Delighted.

"RUN, KESTER!" the warden bellowed, somewhere in the red haze. "RUN, YOU BEAUTIFUL TRAITOR! OH, WE'RE GOING TO HAVE *FUN* NOW!"

---

The Undersiders found him first.

He woke up in a converted ore-processing chamber three levels below the official map of Facility Nine, with a woman pointing a rivet gun at his head. She was maybe thirty, shaved scalp tattooed with prisoner serials that weren't hers — she wore the dead's numbers like armor — and she did not look friendly.

"You're Vaile."

"Yes."

"Logistics officer Vaile. The one who signed the Wing Seven transfers."

Kester swallowed. Wing Seven had been the medical trial block. Forty-two prisoners. None of them had come back.

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she lowered the rivet gun, and said, conversationally: "My brother was in Wing Seven."

"I know," Kester said. "Tomas Reyek. Prisoner 8841-C. He was a welder. He had a daughter named Mira on Ganymede. He made it forty-one days."

Her face didn't change. But something behind her eyes did.

"How do you know that."

"Because I read every file," Kester said. "Every single one. I told myself if I remembered them, it meant something. I told myself a lot of things."

She stared at him. The rivet gun didn't come back up. But it didn't go down either.

"My name is Sable," she said finally. "And I don't forgive you. I want that clear."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Good." She holstered the gun. "Because we don't need your forgiveness. We need your access codes."

---

There's a thing that happens on prison moons, when the regime gets too comfortable. The mines go deeper than the maps. The prisoners outnumber the guards ten to one. And somewhere, in the dark arteries of the facility, a second society grows — not free, exactly, but unadministered. The Undersiders had been building theirs for a decade. Smuggling oxygen. Hiding children born to prisoners the regime had forgotten to sterilize. Stockpiling weapons made from mining equipment and salvaged guard gear.

What they didn't have was a way into the warden's citadel.

What Kester had, burned into his memory from nine years of signing off on everything, was every code, every patrol rotation, every blind spot in the security grid.

"You understand what you're asking," he told Sable, on the third night, in a chamber lit by stolen emergency lamps. "If we take the citadel, we don't just kill Orrel. We trigger the dead-man protocols. The whole facility goes into lockdown. Every prisoner in every wing, sealed in. No oxygen rotation. No food. They'll suffocate in four days."

"We know."

"Then you also know Orrel has a secondary protocol. If the citadel falls and he can't trigger lockdown himself, the reactor vents. The whole moon becomes uninhabitable for a century."

"We know that too."

"So what's the plan."

Sable smiled for the first time since he'd met her. It wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of someone who had done the math a long time ago and come out the other side of it.

"The plan," she said, "is that we don't take the citadel."

She unrolled a schematic across the table. Not of the citadel. Of the comms array on the north pole — the one that broadcast the warden's weekly address to the Hegemony, the one piece of infrastructure Orrel *personally* controlled, the one thing he loved more than the prison itself.

"We take his voice," Sable said. "We put you on that broadcast. The logistics officer, the inside man, with the files, the names, the serials. Live. Uncut. Hegemony-wide."

"He'll kill every prisoner in the facility before he lets me finish."

"Probably."

"Sable — "

"Kester." She looked at him levelly. "There are sixty thousand of us in this facility. There are four million in the Hegemony's other facilities. If we die and they hear us die, maybe the other four million live. Maybe they don't. But right now nobody out there even knows we exist. Right now, in the official record, we already died years ago."

She tapped the schematic.

"You helped make the record. Now you help unmake it."

---

He thought about Orrel's offer, in the tram, for a long time after that.

*I'm going to let you walk out.*

He could have taken it. Could have given up the inspector's data core, run to the Ridge, lived on reclaimed protein and the thin cold comfort of being alive. Orrel would have killed him eventually, sure — but *eventually* was a long word, and Kester Vaile had made a career out of stretching it.

That was the offer, really. Not freedom. Just *eventually*.

He looked at Sable, at the tattooed serials on her scalp, at the schematic of the comms array, at the low red light of Tartaros pulsing through the vents like a dying heart.

"When do we move," he said.

---

Orrel's voice came over the facility PA the next morning, warm as ever, calling Kester by name. Offering terms. Offering forgiveness. Offering the Ridge, the suit, the twelve-hour head start, *all of it still on the table, my boy, all of it still yours.*

Kester listened from a maintenance shaft beneath the comms array, with sixty pounds of improvised charges strapped to his back and a stolen guard's sidearm at his hip, and he thought: *You know what, Bastien. I think I'd rather not run.*

He started climbing.

Above him, through the grating, the red eye of Moloch watched everything.

---

## Chapter Two: The Long Climb

The maintenance shaft beneath the comms array was forty-eight meters of vertical rust, and Kester Vaile was thinking about his daughter.

He hadn't seen Lira in six years. She lived on Europa with her mother, in one of the union habitats where they grew real strawberries under sodium lamps and the children went to school on water-skids across the under-ice canals. He used to send her letters. He used to send her money. He used to tell himself that the work he did on Tartaros was *for* her, that every credit he banked was a wall against the kind of poverty that had eaten his own childhood whole.

Then she'd turned twelve, and she'd written him back exactly once, and the letter had said: *Mom told me what you do. Please don't write me anymore.*

He'd kept climbing the corporate ladder anyway. What else was there to do.

Now, with sixty pounds of charges on his back and his palms slick inside borrowed work gloves, he understood for the first time that he had never been climbing toward her. He had been climbing away.

"Vaile." Sable's voice in his earpiece, dry as bone. "You're slowing down."

"I'm fifty-three years old and I'm carrying a bomb."

"You're forty-nine."

"I feel fifty-three."

Above him, somewhere past the next grating, the comms array hummed at a frequency he could feel in his molars. Below him, three Undersiders were climbing in his wake — Sable, a wiry man named Pell who'd been a structural engineer before his arrest, and a kid called Mouse who couldn't have been more than nineteen and who carried the broadcast deck strapped to her chest like a sleeping infant.

He kept climbing.

---

The plan, as Sable had laid it out, was almost insultingly simple.

The comms array sat on the north pole of Tartaros because that was where the moon's thin magnetosphere was weakest, which meant the cleanest signal out to the Hegemony relays. Orrel had built it himself, fifteen years ago, when he was still a junior administrator with ambitions. He'd designed the security around it personally — biometric locks, redundant patrols, a kill-switch he carried on his person at all times.

What he hadn't done, because no one ever did, was secure the maintenance infrastructure underneath it.

The shaft they were climbing had been drilled by mining contractors a decade before the array was built. It wasn't on any current security schematic. The Undersiders had found it eight months ago, mapping old survey data they'd stolen from a guard's terminal, and they had been patiently, quietly, *insanely* patiently sealing off every alternate route from the maintenance side ever since. Every grate welded shut. Every camera spoofed with a looping feed. Every patrol rotation timed and memorized.

They had been waiting for a key.

Kester was the key.

His biometric signature — retina, palm, voice — was still in the system, because Orrel had wanted him alive long enough to extract the inspector's data core location. The warden had not yet had time, in the chaos of the tram bombing and Kester's disappearance into the under-levels, to revoke his clearance. Orrel was many things, but he was also a man who hated paperwork; revocation required a signed order through Hegemony Central, and Orrel preferred to do it personally, with ceremony, over a glass of the Martian brandy he kept locked in his desk.

Kester had sat across from him during three such revocations, over the years. He had watched Orrel pour the brandy with one hand and sign the death warrant with the other.

He climbed.

---

At the forty-meter mark, Pell tapped his ankle.

"Hold."

Kester froze. Above them, through the shaft's open grating, a guard's footsteps clanged across the array's lower deck. Heavy boots. Magnetic soles. The slow methodical pace of a man who had done this patrol five thousand times and expected nothing.

The footsteps stopped directly above them.

Kester held his breath. He watched, through the slats of the grating, the guard's silhouette: helmet, rifle, the dull green glow of a heads-up display reflected on a faceplate. He could smell the man's cigarettes through the ventilation. *Vorka brand*, he thought, with the strange clarity of fear. *He buys them from the commissary on credit. His name is Renko. He has a wife on Phobos. I approved his hardship leave last year so he could attend her father's funeral.*

Renko coughed. Spat. Moved on.

Kester let out a breath that tasted of metal and old fear, and kept climbing.

---

The grating at the top was sealed with a magnetic lock that pulsed a soft amber every six seconds.

Kester pressed his palm against the reader.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

*He's revoked it,* he thought. *Of course he has. He had two days. He's not stupid. He's*  —

The lock chimed green.

The grating slid open.

Kester pulled himself up into the lower service corridor of the comms array, and helped Sable up after him, and Mouse, and Pell. They crouched in the half-dark, four shapes in stolen guard fatigues, breathing hard.

"He didn't revoke you," Sable whispered.

"No."

"Why not."

Kester was quiet for a moment. Then he said: "Because he wants me to come back."

Sable looked at him.

"He's not a man who lets go of things," Kester said. "He's a collector. Every defector, every rebellion, every prisoner — he has a story about each one. He told me once, in his office, that the worst part of running the facility was that nobody ever got to *finish* their arc. They died too quickly, or they were transferred, or they broke. He wanted resolution." Kester smiled, thin and bitter. "He kept my access live because he wants the resolution. He wants me to come back to him. He wants the conversation."

"Then we'd better not give him one," Sable said.

She drew her sidearm and checked the load.

---

The broadcast room was on the fourth deck.

They moved through the array like men who knew they were already dead, which, Kester thought, was probably the correct mindset. Mouse went first because she was the smallest and quietest. Pell went second because he could read the structural load of every floor plate and tell them which ones would creak. Sable went third because she was the best shot. Kester went last because he was the bait.

If anything went wrong — if a guard rounded a corner, if a sensor tripped, if any one of a thousand things failed — the plan was that Kester would step out, hands raised, and announce himself. Logistics Officer Vaile, ready to negotiate. Orrel's people would converge on him, and the others would have a window.

He had agreed to this readily. He had not told them why.

He had not told them that he could think of no better death than buying Sable her broadcast.

---

The broadcast room door read his palm and opened.

Inside: three technicians, none armed, all of them looking up from their consoles with the slow confused expression of people whose shift had been profoundly uneventful for nine years and was about to stop being so.

Sable shot the first one in the leg before he could reach the alarm. Pell tackled the second. Mouse — sweet, nineteen-year-old Mouse, who had been born in this prison and had never seen an open sky — leveled a salvaged guard rifle at the third technician and said, with the calm of someone reading a grocery list: "Hands. Now. Or you die in here with us. Your choice."

The technician put his hands up.

"Sit down," Mouse said. "Against the wall. Don't talk."

The technicians sat.

Kester was already at the main console. His fingers moved across the interface from muscle memory — he had used this exact system, from the administrative side, every Friday for nine years, when Orrel made him review the warden's weekly address before broadcast. He knew the encryption keys. He knew the routing protocols. He knew exactly how to override the standard transmission and force the signal out to every Hegemony relay simultaneously, on every band, with no preamble and no possibility of recall.

It would take ninety seconds to set up.

It would take Orrel less than that to notice.

"Mouse," he said. "Deck. Now."

Mouse handed him the broadcast deck. He plugged it in. The system handshake completed. The transmission queue cleared.

Above them, somewhere in the citadel six kilometers away, a red light was beginning to pulse on Bastien Orrel's desk.

---

The first alarm went off at the seventy-second mark.

It wasn't a klaxon — Orrel hated klaxons, considered them undignified — but a slow pulsing red wash across the broadcast room's lights, accompanied by a calm female voice that said, with infinite courtesy: *Unauthorized transmission detected. Please cease activity and remain in place. Security has been notified.*

"How long," Sable said.

"Three minutes until the first response team," Kester said. "Six until Orrel himself."

"He's coming personally?"

"He'll come personally. He won't be able to help himself."

The transmission queue chimed: READY.

Kester turned to look at Sable. She was bleeding from the temple — a graze from a ricochet during the technician scuffle, though he hadn't seen it happen — and her pupils were wide and her hands were steady and she was, he realized with a strange detached clarity, the most alive person he had ever seen.

"You should do it," he said. "Not me."

"What?"

"The broadcast. It should be your voice."

"Kester —"

"They know my name in the Hegemony, Sable. They know me as Orrel's man. The logistics officer. The one who signed the papers. If I speak, they'll dismiss it — a cornered bureaucrat trying to save his skin by selling out his boss. They've heard that story before." He pushed the deck toward her. "But they haven't heard yours. The woman whose brother died in Wing Seven. The prisoner who organized sixty thousand people in the dark. *That's* the story they need."

Sable stared at him.

"I came here to die for this," she said quietly. "Not to be its face."

"I know. But you don't get to choose, anymore, what this costs you. None of us do." He tapped the deck. "Speak."

For a long moment she didn't move.

Then she sat down at the console, and pulled the microphone toward her, and said, without preamble:

"My name is Sable Reyek. I am a prisoner at Hegemony Correctional Facility Nine, on the moon Tartaros. The official record says I died in a labor accident in 2371. The official record is a lie. There are sixty thousand of us here. Most of us are listed as deceased. Some of us were never listed at all. I'm going to tell you what happens to us. I'm going to tell you who runs this place. And I'm going to keep talking until they kill me, so I would suggest you listen quickly."

She kept talking.

And six kilometers away, in a citadel lit by red emergency strobes, Warden Bastien Orrel set down his glass of Martian brandy, very carefully, and reached for the kill-switch he kept in his vest pocket.

---

*[end of chapter two]*


## Chapter Three: The Glass

Sable was forty-one seconds into the broadcast when the door to the room blew inward.

It came off its hinges in two pieces, and the man who came through it was not a Hegemony Corrections guard. He was something else — a tall figure in matte black armor that didn't reflect the emergency lights, with a rifle that had no manufacturer's mark and a helmet whose faceplate showed nothing at all. *Internal Security*, Kester thought, with the strange calm of a man recognizing a horror he had heard about but never seen. *Orrel's personal detail. They never leave the citadel.*

They had left the citadel.

Pell was the first one through the door. He took the lead operator at the knees with a length of pipe — a structural engineer's instinct, *break the load-bearing joint* — and the operator went down hard, and Pell shouted "MOVE" without looking back, and the second operator put two rounds through Pell's chest before Pell's body had finished turning.

Pell hit the floor.

He was not dead. Kester could see his eyes still tracking, his mouth working around something that might have been a word. But he was not getting up.

"PELL — "

"KEEP TALKING," Pell said. Or tried to. It came out as a wet sound. He fumbled at his belt with hands that had stopped being able to grip things, and produced a small grey cylinder, and he raised it the way a man raises a glass of wine in a toast, and he said clearly, with the last of the air in his lungs:

"For Tomas."

He pulled the pin.

Mouse moved faster than Kester had ever seen anyone move. She tackled Sable backward over the broadcast console — the microphone trailing wires, Sable still mid-sentence, *they call it the Cleanse and they say it's about justice but* — and the charge in Pell's hand went off, and the second operator was simply gone, and the third was on fire, and the first was screaming because his armor had caught the worst of the blast and his armor was not coming off.

The broadcast room filled with white smoke and the smell of meat.

"Up," Sable was saying. "Up, up, up — Kester, the deck, the *deck* — "

He grabbed the broadcast deck off the console. The transmission light was still green. The signal was still going out. *They didn't kill it,* he thought, dazed. *Pell bought us the line.*

Mouse was already at the back of the room, pulling at a service panel.

"This way," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. There was blood on her face but Kester didn't think it was hers. "Sable. Vaile. *Now.*"

---

The service panel opened onto a maintenance crawl that ran beneath the array's primary deck — the kind of access tunnel that existed only on schematics no current employee was supposed to have. Mouse had memorized those schematics, apparently, because she went into the dark without hesitating.

Kester followed her. Sable came last, the broadcast deck strapped to her back now, still transmitting on its own internal antenna — weaker signal, lower bandwidth, but *signal*. They had thirty minutes of battery. Maybe forty. Long enough for the message to keep finding relays.

Long enough.

Behind them, in the ruined broadcast room, Pell's body was still warm.

---

"Where," Kester said. "Where are we going."

"East lock." Mouse's voice came back over her shoulder. "Pressure hatch. Seals to the under-levels. There's a tram — older than the upper system, off the books. The Undersiders ran it for years before they stopped using it. It still works."

"How do you know it still works."

"I checked it last week."

"You — "

"I check it every week, Vaile. We've been planning this for *months*. Just keep moving."

He kept moving.

---

The crawl ran for two hundred meters and dropped them into a service lock he had genuinely never known existed. Mouse opened it with a keycard she had taken off Pell's body without breaking stride, and Kester watched her do it and felt something twist in his chest that he could not name.

She was nineteen.

She was nineteen and she had taken the keycard off the dead man without looking at his face, because she had known him for years and she had also known, in some operational compartment of her mind, that this was going to happen and she had decided in advance that she would not look. Kester thought of his daughter on Europa. Lira would be twenty-two now. He thought of the strawberries under sodium lamps. He thought of the way Mouse moved — competent, calm, terrible — and he understood that there was no version of this story where she got to grow into the woman she might have been if she had been born somewhere else.

He hated, in that moment, the man who had built her into this. He hated, in that moment, every version of himself who had helped.

The lock cycled green. The hatch opened.

Beyond it: a low corridor, red emergency lighting, and at the end of it, the tram.

---

The tram was an ugly thing — a freight unit, two cars, no seats, the kind of vehicle that had moved ore and prisoners and equipment in some earlier era and been forgotten when the upper system came online. Mouse climbed into the operator's seat. Sable climbed into the rear car with the broadcast deck. Kester was about to climb in after her when the lights changed.

Not the emergency strobes. Something else. The corridor's main lights came up — full white, full power — and a calm female voice said, with infinite courtesy:

*Please remain in place. The Warden is approaching.*

Kester turned.

At the far end of the corridor, a hundred meters away, a blast door was opening.

Behind it: Bastien Orrel. Walking. Unhurried. Six of his Internal Security team in formation behind him. He had not put on armor. He was still in his black dress uniform. He held something in his right hand that Kester recognized, with a slow sick lurch, as the kill-switch.

"Mouse," Kester said, very quietly. "Drive."

"The tram needs forty seconds to charge."

"*Drive.*"

"I can't — Vaile, I can't, the rails won't take voltage until — "

Orrel was halfway down the corridor.

Kester looked at the hatch beside him. It was a pressure door — heavy, manual override, lockable from inside. If they got it shut and sealed before Orrel reached them, Orrel would have to cut through. That would take his team minutes. Minutes they could spend getting the tram live.

"Sable," he said. "Help me with the hatch."

Sable jumped down from the rear car. Together they hauled the hatch toward its frame. It was heavy — designed for vacuum, designed to hold against pressure differentials that would crush a man — and it moved on rails that had not been used in a decade and protested every centimeter.

Orrel was forty meters away.

"Faster," Sable said.

"It's stuck — "

"FASTER, Kester — "

Twenty meters.

The hatch slammed home. Sable threw the manual lock. The bolts engaged with a sound like a bank vault closing. Kester stepped back, breathing hard, and turned to look through the hatch's small porthole — a pane of bulletproof glass the size of his palm, set at face height.

Bastien Orrel was on the other side of it.

He had stopped walking. He was perhaps two meters from the hatch. He was looking through the glass directly into Kester's eyes, and he was *smiling*.

It was not a triumphant smile. It was not a furious smile. It was the smile of a man who had been looking forward to a conversation for nine years and had just been told, politely, that the conversation was going to be deferred. There was disappointment in it. There was also — and this was the part that stayed with Kester for the rest of his life — there was *amusement*.

Orrel raised one hand, slowly, and pressed his palm flat against the glass.

He mouthed something. Kester could not hear him through the door, but he could read the shape of it on the man's lips:

*Hello, Kester.*

Then Orrel's eyes moved. Past Kester. Toward the tram. Toward the rear car. Toward —

"Mouse," Orrel said. Loud enough that the word carried even through the hatch, muffled but clear. "Mouse, Mouse, Mouse. There you are."

Mouse, in the operator's seat, went very still.

"Don't look at him," Sable said. "Mouse. Don't — "

But Mouse was looking at him.

And Orrel was smiling at her, through the glass, with the warm paternal smile he had used at officers' dinners for nine years, and he was speaking — not to Kester, not to Sable, but to the nineteen-year-old in the tram operator's seat — and what he was saying was this:

"Sweetheart. You don't really want to leave."

Mouse said, in a voice so small it was almost a child's: "Sable."

"Mouse, *don't listen to him* — "

"Sable, he's in my head."

Kester turned. Mouse was holding the side of her temple. Just behind her ear. There was a small raised scar there that he had never noticed before because he had never looked, because nobody ever looked at the back of a prisoner's neck, because that was where the Hegemony put the *behavioral compliance implants* in the under-eighteens, the children born in custody, the ones who had grown up inside the system —

Oh god, Kester thought. *Oh god.*

"Sweetheart," Orrel said, through the glass, in his warm dinner-party voice. "Sweetheart, come here."

Mouse stood up.

"MOUSE!" Sable was running for the tram now, but the corridor was twenty meters and the rear car was at the far end, and Mouse was already climbing down, and Mouse was walking — slowly, dreamily, as if through deep water — back toward the hatch.

"Mouse, *stop* — "

"I can't," Mouse said. She was crying. She was crying and she was still walking. "Sable, I can't, I'm trying, I'm — "

"There's a girl," Orrel said softly. "There's my good girl."

"Override it," Kester said. He had Sable by the shoulders. He was shaking her. "Sable, the implant, can you override it — "

"Not from here. Not without the master code. Vaile — "

"Then OPEN THE HATCH."

"*What?*"

"Open it. We hold them off. We get her back."

Sable was staring at him.

"Kester, if we open that hatch he kills all three of us in twelve seconds."

"Then we — "

"And he takes her *anyway*."

Mouse had reached the hatch. She was pressing her own palm against the glass, on her side, mirroring Orrel's. She was looking through the glass at Orrel with an expression that was not love and not fear and not anything Kester had a word for — it was the expression of a child who had been told her whole life that this man was her father and was now, in some compartment of herself that the implant could not reach, *realizing it for the first time*.

"Don't be afraid," Orrel said gently, through the glass. "It's only me."

"I know," Mouse whispered.

"Open the hatch, sweetheart. From your side. The override is in the panel."

Mouse's hand moved toward the panel.

"MOUSE."

It was Sable. Sable, with her broadcast still transmitting on her back, Sable with blood on her face and her brother's serial tattooed on her scalp and her voice cracking around the word — *Mouse*, just the name, just the girl's name, the way you say a name when it is the only thing left that you can give someone.

Mouse stopped.

Her hand hovered over the override.

She turned her head — *fought* to turn her head, Kester could see the muscles in her neck straining against something invisible — and she looked at Sable. Just for a moment. Just for one second.

And she said, with what was clearly the very last piece of herself she had any control over:

"The deck. Take the deck. Don't let him have the deck."

Then her hand pressed the override.

The hatch's manual lock disengaged with a soft chime.

But it didn't open. Not yet. The bolts had retracted but the door was heavy and Orrel's team would still need a moment to haul it back, and in that moment Sable did the only thing she could do — she grabbed Kester by the collar and threw him toward the tram, and she ran after him, and the tram's charge cycle hit forty seconds and the rails went hot and Mouse, on the other side of the hatch, was already opening it for the man who had owned her since before she was old enough to know the word for it.

The tram lurched forward.

The last thing Kester saw, looking back through the rear car's grate, was the hatch swinging open and Bastien Orrel stepping through it. Orrel did not look at the tram. Orrel did not raise his sidearm. Orrel knelt down, slowly, in front of the nineteen-year-old girl who was sobbing now without sound, and he opened his arms, and Mouse walked into them.

Orrel held her.

He held her the way a father holds a daughter who has come home from a long trip.

And as the tram pulled them away into the dark, Kester saw Orrel lift his eyes — over Mouse's shoulder, through the receding light of the corridor, directly into Kester's face — and *wink*.

---

The tram ran for nine kilometers in silence.

Sable sat against the rear wall of the operator's car with her knees drawn up and the broadcast deck in her lap. The transmission light was still green. Somewhere out there, the signal was still finding relays. Somewhere out there, in the Hegemony, people were beginning to hear what she had said about Wing Seven and the forty-one days and the brother named Tomas.

She was not crying. Her eyes were dry and very far away.

Kester sat across from her.

He wanted to say something. He wanted to say *I'm sorry* or *we'll get her back* or *Pell saved us* or any of the things you say after a thing like this, but he understood, with the dim clarity of a man who had finally run out of excuses, that none of those things were his to say. Not yet. Not for a long time.

So he said, instead, the only true thing he had:

"He used a word, Sable. When she was walking back. He said it once and I don't think I was supposed to hear it."

She looked up. "What word."

"*Calderon.*"

Sable's eyes narrowed. "What does that mean."

"I don't know yet," Kester said. "But I'm going to find out."

The tram rolled on, into the deeper dark, beneath the rust-red moon, toward the under-levels where the Undersiders waited and the long work of putting the pieces together would begin.

Above them, in the citadel, Bastien Orrel was already pouring two glasses of brandy.

He set one of them down across the desk from himself, in front of an empty chair, and he raised his own glass to it, and he smiled.

"Soon," he said, to no one. "Soon."

*[end of chapter three]*


## Chapter Four: The Things He Signed

The under-levels of Facility Nine were colder than the upper wings, and quieter, and they smelled of mineral water and old machine oil, and Kester Vaile sat on a folding chair beneath a hanging worklight and tried to remember every piece of paperwork he had signed in nine years.

It was not going well.

He had a stub of pencil and a stack of salvaged ration cards — the Undersiders' substitute for paper, harvested from decades of waste — and he was trying to build a list. Names. Dates. Document categories. Anything he could pull from memory that might mean something to Sable when she came back from wherever she had gone to be alone.

He had written *Wing Seven*. He had written *external consultants*. He had written *Calderon*, three times, in three different places on three different cards, as if writing it more would make him understand it more. It had not.

He had also written a name. *Dr. Sennae Vorlith.* He had stared at the name for a long time before writing it down, because he had not thought of it in seven years and he was not entirely sure he had not invented it. But the more he held it in his mind, the more solid it became. She had come to Tartaros twice a year for six years. She had stopped coming sometime around 2369. He had filed her travel vouchers personally. He had once shared an elevator with her and she had said *good morning, Mr. Vaile*, and her voice had been soft and academic and entirely unmemorable, and he had filed her vouchers for three years afterward without ever once asking what a Dr. Sennae Vorlith was a doctor *of*.

He wrote her name on the card.

He underlined it.

He did not yet know why.

---

Sable was somewhere in the chamber's east wing, doing whatever a person does when they have lost a brother and a friend and a girl they had raised in five years instead of a mother. Kester had not gone after her. He had understood, with the dim clarity of a man who had spent his life avoiding rooms where grief was being conducted, that there was nothing he could offer her except the absence of his face.

So he sat with the pencil and the ration cards and the worklight and tried to remember.

It came back the way memory always came back to him — sideways, in fragments, prompted by the wrong things. The smell of the under-levels' machine oil reminded him of a service report he had filed in 2367 about a malfunctioning autoclave in the medical wing. The autoclave had been running hot for months. The report had recommended replacement. Replacement had been denied. He had refiled the report three times before the request was approved, by which point the autoclave had been running hot for two years, and Kester had not asked why an autoclave running hot was acceptable for two years in a medical wing that handled human tissue.

Now, in the cold of the under-level, he asked.

*Because they didn't want it sterile,* his mind answered, in a voice that was perhaps his own and perhaps not. *Because whatever they were running through it, sterilization would have damaged.*

He wrote *autoclave 2367* on a ration card.

He underlined it.

---

Pell's body was still in the broadcast room.

This was a fact Kester could not stop returning to. The Hegemony would have collected it by now — would have photographed it, catalogued it, filed it under *insurgent killed in lawful response to terrorist action*, and incinerated it in one of the facility's ten thousand efficient procedures. Pell had a daughter on the home world. Kester knew this because Pell had mentioned her, once, in the maintenance crawl, and Kester had nodded and said nothing because he had not wanted to be the kind of man who knew Pell's daughter's name. The man had bought him forty seconds of broadcast with his life. The least Kester owed him was the discomfort of remembering that he had not asked.

He wrote *Pell — daughter — name?* on a card.

He set the card aside.

---

There were people in the chamber with him, though he had been ignoring them. Three Undersiders Sable had introduced him to before she walked away — a woman called Tev who had been a nurse before her arrest, a man called Roon who had been nothing in particular before his arrest and was now apparently the one who managed the chamber's air recyclers, and a thin elderly man called Chayne who Kester suspected had been a teacher of some kind in his previous life and who was watching Kester with an expression of mild, patient curiosity that Kester found unbearable.

"Mr. Vaile," Chayne said, eventually.

Kester looked up from his cards.

"You're trying to remember things."

"Yes."

"Would you like help."

Kester opened his mouth to say no, and then he stopped, and he thought about it.

"Yes," he said. "I would."

Chayne pulled a folding chair over and sat down. He moved like a man who was in pain and had been for a long time, and who had decided years ago not to mention it. He set his hands on his knees. His knuckles were swollen.

"I was a records officer," Chayne said. "Before. On Korya. I worked in municipal archives for thirty-one years."

"Why are you here."

"Because in 2358 I refused to redact a death certificate." Chayne smiled, very slightly. "It was a small thing. A boy had drowned in a canal. The cause of death was negligence — there was a broken safety rail, the city had been notified, the city had not repaired it. I was asked to change the cause of death to *misadventure*. I refused. I was given three opportunities to reconsider. On the third opportunity I was arrested."

"For refusing to redact a death certificate."

"For sedition, technically. The official record is more colorful than the truth usually is." Chayne folded his hands. "Tell me about the documents you signed."

Kester looked at him for a long moment.

Then he started talking.

---

It took him three hours.

He talked about the medical wing's autoclave. He talked about the consultants whose vouchers he filed. He talked about Wing Seven and the forty-two prisoners who went in and the zero who came out. He talked about Dr. Sennae Vorlith, who had said *good morning* in an elevator. He talked about a budget line item called *Substrate Maintenance* that had appeared in the wing's quarterly reports for nine years and that he had assumed meant something about food preparation and had never once verified.

He talked about Calderon. He told Chayne what little he knew — that Orrel had been there, that it had been a battle or a campaign or a meatgrinder, that sixteen thousand men had died there. That Orrel had said the word, once, in the corridor, when Mouse was walking back. *Calderon*. As if it explained something. As if it were a justification.

Chayne listened.

Chayne wrote nothing down. He simply listened, with his hands folded on his knees, and his expression did not change, and when Kester finally ran out of words Chayne sat in silence for a long time before speaking.

"Mr. Vaile," Chayne said. "I would like to tell you what I think you have just described. You may correct me if I am wrong."

"All right."

"You have described a research program. A long one. At least nine years, more likely longer — you came in partway through it, and the autoclave was already running hot before you arrived, which suggests the program predates your tenure. The research has had at least one named principal — your Dr. Vorlith — and probably others you didn't notice because they were better at being unnoticed. The program has consumed prisoners. Specifically, the program has consumed adult prisoners, in batches, with high mortality. You have signed paperwork that supported the program's logistics — supply routing, personnel travel, budget allocations under euphemistic line items — for nine years. You believed, for nine of those years, that the paperwork was administrative."

"Yes."

"It was not administrative. It was procurement."

Kester closed his eyes.

"And the word your warden used in the corridor," Chayne continued, gently, "*Calderon*. I do not know the word. But I know what it sounds like. It sounds like a place a man survived. It sounds like a place a man came back from with a particular set of conclusions about the value of human bodies in the operations of large institutions. I think your warden has been working, for a long time, on a project that he believes will repay his survival with interest. I think the prisoners in Wing Seven were not being killed. I think they were being *spent*."

"On what."

"On the question," Chayne said, "of how to make a body that the next Calderon cannot kill."

Kester sat very still.

"Weapons," he said, after a long time.

"Or something close to it. Soldiers. Tools. Augmented infantry. The terminology will be technical and the technology will be ugly, but the substance, I suspect, is that simple. The Hegemony has wars coming. Your warden is building the answer. Your prisoners are the iterations." Chayne paused. "I am sorry, Mr. Vaile. I think this is what you have been part of."

Kester did not say anything for a long time.

When he did, he said: "I want to be sure."

"I would not be sure on the strength of one conversation in a cold room. But you have the records. Or you can get to them. The Undersiders have been pulling administrative documents for years — most of them are useless to us, but if you know what you are looking for, the documents will tell you. Look for the consultants. Follow Dr. Vorlith's vouchers. She came twice a year for six years; she went somewhere on Tartaros and she went home again. Where did she go. Who did she see. What was her clearance. What was her department of origin." Chayne's hands were still folded. His eyes were very tired. "The answer to those questions will tell you what was in Wing Seven. And once you know what was in Wing Seven, you will know what your warden has been building."

"And then?"

"And then," Chayne said, "you will know what to do. Or you will know that there is nothing to do. Both are possible. Both are answers."

He stood up, slowly, with the care of a man whose joints had been negotiating with him for a decade.

"I will leave you to your cards, Mr. Vaile. If you would like my help again, you have only to ask. I am not going anywhere."

He walked away.

Kester sat with his pencil and his ration cards and his worklight and his understanding, fragile and provisional and almost certainly correct, of what he had spent nine years doing.

He did not weep. He had wept earlier, on the tram, when nobody could see him, and he had decided that further weeping was a luxury he could not yet afford. There was work to do. Chayne had told him what the work was. The work was to verify, and once he had verified, the work would be to expose — or to act, or to abandon, or whichever of the three turned out to be possible. The work was simple in shape and would be very hard in execution.

He picked up his pencil.

He wrote, on a fresh ration card, in capital letters: *VORLITH — DEPT OF ORIGIN.*

He underlined it.

He kept writing.

---

Sable came back to the chamber four hours later.

She looked, Kester thought, like a person who had walked a great distance through cold weather and was now indoors but not yet warm. She sat down across from him without speaking, and she looked at the small forest of ration cards on the table between them — names, dates, line items, underlines — and she said, quietly:

"What do you have."

Kester did not lie. He did not minimize. He did not soften.

He told her what Chayne had told him. He told her he believed it. He told her he was going to verify it, and then he was going to find Dr. Sennae Vorlith, and then he was going to figure out what came after.

Sable listened.

When he was finished, she said: "Soldiers."

"Yes."

"He's making soldiers out of the people he kills."

"Out of their bodies. Or — I don't know yet. Augmenting. Iterating. I don't know the technology. But yes. Something like that."

She nodded, slowly. She did not seem surprised. She seemed, in fact, like a person who had suspected something like this for a long time without permitting herself to name it.

"My brother," she said.

"Sable — "

"He was a welder. He had calloused hands. He had a back that hurt him in cold weather because he'd broken a vertebra in a fall when he was twenty-two. He was not — " Her voice did not break. She was not crying. But the words were coming out very carefully, like a person setting down glass. "He was not the kind of man you would build a soldier out of. He was forty-three. His body was already failing. Why him."

Kester thought about it.

"I don't know," he said. "But I think it matters that you asked. I think — Sable, I think when I figure out what the program is, I will also figure out why your brother. And I think the answer will not be what we expect."

Sable looked at him for a long time.

Then she said: "We need a next move."

"I know."

"The broadcast went out. The Hegemony is going to respond. We have maybe seventy-two hours before they hit the under-levels — they know we're down here, they've always known, they'll be moving units now. Mouse is in the citadel and she is — " Her voice caught, just for a moment, on the name. She kept going. "Mouse is alive. I have to believe that. Which means we have a window, and the window is closing, and we need to know what we are doing before it closes."

"What do you want to do."

"I want," Sable said, "to know what is in Wing Seven."

"That's where I want to start."

"Then we start there. Tonight. Chayne can help with the records. Tev knows the medical wing's old layout — she nursed there for two years before she was transferred. Roon can get us power and air for as deep as we need to go." Sable rubbed her face. "We have two objectives now, Kester. Mouse and the truth. If we can get one we can probably get both. If we can't get either we are going to die down here in the dark."

"Yes."

"You understand."

"I understand."

She nodded.

She stood up, slowly, the way Chayne had stood up — like a person whose body was now a tool she was using rather than a body she was inhabiting — and she walked to the chamber's far wall, where the broadcast deck was charging on a salvaged power coupler. She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she said, without turning around:

"Pell's daughter is named Iyani. She's eleven. She lives on the home world with his sister. He never told you because he didn't trust you yet."

Kester closed his eyes.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Just remember her name. That's the price of him."

"Iyani."

"Iyani."

He wrote it on a ration card.

He underlined it.

---

That night — though night was an abstraction in the under-levels, where the only light was electric and the only clock was the rotation of the air recyclers — Kester lay on a thin pallet in a converted storage chamber and could not sleep.

He kept thinking about Mouse.

Not about Mouse in the corridor, with her hand on the override and Orrel through the glass. He had stopped allowing himself to think about that, because thinking about it produced nothing but a kind of cold sick paralysis that helped no one. He was thinking, instead, about Mouse in the broadcast room. The way she had pointed her stolen rifle at the technician and told him, calmly, to sit down. The way she had moved through the maintenance crawl in the dark like she had been born underground, which, he realized now, she had been.

He was thinking about how she was nineteen.

He was thinking about how Bastien Orrel had said *sweetheart* in a voice that had not been the voice of a captor.

There was something in the shape of that, something he could not yet put words to, something that did not fit cleanly with what Chayne had said about adult prisoners and augmented infantry. Mouse was not an adult prisoner. Mouse had been *born* in the prison. Orrel had known her — *known her well*, known her by name, known her with a familiarity that a warden does not have for an ordinary inmate — for a long time. Years. Maybe her whole life.

*Sweetheart.*

There was a piece of the picture he had not yet placed. He could feel it, sitting somewhere in the back of his mind, the way you can feel the absence of a tooth with your tongue. It was small and it was wrong-shaped and it did not match anything else he had assembled.

He tried to make it fit Chayne's theory. *Augmented infantry. Weapons made of men.* Mouse was not a man. Mouse was a girl. Mouse was a girl who had been born in this prison and had grown up calm and competent and — 

He stopped.

He did not finish the thought.

He turned over on his pallet and faced the wall and told himself, with the discipline of a man who had spent nine years not finishing thoughts, that he would think about it tomorrow. Or next week. Or when the rest of the picture was clearer. There was no point chasing a single piece when the whole was still fragmentary. He would come back to it.

He almost believed himself.

Above him, six kilometers away in the citadel's medical wing, a nineteen-year-old girl was being prepared for something he did not yet have the language to describe. Her implant was being adjusted. Her vital signs were being logged. A woman whose name he had written on a ration card four hours earlier — *Dr. Sennae Vorlith*, who had been said to have stopped coming to Tartaros in 2369 — was reviewing Mouse's file and making small annotations in a hand that had not changed in twenty years.

The annotations used keywords. Not sentences. *Cohort retention. Substrate viability. Threshold response.* The keywords had been chosen carefully, decades ago, by people who had understood that the right phrase, repeated often enough in the right documents, becomes invisible to anyone without the cipher.

Kester had filed those phrases for nine years.

He had not yet realized that he had been carrying the cipher in his hands the entire time.

He fell asleep, finally, just before the air recyclers cycled into their dawn rotation. He dreamed of his daughter on Europa, walking on water-skids across the under-ice canals, holding a strawberry up to the sodium lamps to see the light through it. In the dream she turned to him and smiled, and her smile was Mouse's smile, and he woke up before he could decide what that meant.

---

*[end of chapter four]*


## Chapter Five: The Cipher

The first reports came through the under-levels' stolen feed at oh-six-hundred local time, which was somewhere around mid-afternoon on the home world, which meant that on Andra Prime — the great terraced city where Kester's daughter had been born and where he had not been in eleven years — people were eating their second meal of the day and watching the news on their wall-screens.

They were watching, specifically, an emergency broadcast from the Hegemony Information Office.

Sable, Kester, Chayne, Tev, Roon, and a half-dozen other Undersiders were crowded around the cracked salvaged display in the chamber's east alcove. The feed kept stuttering. The audio was running half a second behind the picture. None of that mattered.

A woman in the gray uniform of the Information Office was reading from a prepared statement. Behind her, a still photograph of Bastien Orrel filled most of the screen — a younger Orrel, Kester noted with a small distant shock, perhaps fifteen years younger, in formal Hegemony Corrections dress, accepting some commendation from a man whose face had been carefully cropped out of the frame.

"— and so the Information Office can today confirm," the woman was saying, "that following the unauthorized broadcast received from Hegemony Correctional Facility Nine yesterday, an internal investigation has been opened into Warden Bastien Orrel's administration of the facility. Warden Orrel has been formally suspended pending the investigation's outcome. The Hegemony takes the allegations made in the broadcast — though we caution that they originated from convicted criminals and remain unverified — with the utmost seriousness. A reform commission has been convened. Federation observers will be invited to participate. Affected families will receive contact from the Office of Penal Reconciliation in the coming weeks."

The Undersiders watched in silence.

"Warden Orrel," the woman continued, "has cooperated fully with the suspension order and is currently in the custody of Hegemony Internal Affairs at an undisclosed core-world facility. He has waived his right to public statement during the investigation. The Hegemony assures the public that this matter will be addressed with rigor, transparency, and respect for the rights of all parties, including the deceased."

The picture changed. A new graphic appeared. The graphic was clean, professional, and read: **PENAL REFORM 2371: A NEW CHAPTER.**

Tev made a small, ugly sound in the back of her throat.

"That's — " Sable said. "That's it?"

"That's the first wave," Chayne said quietly. "There will be more. They will not address Wing Seven directly for several days. They will let the news cycle find its shape, and then they will release a 'preliminary finding' that confirms abuses and identifies a specific subset of personnel responsible. They will arrest five or six guards. They will name Orrel as the central figure but decline to comment further pending trial. They will offer compensation to the families of the named dead. They will be very, very careful with the language. By the end of the second week, the story will be a *reform story* rather than an *atrocity story*. By the end of the month, it will be a story about how well the Hegemony self-corrects."

"You sound like you've watched this happen before," Roon said.

"I have," Chayne said. "Many times. From the inside."

Kester said nothing. He was looking at the photograph of the young Orrel that was still ghosting in the corner of the display. *Cooperated fully with the suspension order.* Of course Orrel had cooperated. Orrel had probably written the suspension order himself, three years ago, against the contingency of exactly this. The man did not do anything without preparing the script for its eventual exposure. He had been ready for the broadcast. He had been ready for the public response. He was, even now, sitting in some pleasant room on a core world drinking tea and waiting for the second wave to break.

"Mouse," Sable said.

"What?"

"They didn't mention Mouse. Or Pell. Or any of the under-levels. They named — they named our broadcast as 'unauthorized' and then they pivoted. They didn't say *prisoners attacked the comms array*. They didn't say *insurgents seized the broadcast*. Why."

"Because we don't exist," Chayne said. "The under-levels do not officially exist. Mouse does not officially exist. If they acknowledge any of it, they have to acknowledge all of it. So they will let the broadcast be the work of 'rogue elements' within the prisoner population — vague, unspecified, no organizational depth — and they will quietly continue to pretend the under-levels are not real. The reform commission will not visit us. The Federation observers will not be brought down here. We are exactly as invisible today as we were yesterday."

"Then the broadcast did nothing."

"The broadcast did a great deal," Chayne said. "It did not do what we hoped. Those are different things."

---

The second item on the feed, twenty minutes later, was a brief note from the Federation Press Bureau.

It expressed condolences for the loss of Special Inspector Adeyemi Ohaeri, whose courier shuttle had suffered a catastrophic engine failure on its return trajectory from the Moloch system. The shuttle's automated logs had been recovered. Mechanical fault was suspected. A full investigation would be conducted. The Inspector's family had been notified.

There was a photograph. Adeyemi had been younger than Kester remembered. She had been wearing a wedding ring he had not noticed in the commissary. She had, the bureau noted, been thirty-eight years old and survived by a husband and two children.

Sable did not say anything. Nobody said anything. Tev stood up and walked away from the display, into a side corridor, and Kester heard her hit something with the flat of her hand — once, hard, the sound of a person who had run out of other ways to express what she was feeling — and then she came back and sat down again.

"We knew," Chayne said gently. "We knew this would happen."

"Knowing," Tev said, "and watching it happen on the news are not the same thing."

"No," Chayne agreed. "They are not."

Kester thought: *Do you sleep, Mr. Vaile.*

He thought: *I am going to find every person responsible for that engine failure and I am going to make sure their names appear in something more permanent than a press release.*

He did not say this out loud. He was learning, slowly, that some things did not need to be said to count.

---

The records came an hour later.

Roon brought them down on a salvaged data tablet — the Undersiders had been quietly siphoning administrative documents from the facility's network for years, storing them on cold drives buried in the under-level's structural concrete, and Kester had given Chayne a list of what to look for the night before. Chayne had given the list to Roon. Roon had spent six hours pulling files.

There were a lot of files.

"Vorlith first," Kester said.

Roon scrolled. "Sennae Vorlith, Doctor of — " He squinted at the screen. "Doctor of *applied biosystemic integration*, that's a department I've never heard of. University of Cresswell, on Andra Prime. Hegemony Research Council, Tier Three clearance. Travel records to Tartaros, twice yearly, 2363 through 2369. After 2369, no travel records to Tartaros at all."

"Where did she go after 2369."

"Doesn't say. The Council records go opaque after that. She didn't retire — she's still listed as active personnel as of last quarter — but her travel logs aren't visible at our access level."

"She went somewhere," Kester said. "She went somewhere off-record. Probably another facility."

"Probably."

"What did she do here. Before she stopped coming."

Roon scrolled again. "Site visits to Wing Seven, every visit. Site visits to the medical wing's secondary lab — that's the one in the lower complex, the one that doesn't appear on most of the floor plans. Site visits to — " He paused. "Huh."

"What."

"Site visits to Building Twelve."

There was a silence. Tev looked up sharply.

"What's Building Twelve," Kester asked.

"The pediatric ward," Tev said.

Kester felt something cold slide along the inside of his ribs.

"There's a pediatric ward."

"There has to be one," Tev said. "There are children in the facility. Children born in custody. They get medical care like anyone else — basic intake, vaccinations, growth monitoring. It's a small ward. Three rooms, when I worked there in 2364. Maybe ten patients at any given time. Why would a researcher in *applied biosystemic integration* be doing site visits to the pediatric ward."

Kester did not answer.

Chayne said, very carefully: "Roon. How often did Dr. Vorlith visit Building Twelve."

"Every trip."

"For all six years."

"Every trip. Sometimes twice in a single visit."

"What was she doing there."

Roon scrolled through the records. "Visit purpose: *cohort assessment.* That's the only phrase listed. For all of them. Six years of *cohort assessment.*"

The phrase landed in the chamber and sat there, in the cold air, between all of them.

*Cohort.*

Kester thought about the word. He thought about how it had appeared in Wing Seven's quarterly reports for nine years — *cohort retention*, *cohort viability*, *cohort response thresholds* — and how he had glossed it, every time he saw it, as *the prisoners in the trial group*. A cohort was a research term. A cohort was the population you tracked across the duration of a study. He had filed the reports and assumed *cohort* meant the adults in Wing Seven, because that was the only cohort the wing officially had.

But Dr. Vorlith had been visiting Building Twelve.

Every trip.

For six years.

"Show me the pediatric records," Kester said. His voice did not sound like his own. "Building Twelve. From 2363 forward."

Roon hesitated. "Kester — "

"Show me."

Roon pulled up the file. It was a thin file. Building Twelve was a small ward and the Hegemony did not waste paperwork on prisoners' children — admission records, growth charts, a few discharge notes when children turned eighteen and were transferred to the general adult population.

Kester read.

He read for a long time.

What he was looking at, he understood eventually, was a list of admissions and discharges over an eight-year period. The numbers were small — between eight and fifteen children in the ward at any given time, with some turnover as new infants were born and as older children aged out into the adult system. There were death notations. The Hegemony was honest about deaths in pediatric care, in a clinical way; it noted the date, the cause, the attending physician, the disposition of the body. *Respiratory failure. Cardiac event. Reaction to medication.* The causes of death looked, on first reading, like the causes of death you would expect in an under-resourced ward in a low-priority facility.

Kester read them again.

He read the names.

He read the dates.

He counted.

In the ward's eight years of records, there had been forty-seven deaths.

"Tev," he said. "How many children would you expect to die in a pediatric ward of this size, over eight years. Best estimate. Including under-resourced conditions."

Tev was quiet for a long moment.

"In a ward this size, with bad equipment and no specialists, in a population already medically compromised by their parents' conditions — maybe eight," she said. "Maybe ten if we had a bad outbreak. Forty-seven is impossible."

"Forty-seven," Kester said.

"That's not a death rate. That's a — " She stopped. She did not finish the sentence.

Kester scrolled back through the records. He was reading the cause-of-death notations again. *Respiratory failure. Cardiac event. Reaction to medication.* He read them one at a time, looking for the pattern he now knew was there. He found it on the fourth pass.

Every notation had been signed by an attending physician.

Twenty-two of the forty-seven had been countersigned, in a small, neat hand in the margin, *S.V.*

Kester closed his eyes.

"She was there for the deaths," he said. "She wasn't visiting. She was *attending.*"

"Kester," Chayne said.

"Twenty-two of them. She countersigned twenty-two of them. The other twenty-five were probably hers too, but she stopped countersigning at some point, or she had assistants do it, or — "

"Kester. Look at the dates."

Kester looked.

The deaths were not evenly distributed across the eight years. They clustered. There were periods of months where no deaths occurred, followed by short windows — a week, ten days, two weeks — during which three or four children would die in rapid succession. Then nothing again. Then another cluster, eight months later. Then nothing.

The clusters lined up exactly with Dr. Vorlith's biannual visits to Tartaros.

She came twice a year. Children died during her visits. She countersigned the deaths. She left.

"This is — " Kester's voice was very quiet. "This is something. This is a thing she was doing."

"Yes," Chayne said.

"What was she doing."

Chayne did not answer.

Tev did not answer.

Sable, who had been watching all of this from across the chamber, had stood up at some point and was now looking at Kester with an expression he could not read.

"Kester," she said.

"Yes."

"What did she do to those children."

He looked at the file again. He read through the cause-of-death notations one more time. He was looking now for what they did not say. The Hegemony's medical records were thorough about *cause* but routinely vague about *circumstance*. There was no notation, in any of the forty-seven records, that the child had been part of a research protocol. There was no notation that any treatment had been experimental. There was no notation that anything unusual had been administered.

Just the cause. Just the date. Just the small *S.V.* in the margin.

But there was something else. Something Kester almost missed because it was so administrative, so routine, so exactly the kind of phrase he had filed for nine years without reading. At the bottom of each death record, in the standard *disposition* field, where the body's destination was recorded, the entries read:

*Disposition: Substrate Recovery Protocol 7-A. Transferred to Medical Wing, Secondary Lab.*

*Substrate.*

He stared at the word.

He had filed quarterly reports for nine years that contained the line item *Substrate Maintenance*. He had assumed it meant food preparation. He had told himself, when Chayne raised it the night before, that he had been wrong about Substrate Maintenance — that it had meant something to do with the autoclave, with tissue handling, with the adult prisoners in Wing Seven.

He had not, until this moment, considered that the substrate might be the *children*.

"Kester," Sable said.

"They were research subjects," Kester said. He was speaking very slowly, because if he spoke slowly he would not have to feel what he was saying. "They were research subjects and when they died their bodies were transferred to the Secondary Lab for — for further work. *Substrate recovery.* They — they were not just being studied, they were being — they were being — "

He could not finish the sentence.

He did not have to. Everyone in the chamber understood.

---

Sable left the room.

She did not slam any doors. She did not hit any walls. She walked out, into the same side corridor Tev had walked into earlier, and the chamber was quiet for a long time.

Tev was crying without sound. Chayne sat with his hands folded on his knees and his eyes closed.

Roon was looking at Kester.

"You didn't know," he said.

"I knew there was a pediatric ward."

"You didn't know about this."

"I knew there was a line item called Substrate Maintenance that I filed every quarter. I — I didn't know. I told myself I didn't know."

"There's a difference," Roon said.

"Is there."

Roon thought about it.

"I don't know," he said, finally.

---

Kester sat with the file open on the tablet for an hour.

He read through the forty-seven records again. He read them slowly. He made himself look at each name. He made himself sit with the dates. He made himself acknowledge that the children had been ages two, and four, and seven, and ten, and one of them had been fourteen years old and had died of *cardiac arrest* on a date that aligned exactly with Dr. Vorlith's spring 2367 visit, and he had probably signed the woman's travel voucher for that trip.

He thought about his daughter.

Lira had been ten years old, on Andra Prime, the year that fourteen-year-old girl had died in Building Twelve. Lira had been eating real strawberries under sodium lamps and going to school on water-skids across the under-ice canals. She had been writing him letters that he had not, that year, yet stopped receiving. She had been — what had she been doing on the spring afternoon when he signed the voucher that brought Sennae Vorlith to Tartaros? He did not know. He had not known then. He could not know now. He had been a man who did not look at his daughter's life with sufficient attention to retain its details, because he had been a man who was busy, and his work had been important, and the work had paid for the strawberries, and on some level he had believed that the strawberries were the point.

Andra Prime. The terraced city. The home world.

He thought of the way Moloch hung in its evening sky — a dim orange star to the naked eye, brightening at certain seasons to a small red disc, visible from Lira's bedroom window. She had asked him, once, when she was very small, what the red star was. He had told her it was a gas giant in another system, very far away, with one inhabited moon where her father went to work sometimes. He had not told her about Tartaros. He had told her *Moloch*, because Moloch was the part she could see, and the truth had always been the part that hid behind the part she could see.

She had been five when he told her that.

He thought, now, with a clarity that arrived too late to be of any use to anyone: *every child on Andra Prime grows up looking at that star. Every child grows up looking at the front of a thing whose back is what we built. They don't know. They never see the back. We made sure they would never see the back.*

He thought: *I have spent nine years on the back of a star.*

He thought: *I have to come home.*

He could not come home. He understood this. There was no version of his life in which he ever again walked the terraces of Andra Prime, or saw the strawberries, or stood beneath a sodium lamp in a quiet residential level and looked up at the artificial dusk and felt the city around him as a place he belonged. That version had ended somewhere — not in the tram with Orrel, not in Wing Seven, but earlier, much earlier, the first time he signed the line item *Substrate Maintenance* without reading it.

He had stopped being a man who could go home a long time ago. He had only just noticed.

---

Sable came back into the chamber after another hour.

She was composed. Her eyes were red but dry. She sat down across from Kester at the table where the ration cards from yesterday were still arranged in their small forest of underlined names.

"We're going into Building Twelve," she said.

"Yes."

"And the Secondary Lab."

"Yes."

"You understand what we're going to find."

"I think so."

"I don't think you do. I don't think *I* do. I think we are going to walk into rooms that we will not be able to walk back out of as the same people. I want you to know that before we go." She looked at him. "I want you to know that I am asking you to come with me anyway. Because I cannot do this without someone who can read the paperwork. And the paperwork is going to be in keywords, Kester. It is not going to be in sentences. They write things in keywords specifically so that people like you, ten years ago, file them without reading them. And now we need someone who knows the keywords."

"I know the keywords."

"I know you do. That's why you're coming."

She stood up.

"Get whatever sleep you can. We move tomorrow night."

"Sable."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry."

She looked at him for a long moment. Her face did not soften. But it did not harden, either.

"I know you are," she said. "It doesn't help. But I know."

She walked away.

---

That night Kester lay on his pallet and tried to sleep and could not. He was thinking, again, about Mouse. He was thinking about the way Bastien Orrel had said *sweetheart* through the glass. He was thinking about the cluster of fourteen-year-old girls in the forty-seven dead. He was thinking about *cohort assessment* and *substrate recovery* and the fact that Mouse was nineteen.

He was thinking about the piece that had not fit, last night, that he had told himself he would think about tomorrow.

It fit now.

It fit the way a key fits a lock you have been holding for years without recognizing. *Sweetheart*. Mouse calm in the broadcast room. Mouse walking back to Orrel through the corridor. Mouse pressing the override with the last piece of herself she could control. The implant in her neck that he had not known about, because he had not been the kind of administrator who looked at the back of a prisoner's neck.

Mouse was nineteen.

Mouse had been born in this prison.

Mouse had grown up in a population that was, the entire time, *being studied*.

He sat up on the pallet.

He understood, with a certainty that arrived before any of the supporting evidence, that the children in Building Twelve had been the *failures*. They had died. They had been *substrate*. But there were children who had not died. There were children who had survived whatever Sennae Vorlith had been doing to them, and those children had grown up, and one of those children was a nineteen-year-old girl who could move through dark maintenance crawls without making a sound and who pointed a rifle at a technician at age nineteen with the calm of a person who had been *prepared* for something.

He sat in the dark and looked at the wall and let the understanding finish arriving.

It was not just augmented infantry. It was not just weapons made of men.

It was *children*.

It had been children the whole time.

Forty-seven of them in Building Twelve, dead, used as raw material. An unknown number who had survived — who had been cultivated, monitored, *grown* — toward something the Board needed. Mouse was one of them. Mouse had probably always been one of them. Mouse's calm, Mouse's competence, Mouse's nineteen-year-old steadiness in a broadcast room with a salvaged rifle — these were not accidents of personality. They were *yields*.

The prison was not a death camp.

The prison was a farm.

He lay back down on the pallet and stared at the ceiling and understood that he had not yet, even now, found the bottom of what he had been part of. He had found another floor. There would be more floors. There were always more floors, in a building like this one. But he had found enough — enough to know what they were walking into in Building Twelve, enough to know what Mouse was now, enough to know what they would find in the Secondary Lab.

He wanted, very badly, to tell Sable.

He did not.

He understood — though he could not have explained why — that this was a piece of the picture she should not yet carry. She was already carrying her brother. She was already carrying Mouse, who she loved and had raised. To put on top of that the understanding that Mouse was not just a captive but a *product*, that Mouse's whole life had been the project, that Sable's years of protecting her had been at best a partial counter-program against something that had been working on her since infancy — that was a weight that would crush her, tonight, before she had to walk into Building Twelve.

He would tell her after.

He told himself he would tell her after.

He almost believed it.

Above him, in the citadel, Bastien Orrel was eating a quiet dinner at his desk. Across from him, in the chair he kept for guests, Mouse sat with her hands folded in her lap. She was eating too, slowly, with the careful manners of a child who had been taught to eat in front of company. Her implant had been adjusted that morning. She was very calm. She was, in fact, listening to Orrel describe — with the warmth of a man telling a daughter about her future — what the next phase of her preparation would involve.

She was listening with great attention.

She was, in some compartment of herself that the implant could not reach, screaming.

## Chapter Six: Building Twelve

They went in through a service shaft that Roon had spent two years opening, one welded grate at a time, and they came out in a maintenance corridor sixty meters east of Building Twelve's south door.

It was 02:14 facility time. The corridor was empty. The pediatric ward, on its visible schedule, ran a skeleton night staff of two — one nurse, one orderly — and Tev had spent the last six hours teaching Sable and Kester both their faces.

Kester carried a stolen sidearm he had not yet fired. Sable carried a pry bar and a salvaged stun baton. Tev carried a medical bag. Roon stayed behind in the under-levels with the broadcast deck and the comms relay; if they did not return by 04:30, he would burn the access shaft and reseal it from the chamber side, and the under-levels would lose Building Twelve forever.

They did not say anything to each other in the corridor.

There was nothing left to say.

---

The south door of Building Twelve opened on Tev's stolen keycard.

The pediatric ward's reception area was small and clean and almost cheerful — a low desk, a few chairs, a wall with a hand-painted mural of fish swimming through an underwater garden. Kester stared at the mural. Someone had painted it carefully. Someone had spent hours on the small details — the orange scales of one fish, the trailing fronds of a kelp plant, a single bubble rising toward the corner of the wall. He thought: *Who paints a mural in a place like this. Who decides the children should have something to look at.*

He thought: *The same kind of person who kept the autoclave running hot for two years.*

The nurse looked up from her terminal.

She was perhaps fifty, gray-haired, wearing the faded blue scrubs of long-term ward staff. She looked at Tev. Her face went through several expressions in rapid sequence — recognition, confusion, fear — and settled on something Kester could not name.

"Tev?" she said.

"Hello, Maren."

Maren did not reach for an alarm. She did not stand up. She looked at Tev, and at Sable, and at Kester, and at the pry bar and the stun baton and the sidearm, and she said, very quietly:

"Are you here for the children."

"Yes," Tev said.

Maren closed her eyes.

"Thank you," she said.

She turned the terminal toward Tev, and she pulled up a series of access codes Kester had not known existed, and she unlocked, in sequence, the doors to the three pediatric rooms and the door to the lower stairwell that connected to the Secondary Lab, and she said:

"There's no one in the rooms tonight. They moved them all forty minutes ago."

Sable's face did not change.

"Where."

"Down. The lab. They got an alert from somewhere — I don't know where, I'm just on duty up here. They came up the stairs and they took every child and they brought them down. I tried to ask. I am not authorized to ask." Maren's hands were shaking now. She had not stood up. "There is one child still in Room Two. They left her. She is — she has been ill for a week, they said she was not viable for transfer. I have been giving her water. I have not been able to do anything else. I am not authorized."

"Her name," Tev said.

"Her name is Aleya. She is seven years old. She came into the ward three months ago when her mother was put into the work rotation. I do not know where her mother is now. I have not been able to ask."

Tev nodded.

"Maren," she said. "We are going to take Aleya. We are going to go down to the lab. We are not going to come back this way. You understand what will happen to you when they find us missing."

"Yes."

"Why are you helping us."

Maren looked at Tev. Her mouth worked for a moment.

"My son," she said. "He came through this ward in 2358. He was four. They told me he died of pneumonia. I worked here for sixteen more years because they told me if I left, my husband would be reassigned. My husband died in 2367 of an industrial accident that I do not believe was an accident. I have been waiting for sixteen years to be asked the question you have just asked me. I am very tired, Tev. Please go get the girl."

---

Aleya was in the second of the three rooms, in a low cot beside an empty cot beside another empty cot.

She was very small. Her hair had been cut short by someone who was not a barber, in irregular tufts, and her cheeks had the hollow look of a child who had been losing weight for some time. She was awake. She was looking at the ceiling. When the door opened she turned her head, slowly, and looked at Tev with the unsurprised expression of a child for whom adults entering rooms had stopped being events that required surprise.

"Hello, sweetheart," Tev said. Her voice was very different from the voice Bastien Orrel had used through the glass. "My name is Tev. I'm a nurse. I'm going to pick you up now. Is that all right."

Aleya considered this.

"Are we going somewhere," she said.

"Yes, sweetheart. We are going to a different room."

"Is it warmer there."

"Yes. It is warmer there."

Aleya nodded.

Tev picked her up. The girl weighed almost nothing. She put her arms around Tev's neck with the practiced ease of a child who had been carried by many strangers in her short life, and she rested her cheek against Tev's collarbone, and she closed her eyes.

Sable was at the door. Her face had gone the particular color it went when she was holding something at a distance from herself.

"The lab," Sable said. "We have to go to the lab."

"Sable — "

"They have the rest of them. We have to go."

Kester said, quietly: "Sable. The cohort has been moved. By the time we get down there they will have moved them again, or — or done what they need to do. We have Aleya. We have Maren's testimony. We have what we came for."

"We came," Sable said, "for the children."

"And we have one of them."

"There are eleven more in that lab."

"And if we go down there now, there will be twelve dead bodies including ours, and Aleya will die in a corridor on the way out, and we will not have been a witness to anything anyone will ever read about." Kester was speaking very carefully. He was speaking the way he had spoken to Orrel at the dinner table in 2367 when Orrel had wanted to send a prisoner he liked to Wing Seven and Kester had argued, on procedural grounds, that the prisoner should be assigned elsewhere. He had won that argument. He had saved that prisoner. The prisoner had died fourteen months later in a labor accident that was probably not an accident, but he had won the argument at the table. "Sable. We came for evidence. We have a witness. We have a survivor. We have a name. We can't save the eleven tonight. We can carry one of them home."

Sable looked at him.

She looked at Aleya, asleep on Tev's shoulder.

She looked at Maren, who had not moved from her seat at the front desk.

"Maren," Sable said.

"Yes."

"What is in the lab. Right now. Tonight."

Maren's hands tightened on the edge of her desk.

"I don't know specifically. I only know what I have been told over the years, in fragments, by colleagues who were not careful. There is a procedure they perform on the children in batches. The children who survive the procedure are — they are kept. Somewhere. Not in this building. The children who do not survive are — there is a recovery protocol." Maren's voice was steady, the way a person's voice is steady when they have rehearsed something many times in their head before saying it aloud. "The procedures happen in waves. Twice a year, when the doctor visits. Tonight is not a wave. Tonight is — I think tonight the children are being moved because of you. I think they are being moved off Tartaros."

"Off Tartaros," Sable repeated.

"There is a transport. It comes once a year. It takes the children who have survived the procedures to — somewhere else. Another facility. I do not know where. I am not authorized." Maren's voice cracked slightly. "If they have moved the children to the lab tonight, it is because the transport is leaving early. It is because they are protecting the cohort from you."

Sable closed her eyes.

For a moment Kester thought she was going to argue. He thought she was going to say *we go down anyway, we stop the transport, we do what we came to do.* He braced himself for it. He had already begun preparing the next argument in his head, the one about Aleya, the one about the lab being a kill-zone, the one about how the war was longer than tonight and they had to live to fight more of it.

Sable did not argue.

She opened her eyes. She nodded, once, to Tev. She turned to Maren.

"Maren," she said. "Come with us."

"I cannot."

"Maren — "

"If I disappear tonight they will know within an hour. If I am at this desk when the morning shift arrives, they will not know for hours. By then you will be deep enough in the under-levels that they will not find you tonight. This is the help I can give you. Please take it." Maren's eyes were wet but her voice was not. "Take Aleya. Tell someone what happened to my son. His name was Riel. He was four. Please."

Sable looked at her for a long moment.

"Riel," she said. "Four years old. 2358."

"Thank you."

"I will tell everyone, Maren. I will not stop telling them."

Maren nodded.

They left.

---

They went out through the maintenance corridor and back through Roon's shaft and they did not encounter another human being for the entire return. Aleya slept against Tev's shoulder for the whole walk. She woke briefly, twice, and asked Tev where they were going, and Tev told her *somewhere warmer*, and Aleya seemed to find this answer sufficient.

When they came up into the under-levels' chamber, the Undersiders rose to their feet without speaking.

Roon saw the child. His face did something Kester had not expected to see on Roon's face — it broke open, briefly, into something that was not joy but was a close cousin of joy, the way a person looks when they have been carrying a load too heavy for too long and have just been told they can set part of it down.

Chayne brought a clean blanket. Someone had heated water on a salvaged burner. Tev carried Aleya to a cot they had prepared three hours earlier, in a quieter alcove with a single worklight, and she set the child down with the practiced gentleness of a woman who had nursed for fifteen years before her conviction.

For perhaps an hour, the chamber was full of life.

Sable sat by Aleya's cot and held the girl's hand and did not let go. Aleya woke once and asked Sable who she was, and Sable said *I'm a friend*, and Aleya nodded and went back to sleep. Tev was at her medical bag, working through it with the focused calm of a professional preparing for something. Chayne brought broth. Roon brought clean water. The worklight was warm and the blanket was clean and the chamber was, for the first time since the broadcast, a place where something had gone *right*.

Kester sat at the table where his ration cards still lay in their underlined forest.

He did not write anything.

He watched Tev.

Tev was crouched by her medical bag with her back to the alcove. She had been at the bag for ten minutes. She was checking and rechecking a small reader Kester recognized as a portable chemistry analyzer — the kind a nurse might carry to test a patient's vitals in the field. Tev's hands were not shaking. Tev's hands had not shaken once in the entire operation. They were not shaking now.

But she had not yet turned around.

Kester stood up. He walked over to her, slowly, the way a man walks when he is hoping the destination of his walk is wrong.

"Tev."

She did not look at him.

"Tev. What is it."

"Don't say anything," Tev said. Her voice was level. "Don't react. Don't let Sable see your face. Walk back to your table and sit down and look at your cards and let me come to you."

Kester walked back to his table.

He sat down.

He looked at his cards.

He saw none of them.

---

Tev came over to him three minutes later. She sat down across from him. She placed the chemistry reader face-down on the table between them.

"Tell me," Kester said quietly.

"There is a compound in her blood. I recognized the symptoms when we got here — she's too sleepy, her color is wrong, her breathing has a particular rhythm. I tested. It's a Hegemony Medical Authority sedative-paralytic in a class called *terminal palliatives*. It is used in cohort terminations when individual subjects fail their thresholds. It is administered to ensure that subjects do not suffer during the termination process. It has no antidote. It is metabolized over six to ten hours and at the end of that window the subject's respiratory function ceases."

Kester closed his eyes.

"How long has she had it."

"Approximately ninety minutes. They administered it when the alarm tripped. They knew we were coming. They knew we might find her. They left her as bait — not to catch us, but to ensure that whatever we carried out was not a witness. The compound is undetectable in autopsy. If she had died in our arms tonight and the Hegemony had recovered her body, they would have found nothing. The official cause of death would have been *post-illness respiratory failure*, consistent with her chart."

"She is going to die."

"She is going to die in five to seven hours, Kester. Yes."

"Can you — "

"I cannot. There is no antidote. There is, in fact, nothing I can do. I can keep her warm. I can keep her hydrated. I can let her sleep through the easy part and I can hold her hand through the hard part. That is what I can do. That is all I can do."

Kester sat very still.

"Sable doesn't know."

"Sable does not know."

"You have to tell her."

"I will tell her. In a few minutes. Right now Sable is sitting by the cot of a child she rescued and she is feeling, for the first time in a very long time, like she has done something that did not fail. I am going to let her have ten more minutes of that. Then I am going to tell her, and after I tell her I am going to ask her to sit with the child for the next several hours, because the child is going to ask for someone, and that someone should be the woman who carried her out."

Kester nodded.

"Tev."

"Yes."

"What do I do."

Tev looked at him.

"You sit with your cards, Kester. You write down everything Maren told us. You write down the name *Riel*, age four, year 2358. You write down everything Aleya says before she goes to sleep. You write down the words Maren used for the procedures. You make a record. That is what you do." She paused. "The rest of us are going to grieve. The chamber is going to be a hard place tonight. Make a record. It is the work you can do. It is the work you are good at. Do it."

She stood up.

She walked over to Sable.

She knelt by the cot.

She began to speak, very quietly.

Kester did not look up. He picked up his pencil. He wrote, on a fresh ration card: *Maren. Pediatric ward, Building Twelve. Son: Riel, age 4, died 2358, official cause pneumonia. Husband: industrial accident 2367, suspect deliberate. Sixteen years' service post-son's death under coercion. Gave us access this night.*

He underlined *Riel*.

He kept writing.

---

He did not look up when Sable made the sound she made.

He did not look up when Chayne began to weep, soundlessly, at the edge of the alcove.

He did not look up when Tev started to sing.

Tev had a low voice and she did not sing well and she was singing something Kester eventually recognized as a lullaby in a language he did not speak, perhaps her grandmother's language, and she sang it slowly and steadily and Aleya — who had woken briefly at Sable's sound and was now drifting back toward sleep — listened to it with her eyes half-closed and her small hand curled in Sable's larger one.

Kester wrote.

He wrote down the details of the operation, in the order they had occurred. He wrote down what Maren had said about the cohort being moved off Tartaros. He wrote down what Tev had said about the compound and its use in cohort terminations. He wrote down a sentence Aleya had said earlier, in the corridor, when Tev had been carrying her — *Are we going somewhere*, the small voice, the hands around the neck — and he wrote it down because Tev had told him to write down what Aleya said and he was going to write down everything Aleya said for as long as Aleya kept saying things, because that was the work he could do, and he had spent nine years learning to do work and not look up from it, and now finally that skill was good for something.

He wrote.

The lullaby continued.

---

Two hours into the vigil, the feed cut through.

Roon, who had been sitting at the comm relay, stood up suddenly and said, "Kester. You should see this."

Kester did not want to see it.

He went over.

The Hegemony Information Office was running a special bulletin. The same gray-uniformed woman from the morning was at her desk again. Behind her, this time, was a different photograph of Bastien Orrel — older, the Orrel of the present day, in a holding facility's neutral background, looking dignified and slightly tired. The caption read: **WARDEN ORREL: CHARGES FILED.**

"— following the conclusion of the preliminary phase of the internal investigation," the woman was saying, "the Hegemony Office of Penal Affairs has today filed formal charges against former Warden Bastien Orrel and six members of his command staff. The charges include negligent administration, falsification of official records, and dereliction of oversight duty leading to prisoner harm. Mr. Orrel has waived his right to public statement. He is currently being held at a secure core-world facility pending arraignment. The Office of Penal Reconciliation will begin processing claims from affected families next week. The Hegemony reaffirms its commitment to full accountability and welcomes Federation participation in the next phase of the investigation."

The picture changed. Updated graphic. **REFORM COMMISSION ANNOUNCES SITE VISIT.**

Roon snorted.

"They're going to send observers," he said. "To a facility where the lab moved tonight."

"Yes," Kester said.

"They're going to find nothing."

"Yes."

Tev was still singing in the alcove.

Kester stood at the relay and watched the bulletin loop. The picture of Orrel — present-day Orrel, the man who six hours ago had been six kilometers above their heads — held steady on the screen. Dignified. Slightly tired. The pose of a man cooperating fully with proceedings against him.

The pose was very good.

The pose was so good that Kester, looking at it, almost believed it himself for a moment — almost forgot what he had seen through the glass two days ago, almost forgot Mouse walking back into Orrel's arms, almost forgot the wink. The pose was so professional and so persuasive that the small part of him that had been an administrator for nine years felt the *quality* of the deception as a kind of admiration. Whoever had staged the photograph knew their work. Whoever had written the press release had been writing them for a long time.

He turned away from the screen.

He went back to his table.

He picked up his pencil and wrote, on a fresh card: *HIO bulletin 03:41 facility time. Orrel formally charged, photographed at "secure core-world facility." Photo composition consistent with managed staging. Likely body double or staged location on Tartaros. Reform commission "site visit" announced — purpose: cover for cohort transfer.*

He underlined *body double or staged location on Tartaros.*

He had not let himself fully think it until he wrote it. He had not let himself believe that the man on the screen and the man who had owned Mouse since infancy were the same man, in the same place, six kilometers above their heads. He had been telling himself, on some level, that Orrel was at least *gone* — that the public consequence of the broadcast had at least removed him from the operational picture, that whatever was happening tonight was happening under a caretaker administration that did not yet have its feet under it.

He looked at the underlined sentence.

He thought: *He is still here.*

He thought: *He was watching the operation tonight. He sent the alert. He ordered the cohort moved. He chose the compound for the child. He did all of it personally. He has not stopped running this prison for one minute since the broadcast went out.*

Across the chamber, Tev's lullaby was beginning to slow.

Kester did not look up. He could hear, in the slowing of the song, the change in Aleya's breathing. He had learned, over the last hours, to listen for the change without looking. The change was in the spaces between the notes. The notes themselves were the same. The spaces had been getting longer for forty minutes.

He kept writing.

---

At 04:51, Tev stopped singing.

She did not say anything. She simply stopped. The chamber went very quiet. Sable was holding Aleya's hand. Chayne was sitting on a folding chair with his face in his hands. Roon had turned off the relay screen and was standing with his back to the alcove, looking at nothing.

Kester stopped writing.

He listened.

There was no breathing in the alcove anymore. Only the hum of the air recyclers, and the low clicking of the worklight, and the small shifting sounds of people not moving.

Tev said, very quietly: "She's gone."

Sable did not respond.

Tev closed Aleya's eyes. She folded the child's hands on the small chest. She pulled the clean blanket up to Aleya's shoulders, the way you tuck in a child who has fallen asleep on the couch and whom you don't want to wake by carrying upstairs. She kissed Aleya's forehead. She stood up, slowly, with the particular stiffness of a woman who had been crouching for many hours, and she walked away from the cot without looking at anyone.

Sable did not move.

Sable was still holding Aleya's hand.

Kester sat at his table and looked down at the ration cards he had been filling for hours, and he saw that one of them — the one nearest his right hand — bore the words: *Aleya, age 7, mother in work rotation, transferred to Building 12 three months prior, terminal palliative compound administered approx. 02:30 facility time. Lullaby in unknown language. Last words to TR: "Are we going somewhere." Died 04:51.*

He had written *Last words to TR* without thinking. He had meant to write *to Tev*. He fixed it.

He underlined Aleya's name.

He set the pencil down.

He did not stand up. He did not go to Sable. He did not say anything. He sat at the table in the chamber under the worklight and listened to the air recyclers cycle into their dawn rotation, and he understood that the chamber had not been a place where something had gone right after all. The chamber had been a place where, for a few hours, they had all believed something had gone right, and the belief had been a gift the Hegemony had let them keep just long enough to make the taking of it worse.

The Hegemony had not lost tonight.

The Hegemony had given them a child to lose.

Six kilometers above them, in a citadel that the Hegemony had publicly told the public was now under caretaker administration, Bastien Orrel was awake. He had been awake all night. He had received the alert at 02:14, when Roon's shaft had triggered a passive sensor that the Undersiders did not know about and that Orrel had installed eight years ago against the contingency of exactly this. He had ordered the cohort moved at 02:18. He had selected the compound at 02:23. He had instructed his medical staff to leave one child behind, at 02:25, with the compound administered, because — he had explained to Dr. Vorlith over the comm, in a voice that was not warm but was patient — *we should let them feel as though they have rescued something. The lesson is more durable that way.*

He had watched the operation through the facility's internal cameras until the team disappeared into the under-levels. Then he had poured himself a small brandy and gone to check on Mouse, who was sleeping in the room across from his office. He had stood in her doorway for a few minutes watching her breathe. Her implant was holding well. Her vital signs were stable. She would be ready for the next phase in approximately thirty-six hours.

He had returned to his desk.

He had reviewed the press release the Hegemony Information Office was preparing for the morning bulletin. He had made one small edit to the language describing his "cooperation" with the proceedings. He had approved the photograph.

He had, before going to bed, written a brief note in his personal log, in the small careful handwriting he had used since his time as a junior officer at Calderon Trench. The note read: *They took the child. They will not take another. The lesson begins.*

He turned off the desk lamp.

He went to sleep.

## Chapter Seven: The Palace Beneath

Sable was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the chamber when Kester found her.

It was perhaps eight in the morning. Aleya's cot had been moved. The blanket had been folded. Tev had taken the body somewhere — to one of the deeper chambers, Kester thought, where the Undersiders kept their small dignities, where the dead were laid out and named and waited for whatever ceremony the community had cobbled together over the years to honor them. Kester had not asked. He understood, by now, that there were rooms in this place that belonged to other people, and that walking into them uninvited was the kind of thing he had spent his life doing and would now spend the rest of it learning not to do.

Sable was on the floor where the cot had been.

She was not crying. She was not, as far as Kester could tell, doing anything at all. She was sitting with her hands open on her knees, palms up, the way a person sits when they have decided to stop holding things.

He stood in the doorway for a long moment.

"Sable."

She did not turn around.

"Come in, Kester."

He came in. He sat down across from her, on the floor, because the chairs felt wrong and the table felt wrong and he understood, without being told, that this was not a conversation that was going to happen at furniture. He sat and he waited.

After a while she looked up.

He had been preparing himself, on the walk over, for what her face would look like. He had thought it would be hard. He had thought it would be the face of a woman who had decided to kill until she died — closed, narrowed, set. He had thought he would recognize the shape of Bastien Orrel after Calderon Trench, and he had been preparing himself to mourn the woman she had been before this morning.

Her face was not like that.

Her face was *gentle*.

She was looking at him with the kind of softness Kester had not seen on her before — had not, he realized, seen on anyone in a long time, perhaps not since his daughter Lira had been very small and had looked at him without yet knowing what he did for a living. It was the softness of a person who had nothing left to defend. It was the softness of a person who had stopped expecting the world to be different than it was.

It was the softness, Kester realized with a small cold lurch, that he had seen on Bastien Orrel for nine years.

It was not the same softness, exactly. Orrel's was performed. Sable's was real. But the *shape* of it was the same — the gentleness of a person who had decided what they were going to do, and who was now being kind to the people around them as a way of saying goodbye to the version of themselves that those people had loved. Orrel had been performing it for nine years because he had decided long ago, on a battlefield, that the world would not be changed by argument. Sable had arrived at the same conclusion overnight.

Kester wanted, very badly, to weep.

He did not.

"There's a deeper section," Sable said. Her voice was soft and level and entirely unhurried, the way a person speaks when they have stopped being in any kind of rush. "Beneath the under-levels. We've never gone down there. We always assumed it was structural — load-bearing concrete, foundation work, nothing built for human use. We were wrong. There's a door three levels below us that Ivo has been studying for two years. He says it's recent. He says it's not on any prison schematic. He says whoever built it put a great deal of care into making sure we would not look at it."

"You want to go through it."

"Yes."

"Today."

"Today."

Kester was quiet.

"Why today," he said.

Sable considered the question, the way a person considers a question that they have already answered to themselves but that they feel they owe the asker an answer to anyway. She tilted her head slightly. Her hands, on her knees, did not move.

"Because there is no point waiting anymore," she said. "We thought we had time. We thought the broadcast had bought us a window — a few weeks, maybe a month, while the Hegemony processed the spin and the Federation observers came and went. We were wrong about that. The Hegemony is not processing. The Hegemony is *moving*. They moved the cohort last night. They will move on us soon. If there is anything left to find on Tartaros, we have to find it now, today, before the next thing happens. After the next thing happens it will be too late." She paused. "Also Aleya is dead and I cannot sit in this chamber any longer."

"Sable — "

"Don't, Kester. I am not going to talk about her. Not today. Maybe not for a long time. There is a place she is, in me, and I am keeping her there, and the place is going to do its work without my supervision. I do not need to manage it. I need to *move*."

She stood up.

She did it with the easy gentleness of a woman who had not slept and had decided that not sleeping was no longer the kind of thing that would slow her down. She brushed her palms on her trousers. She looked down at Kester. Her face had not hardened. Her face was still soft.

She reached down and touched his shoulder.

"Kester," she said. "You don't have to come."

He looked up at her.

"I mean it," she said. The soft voice. The soft hand. "I am not asking you to come. I am telling you what we are doing, because you should know, but I am not asking you to come with me. There is no shame in staying in the chamber. Chayne could use you here. Tev could use you here. The records you have been making — somebody needs to keep making them, and you are good at it, and the work is real. You can stay. I will not blame you. I will not think less of you. I want you to understand this, before you decide. You have been carrying a great deal and you do not owe me the next thing. The next thing is mine. It is not yours."

Kester did not say anything for a long moment.

He was looking at her hand on his shoulder. The hand was warm and steady. It was the hand of a woman who was, as far as he could tell, no longer afraid of anything that could happen to her body.

He was thinking about Bastien Orrel, who had also stopped being afraid.

He was thinking about the shape they shared.

He was thinking that Sable did not yet know she had become this thing, and that telling her would not help, and that he was going to have to walk beside her for as long as he could in the hope that some of his own remaining fear might rub off on her like a blanket on a cold night.

"I'm coming," he said.

She nodded, once. She did not seem surprised.

"Thank you, Kester."

She walked out of the chamber.

He sat on the floor for another minute before he followed.

---

The expedition assembled at oh-nine-hundred, in the small staging area Ivo had been quietly fitting out for two years. Six of them. Sable. Kester. Kell Davour, who had been a Hegemony military police officer and who walked like one and who had not spoken to Kester directly in the three days since he had arrived in the under-levels because she was the kind of person who decided whether to trust a man over a long span of observation rather than a short span of conversation. Ivo, who had blown up the tram that had started this and who, now that Kester was meeting him properly, looked very tired in a way that had nothing to do with the last few days. Yenna Sorell, who was small and quiet and who carried a leather satchel of paper notebooks because she did not trust digital storage on principle. And Resh, the eighteen-year-old, who had not spoken since Mouse was taken and who was not going to start speaking now.

They were checking gear. They were not saying much.

Tev came over from the alcove where she had set up her medical station for the day. She had a small pouch in her hand. She walked up to Kester, who was the easiest of the six to approach because he was the only one not pretending to be busy, and she held the pouch out to him.

"What is it," he said.

"Field analgesic. Three doses. Pell carried it. It's yours now."

He took it.

He did not know what to say.

Tev looked at him. Her eyes were red but her hands were steady. She had been crying for hours, off and on, in the way a woman cries when she has lost a patient and knows she will lose more — quietly, while continuing to work, the tears arriving on their own schedule and dealt with as they came. She was looking at Kester now with an expression he could not read.

"Mr. Vaile," she said.

"Yes."

"I want to say something to you before you go."

"All right."

She was quiet for a moment, the way a person is quiet when they are choosing words they want to be precise.

"You were one of the bad ones," she said. "I want you to know I know that. I want you to know that I have not forgotten it. You signed the paperwork. You filed the vouchers. You moved the supplies that built the wing my friends died in. I will not pretend, to you or to myself, that those things did not happen. They happened. You did them."

Kester did not say anything. He was standing very still.

"But Mr. Vaile," Tev said, "we all do things with our eyes closed. Most of the world is built by people walking around with their eyes closed. The cruelty of it is that the world rewards us for keeping them closed. The work goes faster. The pay is better. The sleep is longer. We close our eyes and we get on with it and we tell ourselves that the things we cannot quite see are not really there." She paused. "And then sometimes a person opens their eyes. It happens for different reasons, to different people. Some open theirs because of love. Some because of grief. Some because they get old enough to be tired of the lie. You opened yours because an inspector asked you a question in a commissary and you could not bear it anymore. That is a stupid reason. That is also a real reason. The reason does not matter, in the end. What matters is that they are open."

Kester's eyes were burning. He did not blink. He was afraid that if he blinked he would cry, and he understood, with the small clear discipline he had been learning, that crying in front of Tev right now would be a thing he was doing for himself rather than for her.

"Now you have to choose," Tev said. "That is the part that comes after. The eyes are open and the choice is in front of you, and the world does not get easier from here — it gets harder, because now you can see, and seeing is heavier than not seeing. I am sorry. I wish it were the other way. It is not." She put her free hand briefly on his arm. "Make the choice well, Mr. Vaile. Make it for the right reasons. And whatever happens to you down there, whatever you find — remember that you opened your eyes. That is the thing you did. Nobody can take it back from you. Not Orrel. Not the Board. Not yourself, in your weak hours. You opened them. The rest is just the work of keeping them open."

She squeezed his arm, once, and walked back to her medical station.

Kester stood holding the pouch.

He did not weep. He did not have time to weep. He put the pouch in his jacket pocket and he turned to the rest of the expedition and he said, in a voice that was almost steady:

"I'm ready."

---

They went down through Ivo's shaft.

It took three hours. The deeper sections of the prison were not, as Sable had said, what the Undersiders had assumed. The first two levels below the under-levels chamber were structural, exactly as expected — load-bearing concrete, exposed rebar, the cold bones of the prison's foundation. The third level was different. The third level had a *finish* on it. The walls had been smoothed. The floor was not concrete but a kind of dark synthetic that absorbed sound. The air was cleaner. The temperature was warmer. The lighting, when Ivo activated the corridor's sensor strips, came up gentle and diffuse — not the harsh white of the prison's industrial fixtures, but something softer, almost domestic.

"This isn't a foundation," Davour said quietly. She had her sidearm out. She was speaking for the first time in the expedition, and her voice was lower and more careful than Kester had expected. "This is a residential level."

"It can't be," Yenna said. "Who would live three levels under a prison."

"Somebody who didn't want to be found," Davour said.

They moved on.

The corridor went for sixty meters before it ended in the door Ivo had been studying for two years.

The door was beautiful. Kester registered this with a small bewildered shock. He had been expecting an industrial blast door, a security lock, the kind of utilitarian barrier the prison used for its hardened sections. The door in front of them was made of a dark hardwood that he did not recognize, inlaid with brass in a pattern he eventually recognized as a stylized version of the Hegemony seal. The lock was not a security lock. It was a *handle*. A brass handle. It looked, to Kester, like the door of a private library on a wealthy man's estate.

Ivo studied it. He had a kit of small tools out. He did not use any of them. After a moment he reached out and tried the handle.

The door opened.

"It's not locked," Ivo said.

"That's worse," Davour said.

They went through.

---

The palace.

There was no other word for it. Kester stood in the doorway and looked at what was on the other side of the brass-handled door, and he understood that the language he had been using to describe his life for nine years had not contained the word for this place, because the existence of this place would have made the rest of his life impossible to continue.

The room beyond the door was a foyer. There was a chandelier — actual cut crystal, hanging from a domed ceiling that had been frescoed with a scene Kester could not parse from this angle but that involved figures in classical drapery and what might have been a bull. The floor was marble. Real marble, with veining that was not a print. The walls were paneled in the same dark wood as the door. There were two chairs against one wall, upholstered in a dark green silk. There was a small table between them with a porcelain bowl on it, the bowl empty but immaculate, the kind of bowl that had been chosen by someone who knew the names of porcelain manufacturers.

The air smelled faintly of cedar.

"What is this place," Yenna whispered.

Sable had walked into the foyer. She was looking at the chandelier with an expression Kester could not read. She walked across the marble floor — her boots, which had been mucky from the deeper levels, made small careful prints — and she stopped at the far doorway and looked through it.

"It keeps going," she said.

It kept going.

The foyer opened on a long gallery, hung with paintings — actual oil paintings, in gilded frames, of landscapes Kester did not recognize and portraits of men and women in formal dress. The portraits were old. The frames had small brass plates. The names on the plates meant nothing to Kester, but he registered, dimly, that they were *names* — that the people in the portraits had been named, that someone had cared enough to identify them, that this was not a generic decoration but a *collection*. Someone's family. Someone's lineage. Someone's pride.

The gallery led to a dining room. Long table. Sixteen chairs. Crystal on a sideboard. A second chandelier, smaller than the foyer's. A fireplace, and in the fireplace, a small neat stack of logs that had not been lit but had been arranged with the kind of care a butler arranges things when the master is expected.

"Somebody has been maintaining this," Davour said.

"Recently," Ivo agreed.

"Look at the dust," Yenna said.

There was almost no dust. The air recyclers were running — Kester could hear them, low and steady, behind the walls — and the surfaces had been cleaned within the last week or two. The dining room had not been used recently, Kester thought. But it had been *prepared* for use. The crystal had been polished. The chairs had been straightened.

They moved through the dining room and into a corridor that turned twice and ended at another door.

This door was different. It was heavier than the dining room's interior doors, and it had a small pad beside it — a security control, the only one Kester had seen so far in the palace, and even it was tasteful, brushed brass set into the wall paneling with the discretion of a thermostat.

Ivo studied the pad.

"This one's actually locked," he said.

He worked on it for ten minutes. Davour watched the corridor behind them. Sable stood very still, looking at the door. Yenna had taken one of her notebooks out and was sketching the corridor's layout with quick neat lines. Resh stood at Sable's shoulder and did not move and did not speak.

The door clicked.

Ivo pushed it open.

---

The room beyond the door was the board room.

Kester knew it the moment he saw it. He had spent nine years filing meeting notes for rooms that looked, in his imagination, almost exactly like this — long oval table, leather chairs with high backs, a sideboard with decanters, a screen on the wall opposite the head of the table, a low light that was warm without being intimate. The room was not lavish in the way the foyer and the dining room were lavish. The room was *functional*. It was the working room of a board that had been working in this palace for a long time, and the room's beauty was an afterthought to its purpose.

There were nine chairs around the table.

Nine.

Kester's mind cataloged this. Nine principals. Nine seats. The Board he had been assembling in his head was larger than he had thought.

There were papers on the table.

They were arranged neatly. Stacked at three of the nine seats. Folders. Notebooks. A bound volume that looked, from across the room, like a printed report. Whoever had last met in this room had left their working materials behind, and whoever maintained the palace had not removed them, because the materials had been left in a state of *paused work* — the meeting was not concluded. The Board would be coming back to this room. Soon, probably.

"Yenna," Sable said quietly.

"Yes."

"Read everything you can. Take it all. Take what you can carry and photograph the rest. We will not get a second chance."

"Yes."

Yenna went to the table.

She moved like a librarian into a fire. She was fast and gentle and she did not waste a motion. She opened the first folder and she began to read, and within twenty seconds her face had changed in a way Kester did not yet have the context to understand.

He went to the table beside her.

He looked over her shoulder.

The folder was a financial report. Quarterly. The header read: *Project Renewal — Q3 2371 — Tartaros Node — Substrate Yield.* Below the header was a chart. The chart showed numbers Kester recognized. They were the numbers he had been signing for nine years. Substrate Maintenance line items. Cohort assessment fees. Travel vouchers for Dr. Vorlith. They were here, in summary form, totaled and signed off on by — 

"There are signatures," Yenna whispered.

There were signatures.

There were nine signatures, on the report's final page. Most of them were illegible — careful, professional signatures of the kind that have been deliberately rendered unreadable by their owners — but two were clear enough to read. One read *S. Vorlith*. One read *B. Orrel*.

Orrel's signature was at the bottom of the second column. He was not at the head of the page. He was in the *middle*. He was — Kester counted — the sixth signature down.

Orrel was not the chair of this board.

Orrel was a *member*. A junior member. Possibly the most junior member. His signature came after five others.

Kester's mouth was dry.

"Yenna," he said. "What else."

She was already reading. She had moved to the second folder. Her notebook was open beside her and she was making notes in a tight quick hand.

"Personnel tracking," she said. "They — Kester, they have files on us. On me. There's a file with my name on it. There's — "

She pulled it out.

It was a thin file. It contained, Kester could see over her shoulder, a copy of an academic paper she had written, with marginalia in three different hands. It contained a printed photograph of her. It contained a memo dated 2370 — last year — that began *Subject Sorell continues to correspond with Federation auditor Adeyemi. Recommend continued surveillance with intervention pending if subject pursues formal investigation channels*.

Yenna sat down, slowly, in the chair nearest the file.

"They knew about me," she said. "They knew I was talking to her. They — they let me keep talking to her. They let her come to Tartaros. They — "

"Yenna."

"They let her come because she was my source. They wanted to see what I would tell her. They wanted to know how much I knew. They — Kester, they killed her because I — "

"Yenna. *No*."

"I told her about Wing Seven. I told her everything I had. She came to Tartaros because of what I told her. She died because of what I told her. They — "

"Yenna, listen to me." Kester crouched next to her chair. "They killed her because she got too close. Not because of you. Because of *her*. She did her job. She came here. She listened to me. She was going to file a report and they could not let her file it. That's why she died. You did not kill her. They killed her. *Listen to me.*"

Yenna was nodding. She was nodding and she was not listening. She was reading the next page of the file. Her hands were shaking now, finally, the way they had not shaken in the corridor.

Sable was standing on the other side of the table. She had not moved. She was watching Yenna read. Her face was still soft.

"Keep reading, Yenna," Sable said gently. "Read all of it. Take all of it. It's worse than you think but you have to keep going, sweetheart. We can grieve later. Right now we need to know."

Yenna nodded.

She kept reading.

The screen activated forty minutes later.

They had been working through the papers in silence — Yenna at the head of the table reading and noting, Kester moving through the financial reports beside her, Davour watching the door, Ivo studying the room's other systems for surveillance, Resh standing at Sable's elbow and Sable standing very still and watching. They had assembled, by then, a partial picture.

The Board had nine members. Vorlith was one. Orrel was one — a junior one. The other seven were referred to in the documents only by initial: *A.M., D.K., R.T., V.S., O.L., J.H.,* and one whose initial appeared in only one document, in a single line at the bottom of a memo, marked *for the Architect's review*.

The Board had been operating since 2349. Twenty-two years.

Tartaros was one of *seven* facilities. The others were named only by code: *Site Two, Site Three, Site Four, Site Five, Site Six, Site Seven*. Tartaros was Site One. The original. The prototype. Each of the other six sites had its own warden, its own scientist, its own administrator — its own Orrel and its own Vorlith and its own Kester. Kester had been one of seven men.

The Harvester was named in three documents.

It was named only by name. There were no coordinates in any document Yenna had so far read. The coordinates were held separately, presumably, by a smaller subset of the Board. The Architect, certainly. Probably one or two of the others.

The Harvester was where the cohorts went. All seven sites' cohorts. For decades. The number of children processed by the Harvester since 2349 was — Yenna read the figure twice, to make sure — *approximately fourteen thousand*.

Fourteen thousand.

Kester had stopped reading. He was sitting in one of the leather chairs. He was looking at a wall. He was thinking, with the dim clarity of a man whose moral universe had just expanded by an order of magnitude, that he had spent nine years on one of seven farms, and that the farms together had fed a single mill, and that the mill had been grinding for twenty-two years before he ever set foot on Tartaros.

The screen on the wall came on.

It was a soft chime first — the kind of chime an executive office uses to indicate an incoming call. Then the screen brightened. Then Bastien Orrel was on it.

He was sitting at his desk in the citadel. The desk Kester had sat across from a thousand times. The brandy decanter was on the corner. The lamp was on. Orrel was wearing his black dress uniform. His hands were folded on the desk in front of him.

He was not smiling.

This was the thing Kester noticed first, and the thing that disoriented him most. Orrel's face, on the screen, was *neutral*. Professional. The face of a station chief reporting to his superiors. There was no warmth in it and no cruelty in it. There was only the steady, slightly tired expression of a man at work.

This was the Orrel the Board saw.

This was the Orrel Kester had never seen.

"Mr. Vaile," Orrel said. His voice was different too — quieter, less performed. "Sable. Others. I will keep this brief, because I assume you are reading the materials and I do not wish to delay your work.

"You have reached the residence. This was always possible. I want you to understand that I am not surprised. I planned for this, though I did not plan for it to happen this week. The recent events have accelerated several timelines. I am adapting. You should adapt as well.

"I want to make you an offer, Mr. Vaile. I am making it because we have nine years of professional acquaintance and because I believe you are, at this moment, in a position to make a decision rather than a mistake. The offer is simple. I will tell you where Mouse is going. I will tell you when she will be there. I will tell you the name of the facility and I will tell you the schedule of her transit. I will tell you all of this, today, freely, with no immediate condition attached. You may use the information as you wish."

Orrel paused.

"In return, I am going to ask you to do nothing immediately. I am going to ask you to live with the information for a few days. I am going to ask you to consider, in those days, what you want for the girl. You have come to feel responsibility for her. I respect this. I share it. We have, between us, been the two men most responsible for what she has become, and I would like us to discuss her future as men who have raised a daughter together discuss her future. I would like you to come and see her, Mr. Vaile. I would like you to come to the Harvester. I will give you safe passage. You may bring whoever you trust. You may bring Sable, if she will come. You may bring all of them.

"Come and see what the next phase is. Come and see what Mouse is becoming. Then make your choice. If you choose to take her and leave, I will not stop you — though I will also not be able to protect you afterward, and others will. If you choose to stay and resume work, there is a position waiting for you that befits your skills. If you choose to do something else entirely, that is also your right. I am not here to coerce you. I am here to *show you*, because I believe you have not yet seen, and you cannot make a decision about Mouse's future without having seen.

"The information about her location and schedule will be transmitted to a drop point in the under-levels at twenty-two hundred local time tonight. Ivo will know how to retrieve it. If you do not retrieve it, no harm done — the offer stands and the information will be made available to you again at a later date.

"That is what I came to say. I understand this is a great deal. I understand you have, at this moment, a great deal else to absorb. I am sorry for the timing. The timing was not entirely mine to choose.

"Goodbye, Mr. Vaile. I look forward to our next conversation."

The screen went dark.

The board room was silent.

Kester was not breathing. He realized this and made himself breathe. He realized, at the same time, that he had been hearing the offer not as a threat but as a *gift*, that some part of him had been listening to Orrel's voice with the same attention he had given it for nine years across a desk, that he had been *agreeing* on some animal level before any of his rational faculties had caught up.

He looked at Sable.

She was looking at him.

Her face was still soft. The softness had not changed. But there was something behind her eyes now that he had not seen before, and he understood, with the cold lurch he had begun to recognize as the lurch of his own life rearranging itself, that she had watched him hear the offer. She had watched his face. She had seen what was there.

She did not say anything.

She turned, slowly, to Yenna.

"Keep reading," she said gently.

Yenna kept reading.

---

It was Yenna who found the Undersider files.

There was a folder, slim, indexed under *Tartaros Node — Surface Containment*. Inside it: surveillance summaries. Photographs taken from inside the chamber, somehow, at angles Kester could not source. A diagram of the chamber's layout that was almost correct but slightly outdated. A list of names, with annotations. *Sable Reyek. Chayne Olret. Tev Marn. Roon Haveric. Ivo Vens.* The names of the principal Undersiders, with brief biographical notes, with assessments of their likely behavior under various contingencies.

The list contained forty-three names of children.

The actual number, Sable had told Kester two nights ago, had been forty-seven.

The Board had been counting wrong.

Yenna read the file aloud, in the voice of a woman who had stopped feeling what she was reading, because feeling it would have stopped her from reading. She read the names. She read the assessments. She read the contingency plans.

There were three contingencies. *Contingency A: Broadcast event with international attention. Response protocol: managed exposure of named warden, public reform commission, internal cohort transfer to Harvester, surface team neutralization within seventy-two hours of broadcast.*

The broadcast had been seventy-two hours ago, almost exactly.

*Surface team neutralization* — that was the Undersiders.

"They're attacking the chamber," Davour said. Her voice was flat. "Right now. This is happening right now."

"Yes," Sable said.

"How long have they been there."

Yenna scrolled through the file. "The transfer of the cohort was at oh-two hundred. The surface team neutralization is scheduled for — " She paused. " — for oh-nine-hundred. That's an hour ago. They've been in the chamber for an hour."

The board room was very quiet.

Sable closed her eyes.

She did not weep. She did not move. Her hands were at her sides. She was breathing, slowly, in through her nose and out through her mouth. The softness of her face had not left it. The softness had become something else — not harder, but *deeper*, the way a lake deepens when you step further out into it.

She opened her eyes.

"We have to go back," she said.

"There won't be anyone left," Davour said.

"We have to go back, Kell."

"Sable. There won't be anyone left. The team that hits the chamber is not Internal Security, it's a clearance unit. They don't take prisoners. They don't leave anyone alive. They've been there an hour. You know what an hour means."

"I know what an hour means," Sable said. "We are still going back."

Davour looked at her.

After a moment Davour nodded, once.

"Take everything," Sable said to Yenna. "Take everything you can carry. Photograph the rest. We have eight minutes and then we go."

Yenna started moving.

Kester stood up from the leather chair he had been sitting in. He had been thinking, for the last several minutes, about Orrel's offer. He had been thinking about Mouse. He had been thinking about the position waiting for him at the Harvester.

He had been thinking about all of these things and he had been ashamed of himself for thinking about them, and then he had been more ashamed of himself for noticing that he was ashamed, because the noticing was a kind of self-regard he could not afford right now. He had stood up to make himself stop thinking.

He went to help Yenna with the papers.

He did not look at Sable.

He was afraid, very specifically, that if he looked at her she would still be looking at him, and he did not yet know what he would do if she was.

---

They left the palace at oh-nine-fifty-two.

They climbed back up through Ivo's shaft as fast as their bodies would carry them. Davour led. Sable came second. Kester was third, with Yenna's satchel over one shoulder, the satchel heavy with paper and the small sealed device on which Yenna had photographed everything she could not carry. Ivo and Yenna and Resh came behind. Nobody spoke. The climb took them ninety minutes, and the ninety minutes felt like a year, because every meter of the climb was a meter closer to whatever was waiting for them in the chamber.

When they reached the under-levels' main corridor, they stopped to listen.

The chamber was silent.

Kester had been hoping, dimly and against all of his better judgment, that they would arrive in the middle of the attack — that there would still be guards in the chamber, that Davour would lead them into a fight, that the question of whether anyone was alive would still be open and they would close it with their hands. He had wanted, with a kind of awful child-want, for the situation to still be a situation he could affect.

The silence told him otherwise.

The clearance unit was already gone. They had finished. They had left. The chamber was — the chamber was whatever it was, now, on the other side of the corridor's last bend.

Davour went around the bend first.

She went with her sidearm raised. She went the way a soldier goes into a room she expects to clear. She had been a Hegemony military police officer for many years before her arrest, and her body had been trained, and her body did the trained thing now, because thinking would have been worse.

She stopped in the chamber's doorway.

Sable went past her.

Kester came next.

What he saw, when he came around the corner, was this:

The chamber's worklights were still on. The air recyclers were still running. The folding chairs and the small forest of his ration cards and the salvaged crates and Tev's medical station were all where they had been when the expedition left, three hours and a lifetime ago.

Tev was on the floor of the alcove. She had fallen between the children's cots and the door. Her body was where she would have placed it deliberately if she had had time to plan — between the door and the cots, between the threat and the children. She had not had time to plan. She had moved, when she heard the boots, and she had moved to the place she would have planned to move to, because the place was the same place either way. She had been hit twice. She was not breathing.

Roon was against the chamber's east wall. He was sitting up. He was bleeding from a wound in his thigh and a wound in his side and he was holding a salvaged welding torch in his right hand as if the welding torch were a weapon, which it was not, and which it had not been when the guards came through, and which he had presumably been using for something practical when the attack began. He was conscious. His eyes tracked Sable as she came into the chamber. He made a sound that was not a word. His mouth tried to make a word and could not.

Chayne was not in the chamber's main area.

Sable moved toward Roon. Davour moved with her, already pulling a field kit from her own pack, already kneeling. Yenna ran for Tev because Yenna had not yet absorbed that Tev was past help, and the running was Yenna's body refusing to absorb it for another few seconds. Resh stood in the doorway and did not move and did not make a sound.

Kester walked to the wall.

He walked to the section of wall, behind one of the salvaged crates, where Chayne had told him three nights ago that he kept his archive — the small false ventilation duct, hidden behind a panel that Chayne had built himself, where for years Chayne had been quietly storing every piece of stolen citadel paperwork the Undersiders had been able to acquire. Hard drives. Printed dossiers. The records of a records officer who had not stopped being one when he was arrested. Chayne's life's work, hidden behind a vent.

The panel had been moved.

Kester crouched down.

He pulled the panel aside, gently, with hands that he was making himself keep gentle.

Chayne was inside the duct.

He was alive. He was breathing in the small thin breaths of a man who had been crammed into a space too small for him for a long time. He was holding, in his arms, a child.

The child was perhaps four years old. A girl. Her face was pressed against Chayne's shoulder and her eyes were closed and her small hands were fisted in the fabric of Chayne's shirt. She was breathing too. She was alive.

She was the only child in the chamber.

Chayne saw Kester.

He did not say anything. His eyes were wet. He moved his head, very slightly, to indicate the child in his arms, and then he closed his eyes again.

"She's all right," Kester said. He did not know if he was saying it to Chayne or to himself or to the child or to no one. "It's all right. It's over. I'm going to get you out. Both of you. It's going to be all right."

He reached into the duct and took the child first.

She did not wake up.

He carried her over to the cot Tev had used for Aleya, the day before, the lifetime before, and he laid her down on the blanket Tev had folded that morning. The child was very small. Her hair was dark and it was wet at the temples with sweat. She had been hidden in that duct for an hour. She would not, Kester thought, remember it. He hoped she would not remember it.

He went back for Chayne.

It took him and Davour together to get the old man out. Chayne's knees, which had been bad before, were now worse. He could not stand. They carried him to a chair and they put him in it and Davour started checking him for injuries, and Chayne, in the chair, was finally able to speak.

"Tev," he said.

"Yes," Davour said gently. "I know."

"She — they came through the south passage. She was at the alcove. She — she didn't move, Kell. I told her to move. I told her to come with me. She did not move."

"I know."

"I had — the child was — I had her in my arms. I went for the duct. I had to choose. I had to — "

"You chose right, Chayne. You chose what Tev would have wanted. Don't talk now. Let me look at you."

Chayne let her look at him.

He was crying without sound. He was not making any noise at all. Tears were running down his face and into his beard and he was sitting in the chair and he was not making any sound.

Kester stood in the middle of the chamber and looked around it.

Nine bodies.

Tev. Roon was alive but bleeding. Chayne was alive in his chair. The child was alive on the cot.

Of the rest of the chamber's population — the unnamed Undersiders, the older children who had been hidden here, the people who had received Sable's broadcast as a victory and Aleya's death as a wound — there were nine bodies on the floor, and there were no others. There had been perhaps thirty people in the chamber when the expedition left. There were thirteen alive now, including the six who had just returned. Seven Undersiders survived: Chayne. Roon. The child. And four others Kester would not learn the names of for another several hours, who had escaped through deeper passages when the attack began, and who would trickle back into the chamber over the course of the night.

Seven.

Seven Undersiders.

Kester counted them on his hands.

He still had three fingers left over.

---

Sable was kneeling beside Tev's body.

She had not, since they came into the chamber, made a sound. She had moved to Roon first, helped Davour stabilize him, accepted the field kit, applied the dressings. She had moved to Chayne next, listened to his account, said *thank you, Chayne, you did right*. She had moved to the child on the cot, looked at her, touched her hair very lightly, said nothing.

She had come to Tev last.

She knelt beside the body. Tev's eyes were open. Sable closed them, gently, the way Tev had closed Aleya's eyes the night before. Sable folded Tev's hands across Tev's chest, the way Tev had folded Aleya's. Sable pulled a clean cloth — there was one in Tev's medical station, and Sable knew where it was because Sable had watched Tev work for years — and she covered Tev's face.

She sat beside the body for a long minute.

She did not weep.

Then she stood up, slowly, and she turned, and she looked at the chamber, and she looked at the seven survivors, and her face was still soft.

She said, in a voice that was perfectly steady:

"We bury them tonight. We take care of Roon. We feed the child. Tomorrow we move. The chamber is no longer ours. The under-levels are no longer ours. The Board has known about this place for years and we have been living in their attic. We are going to find somewhere they have not yet thought of, and we are going to take what Yenna has, and we are going to figure out what to do next." She paused. Her eyes moved across the chamber. They moved across Kester's face. They did not linger. "Kester."

"Yes."

"At twenty-two hundred tonight, you and Ivo will go to whatever drop point Orrel has prepared. You will retrieve the information about Mouse. You will bring it back. We will read it together. We will decide together what to do with it. Do you understand."

"Yes."

"Good."

She turned away from him.

She crouched beside the child on the cot. The child was beginning to stir. Sable put her hand on the child's back, very lightly, and waited.

The child opened her eyes.

She was not afraid. She had been hidden in the duct for an hour and she had not made a sound. She was, Kester thought, the kind of child whose silence had been taught to her early. She looked up at Sable with the unsurprised expression of a child for whom adults appearing over her had stopped being events.

"Hello," Sable said.

"Hello," the child said.

"What is your name."

"Pira."

"Pira. That's a beautiful name. Pira, my name is Sable. Are you hurt anywhere."

Pira considered this. She moved her arms, then her legs. "No."

"That's good. Pira, I am going to take care of you now. Is that all right."

Pira looked at Sable for a long moment.

Then she said: "Where is Tev."

Sable did not answer immediately.

"Tev had to go," she said, finally. "She is somewhere else now. I am going to take care of you instead. Is that all right."

Pira nodded, slowly.

Sable lifted her, gently, and held her against her shoulder, the way Tev had held Aleya in the corridor of Building Twelve, the way Tev had held the broadcast deck against her chest in the comms array, the way Tev had held many things in her short life with the practiced gentleness of a woman who held things for a living. Pira put her arms around Sable's neck. She closed her eyes. She did not, as far as Kester could see, cry.

Sable stood in the ruined chamber with the child in her arms and looked at Kester across the bodies and the bloodstains and the small forest of his ration cards on the table, which had not been disturbed, which had been left exactly where the guards had found them, because the guards had not been here for paperwork.

She said, very softly:

"Twenty-two hundred. The drop. Don't be late."

He nodded.

She walked away from him, with Pira in her arms, into the deeper rooms of the chamber where the dead would be laid out and the living would be tended and the long quiet work of a community burying itself would begin.

Kester stood in the doorway.

He stood there for a long time.

Above him, six kilometers up, in a citadel that the Hegemony Information Office was telling the public was now under caretaker administration, Bastien Orrel was finishing his lunch. Across the desk from him, in the chair he kept for guests, Mouse sat with her hands folded in her lap. She had eaten very little. Her implant had been adjusted at oh-nine-hundred to manage the stress of the morning's operations, which she had observed through the citadel's internal monitoring system at Orrel's invitation. She had watched the clearance unit move through the chamber. She had watched Tev fall. She had watched Chayne crawl into the duct with the child.

She had watched it all with great attention.

She had not said anything.

Orrel set down his fork. He looked at her. He smiled, the warm paternal smile he had been smiling at her since she was four years old.

"How are you, sweetheart," he said.

"I'm fine."

"You watched the operation."

"Yes."

"Did you understand what you were watching."

Mouse considered the question, the way a child considers a question from a parent who has asked it deliberately, who wants a specific answer, who will be disappointed if the answer is not the one being expected. She had been considering questions of this shape for nineteen years. She knew what to do.

"I understood," she said.

"Tell me what you understood."

"I understood that the people in the chamber were going to die. I understood that Tev was going to be one of them. I understood that Sable was going to come back and find her, and that Sable would be different afterward. I understood that the work was necessary and that the work was hard and that the work would continue even though Tev was a person I had loved." Mouse paused. Her hands, on her lap, did not move. "I understood that I was supposed to feel something specific while I watched. I am trying to feel it. I am not sure I am feeling the right thing."

"What are you feeling, sweetheart."

"I am not sure."

"That's all right. That's all right, Mouse. You will understand the feeling in time. The next phase will help you understand it. Do you trust me."

Mouse looked at him.

"Yes," she said.

She was, in some compartment of herself that the implant could not reach, screaming.

The compartment was getting smaller. It had been getting smaller every day for nineteen years. She did not know if it would still be there next week.

She thought it might not be.

She thought, with the small clear discipline of a girl who had been taught from infancy to keep her thoughts from her face: *I have to remember Sable's name. I have to remember Tev's name. I have to remember Pira's name. I have to remember what they look like. Tomorrow I will not remember. I will remember today.*

She remembered.

She kept her hands folded in her lap.

She finished her lunch.

## Chapter Eight: The Combination

They buried Tev in the deeper chamber the Undersiders had used for their dead since the beginning — a small room three levels below the main chamber, accessed through a narrow shaft, which had been a storage bay for ore-handling equipment in the prison's earliest years and which had been converted, ten years ago, into something that the Undersiders did not have a word for and that an outsider would have recognized as a chapel.

There were small alcoves in the walls. Some of the alcoves held photographs. Some held small objects — a comb, a medal, a folded letter. Some were empty. The empty ones were waiting.

Tev was laid out on the central plinth with her hands folded across her chest in the same configuration she had folded Aleya's hands the night before. Someone had brushed her hair. Someone — Kester suspected Davour — had placed a small dried flower from the chamber's hydroponic stock between her fingers. The flower was white and waxy and the kind of flower that did not need much light to grow. The Undersiders had been growing it for fifteen years, Chayne told Kester later, for exactly this purpose.

The whole community was there. The seven survivors. The four who had escaped through the deeper passages and trickled back overnight. Ten of them counting Kester. Roon was carried in on a litter by two of the unnamed survivors — a man called Dren and a woman called Hess, whose names Kester learned that morning and whose faces he would carry forward without yet knowing them — and Roon was placed on the chamber's only chair. He could not stand. He sat with his bandaged side and his bandaged thigh and his eyes very far away, and he did not speak.

Chayne stood at the head of the plinth.

He spoke for perhaps two minutes. He did not say much. He said Tev had been a nurse. He said she had nursed in the medical wing of Building Twelve for two years before her transfer to general population, and she had nursed in the under-levels for fifteen years after, and that in those years she had cared for ninety-three Undersiders that he could name and probably more that he could not. He said she had held the hands of the dying when there was no one else to hold them, and she had held the hands of the living when the living had needed it, and the difference between the two had not always been clear to her in the moment but it had not mattered, because Tev had been a woman who held hands and who did not require the situation to have been clarified before she did so. He said she had sung to a child the night before. He said the child had been a stranger to her but that Tev had treated the child as a granddaughter for the six hours she had with her, because that was the kind of work Tev did, and the work did not require relationship, only attention. He said *thank you, Tev*, and his voice did not break, and he stepped back.

Sable did not speak.

Kester did not speak.

The community stood in silence for a long moment. Then, one by one, the survivors approached the plinth and placed something — a small object, a hand on Tev's shoulder, a whispered word. Pira was carried up by Sable. The child looked at Tev for a long time. She did not seem afraid. Sable whispered something to her, and Pira reached out one small hand and touched Tev's hand, the one with the flower, and Pira said, in her clear small voice: *Goodbye*.

Then Sable carried her back.

The community sealed the alcove. Tev's photograph — there had been one in her medical kit, Davour found it during the cleanup — was placed in the wall. The unnamed dead, eight of them, were placed in alcoves of their own. The community dispersed, slowly, and went back to the main chamber.

The whole ceremony took less than an hour.

It was, Kester thought, the most carefully sufficient ritual he had ever witnessed. There was nothing he could have added to it. There was nothing he could have taken away.

---

He went to the drop with Ivo at twenty-two hundred.

The drop point was three levels above the chamber, in a maintenance recess Ivo had identified that morning — *a place I've used for tool storage*, he said, *the kind of place an Internal Security operator would leave something if they were trying to be casual about it*. Kester accepted the explanation without questioning it. Ivo had been opening locks and finding shafts for fifteen years. His judgment about drop locations was not something Kester had reason to doubt.

They went in silence. Ivo led. Kester followed.

The maintenance recess was a small alcove behind a service panel that Ivo opened in seven seconds with a tool he kept in his belt. Inside the alcove, on a small shelf, was a data slate.

Ivo pulled it down. He handed it to Kester.

"It's yours," Ivo said.

Kester took the slate.

He turned it on. He read what was on it. He read it twice. He did not speak.

Ivo, behind him in the corridor, was doing something with a maintenance panel ten meters down the way. Kester registered this peripherally — Ivo was always doing something with maintenance panels, it was the man's natural state — and did not turn around to look. He was reading.

The slate contained a transport schedule. A single passenger of interest, manifest designation *Subject M-19* — Mouse, he understood, with the small cold lurch that had become familiar — would depart Tartaros at oh-four hundred local time on the morning of the fifth day from now. Departure point: the surface launch facility designated *Pier Three*, located approximately forty kilometers north of Facility Nine, accessible by the maintained vehicle road over the Vorrath Range. Destination: redacted in the slate's display, but the transit duration was given as *approximately fourteen hours*, which placed the destination — Kester ran the math against what he knew of Hegemony shuttle drives — within a star system that did not appear on any map he had ever filed.

The Harvester.

Below the schedule, in a small text field, was a brief note. The handwriting was Orrel's — Kester recognized it from nine years of administrative paperwork — and the note read:

*Mr. Vaile,*

*The schedule above is accurate. Pier Three is sparsely garrisoned at this time; resource demands at the other sites have thinned the security presence considerably. A Hegemony military shuttle will be available in Bay Two and is fueled for the transit duration. Authorization codes for ignition are appended below.*

*Mouse's transit will be on the same shuttle, arriving at Pier Three from the citadel approximately thirty minutes before launch. She will be sedated for the journey but stable. Her presence on the shuttle should not be a complication for your party.*

*I have arranged for your arrival at the Harvester to be uncontested. Customs processing will accept the manifest you carry. You will be met at the receiving bay by an aide who will escort you to the residence wing. I will see you there.*

*Bring whoever you like. I would ask, only, that whatever weapons you bring be left at the receiving bay's secure storage. This is courtesy, not coercion. You will be given them back when you depart, regardless of what you decide.*

*— B.O.*

*P.S. I look forward to it.*

Kester stood in the corridor and read the note three times.

The hand was Orrel's. The tone was Orrel's. The offer was Orrel's. There was nothing in any of it that surprised him.

What surprised him was the *generosity*. The shuttle would be in the bay. Authorization codes were appended. The launch facility was thinly staffed. Mouse would be on board. The arrival would be uncontested. They would even be allowed to bring weapons, only to surrender them at receiving — *courtesy, not coercion* — which Kester recognized, as a man who had filed Orrel's correspondence for nine years, as Orrel's particular way of saying *we both know how this works*.

The whole thing was an open door.

The door was held open by a man who knew, with a confidence Kester could not yet match, what would happen when they walked through it.

Behind him, Ivo finished whatever he had been doing with the maintenance panel — Kester heard the small click of a panel being closed — and Ivo said, in his level quiet voice: *Got what you needed?*

"Yes," Kester said.

"Then let's go."

They went back down to the chamber.

Kester did not know, then, that Ivo's adjustment to the maintenance panel had been a check-in. He would not know it for another two days.

---

The reading took place in the main chamber at midnight.

The survivors gathered around the long salvaged table where Kester's ration cards still lay in their underlined forest. Yenna brought her satchel from the palace. Chayne brought his drives, retrieved from the ventilation duct, the panels still bearing the sticky smudges where his hands had pulled them open in the night of the attack. Roon was carried over. Pira was asleep in a folding cot beside Sable's chair. Resh stood at the back of the chamber and did not sit down. Davour stood at the door and did not sit down. Ivo sat at the table.

The reading lasted six hours.

Yenna led. She had the documents organized — she had spent the day cross-indexing what she had photographed in the palace against what was in her notebooks, and she had a working hypothesis about the keyword cipher. She presented the hypothesis. Chayne tested it against his archive. Kester tested it against his memory of the paperwork he had signed. Sable tested it against the names of the dead.

It worked.

The cipher was elegant in the way bureaucratic ciphers are elegant — not difficult, just *consistent*. *Substrate* meant child. *Cohort* meant the collected children of a single research generation. *Retention* meant survival. *Yield* meant transferred to the Harvester. *Recovery* meant death and biological material extracted. *Threshold* meant the developmental stage at which a child became viable for the next phase. *Augmentation* meant the procedure itself. *Integration* meant the implantation of the behavioral compliance system Mouse had carried in her neck since infancy. *Programming* meant — and here Kester read the word three times to be sure — meant the doctrinal preparation, the years of carefully calibrated exposure to violence and grief and tested loyalties, that produced a finished subject.

Mouse had been *programmed*. The word was on her file. She had been programmed for nineteen years. The forced witnessing of the chamber attack, two nights ago, had been logged in her file as *terminal phase orientation, witnessing protocol, satisfactory*.

Sable read this. She did not respond. She continued reading.

Chayne's archive contained, the four of them now realized, the *low-value* version of the same operation. The Board had been allowing certain kinds of documents to leak into circulation for years, and Chayne had been faithfully collecting them, and the documents had been chosen by the Board's information officers specifically because they would mislead a determined reader into the wrong conclusions. Chayne's reading of the archive over fifteen years had produced a picture of a corrupt prison administration with brutal medical practices and political dissident persecution. That picture was true as far as it went. It did not contain the Cleanse. It did not contain the Harvester. It did not contain the seven sites or the cohort transport or the fourteen thousand children.

But Chayne's archive *did* contain — and this was the realization that took the four of them an hour of cross-referencing to reach — the names of the principals. Not under their initials. Under their *cover identities*. The Board members, when they were referred to in low-classification documents, were referred to by the public roles they held in the Hegemony's official structure. *A.M.* in the palace papers was, in Chayne's archive, *Senior Director Adran Marse, Hegemony Penal Affairs*. *D.K.* was *Senior Auditor Davreen Khoul, Hegemony Office of Federation Liaison*. *R.T.* was *Director Rion Tavers, Cresswell University Research Council* — Yenna's old institution, the source of Yenna's grant, the office that had signed off on her arrest. Yenna read the name. She closed her eyes for a long moment. She did not say anything. She kept reading.

The cover identities all checked out. The Board members were not shadow figures. They were *senior public officials*, every one of them, holding positions that gave them legitimate access to the institutional levers the Cleanse required — penal administration, oversight management, academic legitimization, financial routing, military procurement. The Board did not need to operate in secret because the Board *was* the system. They were doing the work in the open. The work simply did not look like what it was.

Eight names. The ninth was the Architect.

The Architect's cover identity was not in Chayne's archive.

The Architect had been careful.

By oh-three hundred the four of them — Kester, Yenna, Chayne, and now Sable, who had stopped contributing names and had started contributing *patterns* — had assembled a working portrait of the Cleanse from the operational level upward. Seven sites, each producing a cohort of children. The cohorts converging at the Harvester on a roughly biennial schedule. The Harvester's purpose: producing a class of augmented operatives — not soldiers in the conventional sense, but *finished subjects*, individuals with embedded behavioral compliance, conditioned loyalty, and physical augmentations that made them deployable in environments and operational categories ordinary infantry could not survive. Approximately fourteen thousand subjects produced over twenty-two years. The Hegemony had been quietly absorbing these subjects into specialized military, intelligence, and security roles for over a decade. They were already in the field. They were already operating. Some of them, by now, were probably commanding others.

The next war, when it came, would be fought partly by them.

Kester sat in his chair and looked at what they had assembled and understood, with a clarity that arrived too late and too completely to be of any use, that the broadcast had been a small thing.

The broadcast had revealed Wing Seven. Wing Seven was one wing, on one site, of seven sites, run by one Board, that had been operating for twenty-two years. The broadcast had been a single drop of water thrown at a fire that had been burning since before Kester had children of his own.

He looked at Sable.

She was looking at the documents.

She was nodding, slowly, the way a person nods who has finally been given the picture they have been demanding for years and who is not surprised by the picture, only tired by it.

She said, very quietly: "The archive has to go home."

"Yes," Yenna said.

"Andra Prime. Federation observers. Opposition press. Adeyemi's family. Whoever you can reach, Yenna. The picture has to be assembled there. They cannot spin a fourteen-thousand-children dataset. They can spin Wing Seven. They cannot spin this."

"I know."

"You will go. Chayne will go with you. Pira will go with you."

Yenna looked up. "Pira — "

"The Harvester is no place for her. Andra Prime is no place for her either, but it is more of a place for her than the Harvester. She will go with you. Chayne can carry her when she gets tired. You will be a family of three traveling. The Hegemony's surveillance net is degraded — Yenna, the file the Board has on you is two years old, there are paths in the system you can use. You know the paths. You know the names." Sable was not looking at Yenna directly. She was looking at the papers. "You will go on the morning shuttle from the launch base, after we take the base. You will fly under a civilian transit code we will manufacture for you. You will have eighteen hours of invisibility before the Hegemony pieces together what we did at the base. Use them. Get to the surface of Andra Prime. Get to your contacts. Begin the work."

"Sable, the rest of you — "

"We will go to the Harvester. We will get Mouse. We will get whatever evidence we can carry. We will come back." Sable paused. "Or we will not. Either way, the archive has to be on Andra Prime when the Hegemony figures out what has happened. The archive does not depend on our return. The archive *cannot* depend on our return. Do you understand."

Yenna was crying, silently, the way she had cried in the palace.

"I understand," she said.

"Good."

Sable stood up.

"We move at first light," she said. "Sleep if you can. Pack what you need. Yenna, write down your contacts on Andra Prime tonight, in case — in case the journey is harder than we hope. Make sure two people know the names. Chayne should have them. So should Roon, if he is staying."

"Roon is staying," Roon said, from his litter. His voice was thin but clear. "I cannot travel and I would slow you down. Dren and Hess will stay with me. We will hold what we can hold of the chamber for as long as we can hold it, and if the Board comes back we will move deeper. There are passages I have not shown you, Sable. There are passages even Ivo does not know about. I have been keeping them in reserve. I will keep them in reserve."

Sable looked at Roon for a long moment.

"Thank you, Roon."

"Get the girl, Sable. Get the truth home. The rest of us will be here, or we will not, and either way you will have done what you went to do."

Sable nodded.

She walked away from the table.

The reading was over.

---

The hours before dawn were quiet.

Yenna sat with Chayne in a corner of the chamber and made her list of contacts on Andra Prime. There were six names. Two of them were academics who had survived after Yenna's arrest. One was a journalist who had written about disappeared persons under a pen name and who Yenna believed was Adeyemi's primary source in the Federation press. One was Adeyemi's husband — a man Yenna had never met but whose name she had known for years from the dedications in his wife's published reports, a man who would now be in mourning and would also be, perhaps, capable of acting on what they brought him. One was a sympathetic lawyer in the Federation oversight apparatus. One was a former colleague of Chayne's from the municipal archives on Korya, who had escaped his arrest and was rumored to be living quietly under a different name on Andra Prime's outer settlements.

Six names. Six possible doors. Yenna wrote them down and gave one copy to Chayne and one copy to Sable and one copy to Roon, and Roon hid his copy in a place even Dren and Hess did not know about, because Roon was a man whose paranoia had now been validated by the chamber attack and who was no longer willing to assume the chamber was a safe place for paper.

Kester did not sleep.

He sat at his table with his ration cards and he wrote, for the last time, what he had learned. He did not know if the cards would survive. He did not know if anything in the chamber would survive. He wrote anyway, because writing was the work he could do, and the work was the only thing that had not yet been taken from him.

He wrote about the seven sites. He wrote about the Harvester. He wrote the cover identities of the Board principals. He wrote the keyword cipher in full, two columns, code on the left and meaning on the right. He wrote Mouse's transport schedule. He wrote Pier Three. He wrote what was in Orrel's note, word for word, because his memory was good and because the note itself was now the slate that Sable had taken into her own keeping, and Kester did not know whether he would see it again.

He wrote, at the bottom of the last card, in capital letters: *THE BOARD HAS BEEN CHANNELING US. EVERY MOVE WE HAVE MADE SINCE THE BROADCAST WAS ON RAILS THEY LAID. THE HARVESTER IS THE END OF THE RAIL. WE ARE GOING ANYWAY.*

He underlined the last sentence.

He set the pencil down.

He looked at Sable across the chamber.

She was sitting beside Pira's cot. She was not sleeping. She was watching the child sleep, the way a person watches a child sleep when they are trying to memorize the face. Pira's small mouth was slightly open. Pira's hand was curled against her cheek. Sable was sitting with her hands open on her knees, palms up, the way she had sat on the chamber floor the morning Kester had found her.

Kester understood, watching her, that Sable had been spending the last few hours saying goodbye to Pira in the way she could — silently, by sitting beside her, by not waking her, by being present for the hours she would never have again. Sable would do the verbal goodbye in the morning, briefly, because Pira was four and would not benefit from a long farewell. The real goodbye was happening now, in the quiet, in the chamber where everyone else was busy with maps and lists and lasts.

Kester turned away.

He thought about his own daughter, Lira, on Andra Prime. He thought about her being twenty-two years old and not knowing whether her father was alive. He thought about the strawberries under sodium lamps. He thought, with the small cold administrative clarity he had been using for years to avoid feeling things, that if Yenna succeeded in her mission, then within perhaps a month Lira would learn that her father was the man whose paperwork had built Wing Seven, and that the learning would be public, and that it would happen to her in a city of sixty million people most of whom would be reading the same news at the same time.

He did not know what she would do with it.

He did not know what he wanted her to do with it.

He closed his eyes.

He thought: *If you hate me afterward, you will be right to hate me. If you understand, eventually, why I am at the Harvester rather than coming home to you, you will be a kinder daughter than I deserved. Either way, Lira. Either way, I love you. I have always loved you. I just spent nine years not letting it matter, and now it is too late to make it matter in any way that will reach you in time.*

He kept his eyes closed for a long time.

---

Dawn was an abstraction in the under-levels, but the chamber's clock said oh-six-hundred when Sable said *we go*, and the survivors moved.

Roon, on his litter, was carried back to the deeper rooms by Dren and Hess. Sable knelt beside the litter for a moment. She did not speak. She put her hand on Roon's hand, and he closed his fingers around hers, and they stayed like that for perhaps thirty seconds. Then she stood up and walked away.

The split happened at the chamber's door.

Yenna was carrying her satchel of palace papers and a sealed case of the photograph drives, copied from the originals, with a backup buried in a panel Roon had prepared in the deeper rooms. Chayne was carrying a smaller bag of his own — his archive's original drives, his notebooks, a few personal items Kester did not see. Pira was held in Sable's arms. She had been awake for ten minutes. She was eating a small piece of dried fruit Tev had set aside two days ago, which Davour had retrieved from Tev's medical station that morning without saying why she was retrieving it.

Sable carried Pira to Chayne. She knelt down so the child was at Chayne's eye level. Chayne, in his folding chair, opened his arms.

"Pira," Sable said, very softly.

"Yes."

"This is Chayne. You know Chayne. You are going to go with Chayne for a while. Chayne is going to take care of you. He is going to take you to a place that is warm and that has food and that has people who will be kind to you. Yenna is going with you. Yenna is going to help."

Pira looked at Chayne. She had known him her whole life. She did not seem confused.

"Where are you going," she said to Sable.

"I am going somewhere else, sweetheart. I have to do something. I will come find you when I am done."

"When."

Sable did not answer immediately. She was looking at Pira's face. Her own face was, Kester saw, very still — not soft anymore, not exactly, but *held*, in the way a person holds their face when they are determined not to lie to a child but also cannot afford to tell the truth.

"As soon as I can," Sable said. "I do not know how long. But as soon as I can. I promise."

Pira considered this.

"Will you remember me," Pira said.

Sable made a sound. It was not quite a sob. It was the sound a person makes when they have been hit very hard and are trying not to make the louder sound that comes after.

"Yes," she said. "Yes. I will remember you. I will remember you every day. I will remember your name and your face and the way you said *goodbye* to Tev yesterday, and I will remember everything, Pira. I will remember everything."

"Okay."

Pira turned in Sable's arms and reached for Chayne. Chayne took her. The transfer was quick and gentle and the kind of transfer Pira had clearly experienced many times in her short life — handed from one set of arms to another by adults who had decided things she was too small to know about. She curled against Chayne's shoulder. She did not cry. She put one hand into Chayne's beard and held on.

Sable stood up.

She did not look at Pira again.

She turned to Yenna. "Go. The civilian transit shuttle leaves at oh-eight hundred from the base's east bay. Davour will brief you on the timing while we are en route. Once you are in the air you do not stop until you are on Andra Prime. Do you understand."

"Yes."

"I am sorry, Yenna. I am sorry to send you with this much. I am sorry I am not coming with you."

"Sable."

"Yes."

"You are coming back. Tell me you are coming back."

Sable looked at her.

"I will try," she said.

It was not what Yenna had asked for. Yenna's face registered that. Yenna nodded anyway, because Yenna was a woman who had stopped requiring the people she loved to lie to her.

Sable turned to Chayne. She put her hand on his shoulder, lightly. She did not speak. He did not speak. He nodded to her once, and she nodded once back, and that was the goodbye between them — the shortest one, between two people who had read each other's archives and who did not need words to mark the parting.

Chayne carried Pira out, with Yenna walking beside him, and Davour following with their small cases.

The remaining party — Sable, Kester, Resh, Ivo, and Davour, who would return after seeing Yenna's group to the shuttle — assembled at the chamber's south exit and began the trek to the surface.

Kester looked back, once, before they left.

The chamber was quiet. Roon's litter was no longer visible. The salvaged table where the reading had happened was still strewn with papers — Yenna had taken what she needed, Kester had taken his ration cards, but the bulk of the working materials remained, abandoned, the surface of an investigation no one would be coming back to. The ceiling lights hummed. The air recyclers cycled. The chamber had been a society for ten years and was now a room.

He turned away.

He kept walking.

---

The surface of Tartaros was cold in a way Kester's body had forgotten.

He had not been on the surface in eleven years. He had forgotten the feeling of the suit — the slight pressure imbalance against the skin, the close hum of the rebreather, the way the helmet's faceplate fogged at the edges if you breathed too hard. He had forgotten the *light*. The light on Tartaros was not light. It was a kind of brown-red haze, the sun a small white smear behind the moon's perpetual atmospheric dust, and the shadows fell long and ambiguous across the surface in a way that made distances impossible to judge.

Above him, Moloch hung in its place.

It was the first time Kester had seen Moloch in years.

The gas giant filled half the visible sky. It was so large and so close that the edge of it curved away beyond the horizon in both directions, and the great red eye — the perpetual storm Kester had seen from the tram window what felt like a lifetime ago — was directly overhead, watching him from above with the slow indifference of weather. Kester stood on the surface of Tartaros in his pressure suit and looked up at Moloch and thought, with the small clear discipline of a man who had spent his life filing things, *my daughter has seen this from her window every evening of her life. She has been looking at the front of this. I have been working on the back of it. We have been on the same side of the same star for twenty-two years and we have never seen it together.*

The vehicle was a tracked transport — the kind the Hegemony used for surface maintenance work, the kind Kester had filed maintenance reports for during his early years on Tartaros. Davour drove. Sable sat in the passenger seat. Kester, Resh, and Ivo sat in the rear cabin with the gear.

The road over the Vorrath Range was a maintained route — graded, marked, swept by automated rovers — and the transport could make Pier Three in approximately three hours. They had time. They were not yet behind a clock.

Kester sat in the rear cabin and watched the surface scroll past the small armored window. He thought about Lira. He thought about the strawberries. He thought about the version of his life in which he had stayed on Andra Prime, in which he had refused the Tartaros posting eleven years ago, in which he had been a junior administrator in some municipal office and had eaten dinner with his daughter every night. The version of his life would have been small. The version would have been ordinary. The version would have been, he understood now, the only version worth wanting.

He had not wanted it at the time. He had wanted the senior posting and the better salary and the chance to advance. He had wanted, as he understood now, the specific thing Orrel had wanted after Calderon Trench: to stop being a body in someone else's accounting. He had not realized that the alternative to being a body was being the man who counted them.

He sat with this for the duration of the drive.

He did not say anything.

Sable, in the front, did not say anything either. Davour drove. Resh stared out the window. Ivo, beside Kester, occasionally checked the transport's cabin systems with the small pre-occupied attention of a man who was used to checking things.

Pier Three rose on the horizon two hours and forty minutes after they left.

It was, as Orrel had described, sparsely garrisoned. Kester counted, through the binoculars Davour passed him, perhaps fifteen guard silhouettes patrolling the perimeter — about a third of what a properly staffed launch facility this size would have. The Board's resource demands at the other sites were real. The base was running thin.

*Or it had been thinned for them*, Kester thought.

He looked at Sable.

Sable had the binoculars now. She was studying the base. Her face under the helmet visor was unreadable.

"It looks like he said it would," she said, over the suit comm.

"Yes," Davour said.

"That makes me trust it less, not more."

"Yes."

"We go anyway."

"Yes."

She lowered the binoculars.

She turned her head, slightly, and looked at Kester. He met her eyes through the visor. She did not say anything. She did not have to. He understood, with the kind of mute administrative clarity he had been using for hours now, that she was telling him: *I know this is a road they laid. I know we are going down it. I am going down it anyway. So are you. We will see each other on the other side or we will not. Either way, this is what we are doing.*

He nodded, once.

She turned back to the base.

"Davour," she said. "Brief us."

Davour began the briefing.

The chapter was over and the next thing was beginning, and Kester sat in the rear of a tracked transport on the surface of Tartaros under the great red eye of Moloch and listened to Kell Davour, formerly of the Hegemony Military Police, walk them through the assault on a launch base whose vulnerabilities had been written specifically for them.

## Chapter Nine: The Long Quiet

Davour's briefing took nine minutes.

Pier Three had three perimeter checkpoints, two interior security stations, a control room overlooking the launch bays, and a flight crew quarters where the off-duty pilots slept. Total guard count, by Davour's estimate from the binoculars and from her seven years of Hegemony Military Police experience reading bases like this: twelve to fifteen active personnel, of whom no more than four would be combat-rated, the rest being base security with light training and slow reaction times. The civilian shuttle Yenna's group needed was in the east bay, scheduled for departure at oh-eight hundred under a routine logistics manifest. The military shuttle for Sable's group was in Bay Two, fueled for fourteen hours of transit, with the authorization codes Orrel had provided.

The plan was simple. Davour, Sable, and Resh would go in through the west service hatch — a maintenance entrance Davour had used during her MP years, which was not on the active patrol's primary attention list. They would clear the interior in stages: control room first, then the security stations, then the crew quarters. Kester and Ivo would stay with the transport at the perimeter and handle the loading of Yenna's group onto the civilian shuttle once the interior was clear. The whole operation was estimated at twenty-two minutes from breach to first launch.

"Kester," Davour said. "You won't fire."

"No."

"Good. You're not trained. Stay with the transport. Help Yenna's people load when they arrive. Don't approach the bays until I clear you on the comm."

"Understood."

"Ivo." Davour turned to him. "You're with Kester. Help with the loading. If anything goes wrong on our side, you and Kester get Yenna's group out on the civilian shuttle and you abandon the military shuttle. Their flight matters more than ours. Clear?"

"Clear," Ivo said.

Sable was checking her sidearm.

"Twenty-two minutes," she said. "Let's go."

---

It took nineteen.

Kester sat in the rear cabin of the tracked transport with Ivo and listened to the operation unfold over the suit comm. Davour's voice was flat and procedural — *west hatch breached, no contact* — *control room cleared, two down* — *security station east cleared, one down* — *crew quarters cleared, four down, all sleeping when we arrived* — and Sable's voice was less procedural, lower, the voice of a woman who was performing competently without quite being present in what she was performing. Resh's voice did not appear on the comm at all. Resh, Kester realized at some point during the operation, had been issued a sidearm but had not been given firing instructions. Resh was on the operation as a body, not as a fighter. Sable wanted him close. She did not want him to kill anyone.

When Davour's voice came over the comm at minute nineteen — *interior secured, civilian shuttle inbound from Yenna's transport, we are clear for loading* — Kester realized that he had been holding his breath in irregular, shallow patterns for nearly twenty minutes, and that his neck and shoulders ached as if he had been lifting something.

He had not been lifting anything.

He had been listening to his friends kill seven strangers, and his body had responded.

He exhaled.

He climbed out of the rear cabin.

The base was quiet. Pier Three's hangars opened to the surface above through enormous mag-sealed doors that rolled back on tracks when the launch sequence initiated; for now, the doors were closed, and the surface was cold and dim under Moloch's red light, and the bodies of the perimeter guards were small dark shapes on the concrete.

Davour came out of the western hatch with Sable and Resh. Sable's helmet was tucked under one arm. Her face was the same face it had been all morning — soft, contained, present. She had killed two people in the last nineteen minutes. Her face did not register this in any way Kester could read.

Yenna, Chayne, and Pira arrived in their own tracked transport ten minutes later. The civilian shuttle was already on its taxi rail. Davour walked Yenna through the manifest — *you are listed as Hegemony Education Authority returning from a corrections-system inspection, you will not be questioned at Andra Prime customs because the manifest is signed by an officer who does not exist but whose seal we have replicated, the shuttle's transponder will read clean for the duration of the transit* — and Yenna nodded and absorbed the information and asked exactly two clarifying questions, both of them sharp.

Pira held Chayne's hand at the foot of the loading ramp.

She was looking around the hangar with the wide quiet eyes of a four-year-old who had never seen a hangar before. The space was vast. The shuttle was huge. The mag-sealed doors above them were larger than any structure she had encountered. She held Chayne's hand and she looked, and she did not speak.

Sable did not approach her.

This was, Kester realized later, deliberate. Sable had said her goodbye at the chamber. Approaching Pira here would be walking it back. Sable was going to let the girl board the shuttle without a second goodbye, because a second goodbye would be for Sable, not for Pira, and Sable had decided several hours ago that nothing on the Harvester run would be done for herself.

Pira saw Sable across the hangar. Pira raised one small hand, briefly, in something that was not quite a wave. Sable raised her own hand, equally briefly. The moment was perhaps three seconds long.

Then Chayne and Pira and Yenna walked up the ramp.

The shuttle's hatch sealed.

The mag-sealed hangar doors above them began to roll back.

The civilian shuttle launched at oh-seven-fifty-two, eight minutes ahead of schedule, into the Tartaros morning sky.

It was tracked, because all shuttles were tracked, but it was tracked under a civilian transit code that had been laundered through three Hegemony bureaucratic layers and that would not produce an alert flag in the Federation customs system on Andra Prime. Yenna had eighteen hours before the Hegemony pieced together what had happened at Pier Three. By the time the alarm went up, she would be on the surface.

Kester watched the shuttle clear the doors and rise into the brown-red haze of the upper atmosphere. He watched it until it was a small bright point against the sky, and then he watched the sky where the point had been until even the afterimage faded.

He thought: *That is my daughter going home in a stranger's arms.*

He did not know what he meant by it. He thought it anyway. He stood in the cold of the hangar and watched the empty sky and let the thought sit in him, and after a moment he understood that what he meant by it was that some part of him had been hoping, perhaps without admitting it, that Yenna's group would somehow find Lira on Andra Prime — that Yenna would knock on a door and his daughter would be on the other side of it, and that the archive would reach her before the news did, and that she would understand what he had done and why he was at the Harvester and that she would not have to assemble it from a press release.

He understood, watching the empty sky, that this was not going to happen.

Lira would learn the truth from the news, like everyone else. He had given up the right to be the one who told her, eleven years ago when he took the Tartaros posting, and that right was not something he could now take back by sending an academic stranger to her door. The archive would reach the Federation press through whatever channels Yenna could open. Lira would read about her father in a newspaper. There would be no version of this in which she heard it from him first.

He had known this for hours.

He had only just allowed himself to feel it.

He turned away from the hangar doors.

He walked back to the military shuttle.

---

The military shuttle in Bay Two was a Class III Hegemony transport — a long-haul vessel, designed for fourteen-hour transits, with sleeping berths for eight and a small cabin for the flight crew. Davour took the pilot's seat. Sable sat beside her in the co-pilot's chair. Kester, Resh, and Ivo took the rear cabin.

The launch was uneventful. The authorization codes Orrel had provided were valid. The shuttle's transponder identified it as a Hegemony Internal Security transport on a manifest that Davour did not read aloud and that Kester chose not to ask about. The mag-sealed doors above Bay Two rolled back. The shuttle rose. Tartaros fell away beneath them, the great red eye of Moloch filled the upper viewport for several long minutes, and then the moon's gravity released them and they were in transit.

The Harvester was fourteen hours away.

The shuttle was very quiet.

---

Ivo went to sleep almost immediately.

This was, Kester thought at the time, sensible. Ivo had been on his feet for thirty-six hours. He had climbed shafts, planned operations, retrieved a drop, helped pack the chamber, and ridden across the Vorrath Range in a tracked transport. The man was older than the rest of them. He needed sleep. He took one of the rear berths, lay down in his suit liner with the helmet beside him on the floor, and was breathing slowly within three minutes.

Resh sat in the cabin's small fold-down seat and stared at the bulkhead.

Kester sat opposite him and tried, for a while, to do nothing. He had the photograph drives in a sealed pouch under his suit liner — Sable had insisted, before they left the chamber, that Kester carry a backup in case Yenna's group did not make it. The pouch was warm against his ribs. He could feel the slight angles of the drives through the fabric.

After perhaps an hour, Davour spoke from the cockpit, over the cabin comm: *Yenna's group is clear of Tartaros space. Their transit is nominal. We have a window.*

Kester sat up.

"What window."

"Comm window. The shuttle's long-range relay is up. We can talk to Yenna's shuttle on a tight beam if we do it quickly. They'll have a window of their own when they reach Andra Prime's near-orbit, but they won't be able to reach us once they enter the atmosphere. If you want to say anything to Chayne or Yenna, now is the time."

Sable said, from the co-pilot's seat: *Open it.*

The comm clicked.

Yenna's voice came through after a moment. "We're here. Chayne is here. Pira is asleep."

"Yenna," Sable said. "How is the transit."

"Clean. The transponder is reading exactly the way Davour said it would. We have not been challenged. Pira ate a small meal an hour ago and went down without protest. She has been very calm, Sable. She has not asked for you. I want you to know that, because I think you would want to know."

There was a pause from the co-pilot's seat.

Then Sable said: *Thank you, Yenna.*

"Chayne is here. He wants to speak."

Chayne's voice, when it came, was thin but steady. "Sable. Kester. I have been working on the archive during the transit. I have been cross-referencing the Board principals against the documents I had on the original drives. There is something I want to tell you both."

"Go ahead, Chayne," Sable said.

"There is a name that I had in my archive that I could never place. It appears in eleven separate documents over a span of six years, always in technical contexts — equipment authorization, personnel travel, medical authorization. The name is *Volner*. Doctor Henna Volner. I had never been able to find a public Hegemony identity to match it. The name was not one of the Board principals, as far as I could see, and it was not Vorlith or any of her known associates. I thought it was perhaps a clerical error. I thought it was perhaps a name that had been used as a placeholder in some early documentation system."

"And?"

"And in Yenna's palace papers, the name appears once. In a memo dated 2364. The memo is a personnel transfer authorization. It moves Doctor Henna Volner from Site Four — wherever Site Four is — to Tartaros, on a temporary rotation, for the purpose of *general medical support and asset cultivation*."

Kester's hand moved, very slightly, toward his pouch.

He stopped the motion.

He looked across the cabin at Ivo, who was still asleep, his breathing slow and even, his face turned slightly toward the bulkhead in the dim cabin light.

"*Asset cultivation*," Sable said, slowly, from the co-pilot's seat.

"Yes," Chayne said. "It is one of the keywords. I cross-referenced it. It appears, in Yenna's papers, in three other places. In each instance, it refers to the recruitment of an internal asset — someone inside a target population, selected for compromise, cultivated for information. The keyword is specific. It is not a general medical term. It is a recruitment term."

"Doctor Henna Volner," Sable said. "When was she on Tartaros."

"From 2364 to 2370, on rotation. She would come for stays of two to three weeks at a time, several times a year. She used the citadel medical wing for her rotations. Her name appears on the wing's duty rosters during her presence."

Sable was quiet for a long moment.

Behind Kester, in the rear cabin, Pira's small voice came through the comm.

She had not been asleep, after all. She had been listening, the way children listen when adults are speaking words they do not understand, the way a child stays awake when she has been told to sleep because something interesting is happening in the next room. Her voice, when it came, was bright and slightly muffled by what Kester guessed was a blanket pulled up to her chin, and it said:

"Volner. That's the doctor's name. Ivo says it sometimes."

*Yes.*

*Did you hear what the child said.*

*Yes.*

*Yes.*

Her face was no longer soft.

It was, Kester realized with a cold lurch of something close to relief, the face he had been waiting to see all morning. The face of a woman who had finally been given the thing she could move on. The softness — the dangerous gentleness that had frightened him since the morning of chapter seven — had not disappeared, but it had been *located*. It had a target. It had a name.

He told her.

He told her about Ivo's eight-second adjustment to the maintenance panel at the drop. He told her about how Ivo had known the location of the drop point in a way Kester had not at the time questioned. He told her about how the launch base's vulnerabilities had matched Orrel's letter exactly, and about how he had registered this as suspicious without yet knowing the shape of the suspicion. He told her about the small things — Ivo's habits with the chamber's access panels, Ivo's depth of knowledge about the citadel's systems, Ivo's particular history of having been a prisoner before the Undersiders rather than coming up from the chamber population, which was the standard recruitment path for the under-levels' technical staff.

"Kester," Sable said.

"Yes."

"You filed paperwork for nine years and did not see what was happening in Wing Seven. Forgive yourself for missing one man over three days. We do not have the time."

He was silent.

"What is on the comm," Sable said to Davour.

"Yenna and Chayne are still there. They heard Pira's sentence."

"Patch us back."

The comm clicked.

"Yenna," Sable said. "Chayne. Are you still there."

"We are here."

"We heard."

"Volner is Ivo's recruiter. He has been a Board asset for at least seven years, possibly longer. The chamber attack was timed to his information. The launch base vulnerabilities were laid for us through him. The drop was retrieved with him as the second hand. Everything we have done since the broadcast has had his thumbprint."

Chayne said: "Sable. The four uncounted children."

"He hedged," Chayne said. "I want you to know that, before you do whatever you are going to do. He was a traitor. He was also a man who held back. The four children we still have, the ones who are with Roon now — they are alive because Ivo did not give them up."

The comm closed.

Then she turned to Kester.

Ivo did not fight when Kester woke him.

Kester had been preparing himself, on the short walk back through the cabin, for a struggle. He had been preparing himself to grab Ivo's wrists, to press him down on the berth, to call for Davour. He had been preparing himself for violence, in the small cold administrative way he prepared himself for things. None of it was necessary.

Ivo opened his eyes. He looked at Kester. He looked at Kester's face. He read whatever was on it.

He said, very quietly: "How."

"Pira."

Ivo closed his eyes for a moment.

"The child," he said.

"Yes."

"Yes. All right."

He sat up. He did not reach for his sidearm. He did not move quickly. He moved like a man who had been expecting this conversation for a long time and who had decided, at some point years ago, that when it came he would not make it harder than it had to be. He stood up. He looked at Resh, who had stood up at the cabin's far end and who was holding his sidearm now and who looked very young and very uncertain.

"Sit down, Resh," Ivo said gently. "I am not going to do anything. Sit down. This is not yours."

Resh sat down.

Ivo walked toward the cockpit. He did not look back. Kester walked behind him.

In the cockpit Sable was waiting in the co-pilot's chair, turned to face the cockpit door. Davour was at the controls, half-turned, watching. The cockpit was small and the four of them filled it, and the door behind them was closed, and the porthole showed the long dark of transit and the small white smear of a star that was not the Hegemony's home sun and that Kester did not know the name of.

"Sit," Sable said.

Ivo sat in the small jump seat behind the pilot's chair.

Sable looked at him.

She did not speak immediately. She was, Kester realized, *studying* him, the way a person studies a face they think they know and are now seeing for the first time. Her own face was no longer soft. Her own face had in it, finally, the thing Kester had been waiting to see — anger. Pure anger, clean and undiluted, without the dangerous gentleness she had been carrying since the morning of Aleya's death.

"Talk," she said.

Ivo talked.

He talked for perhaps eleven minutes. He did not embellish. He did not justify. He told them about his prison sentence — fourteen years for industrial sabotage, a charge that had been technically accurate, a strike action gone wrong on Andra Prime that had killed three workers in a refinery accident and that had been prosecuted not as labor activism but as terrorism. He told them about his wife, Maela, and his son, Theron, who had been six when he was sentenced. He told them about the medical issue that had brought him to the citadel in 2364 — a kidney condition, treatable, but requiring equipment the prison's general infirmary did not have. He told them about Doctor Volner. He told them what she had said about Maela and Theron. He told them about the photographs she had shown him. He told them how she had let him hold the photographs for several minutes before taking them back.

"I did not say yes," he said. "Not that day. I did not say yes for many weeks. I told myself I would refuse. Then she came back during my next medical visit, and I did not refuse, and she explained what would be expected of me, and she sent me back to general population, and a month later I was transferred to a labor rotation where the Undersiders found me. I did not know they would find me. I learned later that the transfer had been arranged. I had been delivered to them. I joined them. I worked. I told myself, for many years, that I was hedging — that I gave Volner enough to keep the home alive but not enough to compromise the Undersiders. I told myself this. I do not know if it was true. I think parts of it were true. I think parts of it were what I needed to think to keep doing the work. The two are not always the same."

Sable was listening without expression.

"I did not see Volner after 2370," Ivo said. "She was rotated out. My handler became a procedure. Dead drops. Maintenance panels. I have not spoken to a person about my work for the Board in five years. The information has been flowing into a system, and the system has been keeping its end of the agreement, in that the allowance payments have continued and the boy has been at school and Maela has been — Maela has been told I died on Tartaros, ten years ago. She remarried. The boy is fourteen. He thinks his stepfather is his father. I have known these things for some time. I have not allowed myself to fully think about them. Thinking about them would have stopped me from being able to continue."

He paused.

"Two days ago I made an adjustment to a maintenance panel during the drop," he said. "I want to confirm this for you. The adjustment was a check-in. It told my handlers that the drop had been received and that the team was reading it. I do not know precisely what they did with the information. I assume they passed it to Orrel. I assume the launch base was thinned for us in part because of the check-in. I assume the timing of the chamber attack was informed by what I had previously reported about the expedition."

"Did you give them the cohort count," Sable said.

"I gave them forty-three."

"There were forty-seven."

"There were forty-seven. I gave them the four I would not give. The four I — " He stopped. He swallowed. "The four I told myself were my line. The line I would not cross. The line that meant I was not entirely what I was."

"Their names," Sable said.

Ivo gave her four names. They were, Kester noticed, the four youngest children Sable had been hiding. Two of them were the children Roon was now sheltering in the deeper passages.

"Anything else," Sable said.

"I gave them everything else, Sable. Everything. The chamber's layout. The shaft system. The cipher Chayne was working with — though that was one I gave reluctantly, because I did not understand at the time what they would do with it. I gave them the names of the Undersiders. I gave them assessments of the principal members. I gave them — I gave them a profile of you, Sable. Years of profile. Your habits, your weaknesses, the people you trusted, the people you did not. I gave them what I knew about Mouse. About Tev. About Pell, before he died — I gave them Pell's role in the broadcast plan, before the broadcast went out. They did not stop the broadcast because they did not want to stop it. They wanted it to happen. I helped them arrange for it to happen the way they wanted it to happen."

"Pell," Sable said.

"Yes."

Sable's hands, on her knees in the co-pilot's chair, did not move.

"All right," she said.

Ivo waited.

"Do you have anything to ask of me, Ivo."

Ivo looked at her.

"No," he said. "I am not going to ask you for anything. I do not deserve to ask you for anything. I will tell you what is true and you will do what you need to do, and that is the entire transaction. I am sorry, Sable. I am sorry, Kester. I am sorry."

"You have not said *Tev*," Sable said.

Ivo closed his eyes.

"Tev," he said. "I am sorry, Tev."

Sable nodded, slowly.

She turned to Davour.

"How long until the Harvester."

"Six hours, fourteen minutes."

"How long until atmospheric entry."

"Five hours, forty-eight minutes, give or take. The descent profile is a long approach — they want shuttles down at low altitude for a stretch before the receiving bay so the customs sweep has time to read the manifest. We will be in atmosphere for about ten minutes before we land."

"All right." Sable stood up. She did this slowly, with the deliberation of a woman who had been making a decision throughout Ivo's talk and who was now ready to execute it. "We do not throw him out into space. We do not waste a body inside the shuttle. We do not give the Hegemony a corpse to find at the bay."

She turned to Ivo.

"You go off in atmosphere. Once we are low and the air is thick enough that opening the hatch will not vacuum the cabin, we will open the hatch. You will be at the hatch. You will jump."

Ivo did not respond immediately.

"That is the kindness," Sable said. "It is the only one I have. If you jump, you choose the angle. You roll when you hit. You give yourself a chance. If you do not jump, I will shoot you in the leg and I will kick you out. The leg is what you get either way. The choice is whether the leg is the only injury or whether you are bleeding when you go off the platform. Do you understand."

"I understand."

"Good."

Sable looked at Davour.

"Find a window. Somewhere remote. The night side if we cross it, otherwise a stretch of terrain without facility traffic. I want him on the ground at low altitude with no surveillance witnessing the drop. We take the leg in atmosphere, drop him, close the hatch, continue to the bay. The shuttle's transponder reads clean for the whole operation. The customs station does not see anything."

"Copy. I'll route us through the southern continent on the inbound. There's a stretch of grassland that doesn't have any monitoring relays for about four hundred kilometers. We can take him low over the middle of it."

"Good."

Sable turned back to Ivo.

"Sit in the rear cabin. Do not speak to Resh. Do not approach the controls. Kester will sit with you. We will tie your hands when we begin the descent so that there is no ambiguity at the hatch. Do you have any final thing to say."

Ivo looked at her.

"No," he said. "Nothing that will help."

"All right."

He stood up. He walked back through the cockpit door, into the rear cabin, with the steady careful walk of a man who had not yet been killed and who did not know how to feel about the fact.

Kester followed him.

In the rear cabin, Resh was sitting in his fold-down seat with his sidearm in his lap. He looked up at Kester, and at Ivo behind Kester, and his face did not change.

"What's happening," Resh said quietly.

"Ivo is going to sit," Kester said. "He is going to sit until we begin the descent. I am going to sit with him. You can sleep, Resh. I will keep watch."

"Why does he need to sit."

"Resh."

"Why does he need to sit, Kester."

Kester looked at the boy. Resh was eighteen. He had been silent for three days. He was holding a sidearm that he had not yet been authorized to fire.

"He fed information to the Board," Kester said. "For years. The chamber attack was because of him. Pell was because of him. Tev was because of him. Sable will explain the rest when she is ready. For now he sits. We do not hurt him. We do not yell. We sit."

Resh looked at Ivo for a long time.

His face did not change.

After a moment he set his sidearm down on the small fold-out table beside his seat and he turned his face to the wall and he did not look at Ivo again for the rest of the transit.

Kester sat across from Ivo in the rear cabin.

The cabin was very quiet.

After a long time — perhaps an hour, perhaps two — Ivo said, in a voice so quiet Kester almost did not hear it:

"I am sorry, Mr. Vaile. For what it is worth. I am sorry. I liked you. I liked all of you. I want you to know that. The liking was real. The other thing was also real. Both were real. I do not know how to explain it better than that."

Kester thought about it.

He thought about Tev, and Aleya, and Pell, and the eight unnamed dead in the chamber. He thought about Pira's small bright voice on the comm, saying *Volner. That's the doctor's name. Ivo says it sometimes.* He thought about Mouse, six hours ahead of them in another shuttle, sedated, on a transport that the Board had arranged.

He thought about the man across from him.

He said: "I believe you."

Ivo closed his eyes.

"Thank you," he said.

He did not say anything else for hours.

The Harvester planet was visible through the cockpit porthole at hour eleven of the transit.

Kester saw it briefly, when he went forward to deliver Davour a ration pack. The planet was small and green-blue from this distance — *terraformed*, Davour said, *or at least atmospherically processed, the surface readings are within human-tolerable range*. The system around it had two suns, both of them small and yellow, neither of them on Hegemony charts. The planet was the only inhabited body in the system. There were three orbital stations, none of which appeared to be transmitting on civilian frequencies. The Harvester's traffic was all encrypted or silent.

The shuttle's transponder, broadcasting under the codes Orrel had provided, was met by a customs hail at hour twelve. Davour responded with the manifest. The customs station accepted it without query. *Welcome to the facility, Hegemony Internal Security transport,* the customs voice said, in a flat synthesized tone. *Proceed to receiving bay seven on the southern approach. Please ensure all weapons are secured for offload review.*

Davour acknowledged.

The shuttle began its descent.

The descent profile, as Davour had described, was a long one. The shuttle would skim the upper atmosphere for several minutes, drop through the lower thermosphere over the planet's southern continent, and then run a low-altitude approach across roughly six hundred kilometers of terrain before reaching the receiving bay's pad. The whole approach was a designed-in vulnerability of Hegemony shuttle traffic — the sustained low-altitude segment was a customs measure, intended to give surface monitoring time to verify the manifest before the shuttle reached a sensitive facility — and it was the segment Sable was going to use.

Davour brought them in.

The first eight minutes of the descent were unremarkable. The shuttle bucked gently as it crossed the troposphere boundary; the porthole filled with the green-tinged orange of atmospheric entry; the cabin's deck heated through the suit liners and then cooled again as the shuttle's thermal systems caught up. The porthole cleared. The shuttle was below the cloud layer.

Below them, the southern continent was a vast unbroken plain — long grass, low scrub, the pale yellow of a biome Kester did not recognize, lit by the late afternoon angle of the system's smaller sun. The terrain was empty. No facilities. No roads. No relays. Davour, in the cockpit, said: *Coming up on the window. Five minutes.*

Sable came through the cockpit door.

She looked at Ivo. Ivo was sitting in the same seat he had been sitting in for ten hours. His face was very tired. His hands were resting on his knees.

"Stand up," Sable said.

Ivo stood up.

"Hands."

He held them out. Davour, who had come back through the cockpit door behind Sable, produced a length of suit-liner cord and tied his wrists in front of him with the precise economical knots of a woman who had been trained to restrain prisoners and who had not forgotten the training. The cord was cinched tight. Ivo did not flinch.

"Walk to the hatch," Sable said.

Ivo walked.

The hatch was on the rear cabin's port side — a standard atmospheric egress hatch, sealed with a manual override that could be opened in atmosphere without venting the cabin once the pressure differential equalized. Davour cycled the equalization. The hatch's status indicator went from red to amber. The shuttle's altitude was approximately three hundred meters above the grassland — low, but not low enough that a fall would be survivable without a roll. Sable had given Ivo a chance, but the chance was narrow.

The shuttle's airspeed was the larger problem. They were moving at perhaps four hundred kilometers per hour. Hitting the ground at that speed, even with a perfect roll, would be catastrophic.

Davour, at the cockpit door, said: *Reducing airspeed. Coming down to one-fifty. We have ninety seconds before the customs sweep gets curious about the speed deviation. Make it count.*

The shuttle's deceleration registered as a brief lurch. Kester braced himself against the bulkhead. Ivo did not brace. Ivo stood at the hatch with his hands tied and his eyes closed.

Sable was beside him.

"Open it," she said to Davour.

The hatch opened.

The atmosphere of the Harvester planet rushed in — warm air, smelling faintly of grass and ozone, the particular air of a world that had been terraformed and had grown into the terraforming. The cabin filled with the noise of the shuttle's passage. The grassland scrolled past below them, a green-yellow blur four hundred meters down, slowing as Davour bled off speed.

"Ivo," Sable said.

He opened his eyes.

"Jump."

Ivo looked at her. He looked past her, at the open hatch. He looked at the grassland. He looked at the distance.

He hesitated.

It was, Kester thought afterward, perhaps a full second. Maybe a second and a half. Long enough that another person in another mood might have given him another moment, might have understood the hesitation as the natural response of a man who had been given a kindness and was trying to use it. Sable was not in another mood. Sable had been preparing herself for the hesitation since the moment she made the offer. She had decided, in advance, that the hesitation would cost him.

She raised her sidearm and shot him in the right thigh.

The shot was loud in the cabin. Ivo grunted — the same small involuntary sound he might have made on the platform in a different version of this morning — and his leg buckled. He did not fall. He sagged against the hatch frame, his bound hands coming up reflexively, his mouth opening on something that was perhaps the beginning of a word and that did not finish becoming one.

Sable kicked him.

The kick was professional and economical, the side of her boot against the small of his back, and Ivo went through the hatch sideways and down, with his hands tied and his leg bleeding and the wind taking his face away from Kester's view in less than a heartbeat. Kester saw him for one moment as a small twisting shape against the green-yellow blur of the grassland below, and then Ivo was gone, and the cabin was full of the noise of the wind, and Sable was already turning back.

"Close it," she said.

Davour cycled the hatch.

The hatch sealed.

The cabin's noise dropped, abruptly, to the ordinary hum of the shuttle's atmospheric flight. The pressure stabilized. The smell of the grassland faded.

Davour, at the cockpit door, said: *Bringing us back up to nominal speed. Customs sweep is not flagging the deviation. We are clean. Receiving bay in twelve minutes.*

Sable walked back to the cockpit without speaking.

Kester stood by the closed hatch and looked at the small smear of blood on the deck where Ivo had sagged against the frame. The smear was not large. The shot had been a clean one. Most of the blood had gone with the body, into the four-hundred-meter fall, into the grassland.

He thought about the grassland.

He thought about a man with bound hands and a wounded leg falling four hundred meters and hitting tall grass. He thought about whether the grass would be tall enough to slow the impact. He thought about whether four hundred meters and a hundred-fifty kilometers per hour and a wounded leg and bound hands could produce a survivor.

He understood that the answer was almost certainly no.

He understood that *almost certainly* was the gift Sable had given Ivo, and that the gift was small but not nothing, and that the fact that Sable had bothered to give it at all was a piece of who she still was that he would have to remember when the rest of her had become harder to see.

He turned away from the hatch.

He went back to his fold-down seat.

Resh was looking at him.

"Did he jump," Resh said quietly.

"He hesitated," Kester said. "Sable shot him and kicked him. He went out the hatch."

Resh nodded, slowly.

"Was he alive when he went out."

"Yes."

"Will he survive."

"No."

Resh was quiet for a long moment.

Then he said: "Good."

Kester looked at him. Resh was eighteen. He had been silent for three days. He had just said *good* about a man Kester had liked, who had held doors for him, who had taught him how to read a maintenance shaft, who had been a member of the resistance for fifteen years and a Board asset for at least seven.

Kester did not correct him.

He sat in his fold-down seat and he closed his eyes and he understood, with the small administrative clarity that was now more or less the only mode of thought he had access to, that the resistance was no longer a community. It was a small group of people on a shuttle, bound for a meeting with a man who had been preparing them, and they were each of them carrying their own version of what the last week had cost, and the versions did not match. Sable was carrying one thing. Davour was carrying another. Resh was carrying a third. He himself was carrying a fourth, which was the weight of a man whose paperwork had built the room they were now flying toward and whose silence at an open hatch had just helped close another transaction.

The shuttle continued its descent.

The shuttle landed at receiving bay seven at hour fourteen, eleven minutes — a few minutes behind schedule, on a strip of polished landing pad in a hangar that opened to a green sky and that contained, Kester saw through the porthole, the silhouettes of perhaps twenty other shuttles in adjacent bays.

The hangar was warm.

The shuttle's exterior hatch released.

Davour, in the cockpit, said quietly: *We are clean. Customs reads us as a standard arrival. There is a reception waiting at the door.*

Sable came back through the cockpit door. She had cleaned her sidearm during the final approach. The smear of blood by the hatch had been wiped down with a suit liner cloth. The cabin was, to a casual inspection, a normal arriving Hegemony military shuttle with four passengers and no incidents.

She nodded to Kester.

Kester stood up.

The four of them — Sable, Kester, Davour, and Resh — moved to the exterior hatch. Davour cycled it. The hatch opened onto the small extruded landing platform, and beyond the platform, the hangar's polished floor, and beyond the floor, the figure waiting at the hangar's interior door.

The figure was small and wore a neat uniform Kester did not recognize. The figure raised one hand in greeting and walked toward them, and as the figure approached Kester saw that it was a young woman — perhaps twenty-five, perhaps younger — with a face that was calm and professional and that bore no insignia identifying her institution.

She stopped a respectful distance from the foot of the loading ramp, which had already extended.

She bowed, very slightly, in the formal Hegemony manner.

"Mr. Vaile," she said. "Welcome to the Harvester. I am Aide Veneya. The Warden has been expecting you. He apologizes for not meeting you personally; he is currently in a session with a subject and will join you in the residence wing within the hour. If you will follow me, I will escort you to your quarters and arrange refreshments. The journey has been long. We have prepared rooms."

She turned. She began to walk, with the easy professional pace of a person who had escorted many guests through this hangar and who expected, without question, that her guests would follow.

Sable looked at Kester.

Kester looked at Sable.

Behind them, four hundred kilometers and several hours back along the descent path, in a stretch of grassland that did not appear on any monitoring relay's map, the body of a man with bound hands was lying somewhere in the tall yellow grass, and nothing in the universe was attending to it, and nothing was going to.

They walked down the loading ramp.

They followed Aide Veneya through the hangar's interior door.

The door was already opening, and the residence wing was already waiting.

## Chapter Ten: The Dinner

The residence wing was beautiful.

This was the thing Kester noticed first, walking behind Aide Veneya through the long corridor that led from the hangar's interior door to the wing's east entrance. The walls were paneled in a pale wood he did not recognize. The floor was a kind of stone, polished but not slippery, that registered a soft warmth under his boots — *underfloor heating*, his administrator's mind cataloged, *a residential luxury, not a facility specification*. The corridor's lighting was indirect and warm. There were small alcoves at intervals containing arrangements of dried grasses and pale flowers, and the alcoves were lit individually, the way museum displays are lit. The whole effect was that of a private estate, not a research facility.

Kester glanced at Sable as they walked. Sable's face, under the residence wing's warm light, was carefully blank. She was registering the same details Kester was registering, and she was making no expression about any of them.

Davour walked behind Sable with her hands relaxed at her sides. Kester recognized the posture. Davour's hands were relaxed because tense hands would have signaled to anyone watching that Davour was preparing to do something. Davour was, in fact, preparing to do something — she had been preparing to do something since the moment they stepped off the loading ramp — but her body had been trained to keep its preparations invisible, and the training was holding.

Resh was last. Resh was walking with his eyes down, the way he had walked since Mouse was taken, and Kester was the only one of them watching him closely enough to notice that his eyes were not actually down. They were tracking. Resh was looking at the floor in his peripheral vision but he was *seeing* the corridor — the alcoves, the lighting, the staff who passed them in the opposite direction with the small professional nods of residence employees acknowledging guests. Resh had not been silent for three days because he had stopped paying attention. Resh had been silent for three days because he had been paying attention to *everything*, and he had not yet found the right thing to say.

Aide Veneya stopped at a junction.

"This is the east entrance to the residence wing," she said. Her voice was the same calm professional register it had been in the hangar. "The wing is divided into four quarters by service corridors, and you will each be assigned quarters in different sections, for your privacy and comfort. We have, however, accommodated Madam Reyek's request that the young man Resh share her quarters; we agree that this is appropriate, given his age."

Sable had not made the request out loud. Sable had simply, in the corridor, taken Resh's hand once for perhaps two seconds, and the gesture had been observed and translated by the residence staff before any of them had reached the junction. Aide Veneya was telling them, in the courteous form of a granted request, that the residence had been listening for things the guests had not yet asked for and was anticipating them.

"Thank you," Sable said.

"Of course, madam. Mr. Vaile, your quarters are this way, in the south quarter; the Warden has asked that you be placed in the suite nearest his own, as it is the most comfortable available, and he wishes to honor your seniority. Madam Reyek and Resh, your quarters are in the west quarter, with a view of the eastern gardens. Captain Davour, your quarters are in the north quarter; we have given you a suite that is somewhat more secluded, in keeping with what we understand of your military preferences."

Davour's mouth did not move. She did not nod. *Military preferences*, Kester translated, was the residence's polite phrase for *we have put you in a room with three doors we can lock from outside*.

"Each of your suites contains fresh clothing appropriate to the climate of this facility. The Warden has invited you to dinner in the small eastern dining room at twenty-hundred local time. That is approximately one hour from now. Staff will collect you from your quarters at five minutes before the hour and escort you to dinner. We hope you will find your suites comfortable and that the hour is sufficient for you to refresh yourselves after your journey." Aide Veneya inclined her head, very slightly. "Do you have any questions before I show you to your rooms."

Sable said, in a perfectly even voice: "No."

"Then please follow me."

They walked.

---

Kester's suite was on a long quiet hallway in the south quarter, behind a door that opened to his hand without prompting — *biometric reader*, his administrator's mind noted, *they have my prints already, they have probably had them since I was hired on Tartaros eleven years ago*. The suite was a single large room with a small attached bath. There was a bed, made up with what felt to Kester's hand like real linen. There was a desk against one wall with a small lamp on it and a chair drawn up. There was a window that looked east, over a garden that was already darkening toward evening.

On the bed, neatly folded, was a set of clothes.

Kester stood in the doorway and looked at them for a long moment.

The clothes were soft cotton in muted colors — a pale gray pair of loose trousers, a long cream-colored tunic, a thin dark robe to wear over the tunic against the evening's coolness. There was a pair of soft slippers on the floor beside the bed. The clothes were exactly his size. He could tell by looking at them. The trousers had been hemmed for his height. The tunic's collar had been cut for his neck.

He set down the small bag he had been carrying — which contained, he realized with a small cold administrative lurch, only the photograph drives in their sealed pouch, his ration cards, and Pell's analgesic from Tev. Everything else they had brought through the launch base had been quietly removed from their possession at some point during the walk from the hangar. The removal had been so smooth that Kester had not registered it happening. His sidearm was gone. The small tools Davour had given him before the assault on Pier Three were gone. He had not even noticed when they had left him.

He sat on the bed.

He sat for perhaps two minutes without moving.

Then he stood up, because there was an hour and the hour was the only resource he had, and he walked into the small attached bath and turned on the water. The water came on instantly, hot. Of course it came on hot. He stripped off the suit liner he had been wearing for thirty-six hours, including across the surface of Tartaros and through the launch base assault and across the fourteen hours of transit, and he stood under the water for a long time.

He thought about Lira.

He thought about Pira on the shuttle to Andra Prime.

He thought about the four uncounted children in the under-levels with Roon.

He thought, with the small dim clarity of a man who had been outwitted at every stage of an operation he had thought was his, that he was now in exactly the place a Hegemony Internal Security report would describe as *Detained Subject Vaile in custody at the Harvester facility, pending arraignment*. He had walked here under his own power. He had hijacked a military shuttle to do it. The shuttle's manifest, signed by Orrel, was now the legal record by which his presence on this planet was documented. There was no version of the next several days in which Kester did not go on trial somewhere, for something. The only question was whether the trial was Hegemony or Federation, and whether he was alive when it happened.

He turned the water off.

He dressed in the clothes the residence had provided.

The clothes fit perfectly. The fabric was soft against his skin in a way that nothing he had worn in a week had been soft, and his body responded to the softness with a small involuntary relief that he hated himself for. He stood in front of the small mirror over the bath's sink and looked at the man in the cream tunic and the dark robe and the soft trousers, and he thought: *I am dressed exactly the way a senior administrator on a working visit to the Harvester facility would be dressed. The clothes are not a costume. The clothes are a uniform for a job I have been offered and have not yet refused.*

He looked at himself for a long moment.

He thought: *They have decided what I am going to be. I have not yet decided.*

He turned away from the mirror.

He waited.

---

A staff member knocked at five minutes before twenty-hundred.

The staff member was young — perhaps twenty-three, perhaps twenty-four — and wore the same neat uniform as Aide Veneya, in a slightly darker color. The staff member's face was pleasant and unmarked and entirely untroubled. He bowed slightly, the way Aide Veneya had bowed in the hangar, and said: "Mr. Vaile. The Warden's compliments. Dinner is being served. May I escort you."

"Yes."

Kester walked behind him through the corridors of the south quarter, into the central junction, through a wider corridor that passed the kitchen on one side and a small library on the other, and into a room that was the small eastern dining room.

The room was beautiful.

A long table, polished to a soft shine, set for eight with cream-colored linen and silver and small arrangements of the same pale flowers Kester had seen in the corridor's alcoves. Candles — actual candles, not electric simulations — burned in small glass holders along the table's length. The room had three large windows along its east wall, looking out into the now-dark gardens, and the gardens were lit with strings of small soft lights that caught faintly on the leaves of plants Kester did not recognize. The effect of the windows and the candles together was that of a room held between two kinds of light — the warm interior glow and the gentle cool fairy-glow of the lit garden — and a person stepping into the room would have been forgiven for thinking they had walked into a private estate's most beloved dining room on the evening of an important dinner.

Sable was already at the table.

She was standing behind one of the chairs, her hand resting lightly on the chair's back, and she was not yet sitting because she was waiting — Kester registered, with the quiet professional attention he had been trained for over nine years — for the host to arrive. Sable had been raised somewhere that had taught her how dinners worked. The training had not surfaced in nine days. It was surfacing now, because the room required it.

She was wearing a dark green dress in the same soft fabric as Kester's tunic, with a thin shawl over her shoulders. Her hair had been combed. She did not look like Sable. She looked like a woman who had been invited to a dinner.

Resh stood beside her. Resh had been put in a soft tunic and trousers similar to Kester's, but in slightly brighter colors, the way a residence might dress a young guest. The clothes did not fit Resh's silence. The boy looked uncomfortable in them, the way a teenager looks uncomfortable in adult clothes at a wedding. He did not look at Kester when Kester entered. He was looking at the floor.

Davour was at the table's far end. She had been put in something close to a captain's evening uniform but stripped of insignia — a dark coat over a high-collared shirt, both in the residence's muted palette. She looked, Kester thought, like a soldier at a state dinner, which was perhaps exactly what the residence had been aiming for. Her hands were at her sides. Her face was unreadable.

Two other people were at the table.

Aide Veneya stood at one of the chairs on the host's side, her face composed. Beside her was a woman Kester had not seen before — older, perhaps fifty, with silver hair pulled back severely and a face that gave nothing away, wearing a long dark dress in the same soft fabric. She did not introduce herself. She did not need to. *Senior staff*, Kester's mind catalogued. *Possibly residence director. Possibly something else. Wait.*

The chair at the head of the table was empty.

The chair to its right was empty.

Sable was looking at the empty chairs.

She had not yet seen what she expected to see, and Kester realized, watching her face register the missing presence, that Sable had been bracing herself since they walked into the room. Sable had been preparing herself to see Mouse. Sable had been preparing herself for the impact of the first sight, and the room had not yet given her that impact, and the bracing was beginning to cost her.

The door at the room's far end opened.

Bastien Orrel walked in.

He was, Kester saw immediately, dressed exactly the way Kester had pictured him dressing for a dinner like this — in a dark formal version of the same soft-fabric aesthetic, with a high-collared shirt under a long fitted jacket, in a deeper shade of the room's muted palette. He looked relaxed. He looked, Kester thought with a small cold lurch, *at home*. The Harvester was not a workplace for Orrel. The residence wing was where Orrel *lived*, on his rotations, and the home he was inhabiting was a more comfortable home than any home Kester had ever entered.

"My friends," Orrel said. He spread his hands slightly, the gesture of a host welcoming guests. "I am sorry to have kept you. The session ran long. Please, sit. We have so much to discuss."

He moved toward the head of the table.

Behind him, walking at his left shoulder, came Mouse.

---

She was wearing a dress.

This was the first thing Kester registered. Mouse had spent her nineteen years in prison fatigues — practical clothes, work clothes, the stained and patched garments of a population that did not have access to anything else. The dress she was wearing now was nothing like that. It was in the same soft fabric as Sable's, but in a pale color that was not quite white and not quite gray. It had been cut for her. It fit her with the considered grace of clothing that had been designed for the wearer rather than purchased in a generic size. Her hair had been brushed back from her face and held with a small clip. Her face was clean. She walked with her hands clasped lightly in front of her, the way a young woman walks at a formal dinner.

She did not look like Mouse.

She looked like someone Mouse might have grown into in a life that had not included Tartaros.

Kester saw Sable's hand tighten on the back of her chair.

Mouse went to the chair on Orrel's right. She did not look at Sable as she passed. She did not look at Kester. She looked, Kester thought, at the chair, and at the place setting in front of it, and she stood behind it the way Sable had stood behind hers — waiting for the host.

Orrel reached the head of the table.

He paused. He looked around at his guests. His face was warm.

"Please," he said. "Sit."

They sat.

Mouse sat with the fluid graceful motion of a young woman who had been taught, somewhere, how to sit in a chair at a dinner table. Aide Veneya and the silver-haired woman sat. Sable sat. Resh sat. Davour sat. Kester sat last, at the seat Aide Veneya gestured him toward, which was — he registered — the chair to Orrel's *left*. Mouse on Orrel's right. Kester on Orrel's left. The seating was deliberate. Orrel had placed his two intimates flanking him. He had placed Sable across the table at the far end, in the position of an honored guest who was nonetheless not part of the inner circle.

Sable was perhaps three meters from Mouse.

Mouse, sitting across the table from Sable, did not raise her eyes.

A staff member appeared with a covered dish. Then another. Then another. The dishes were placed at intervals along the table and uncovered with small simultaneous gestures, and the food revealed beneath them was — Kester registered with another small cold lurch — *good*. Not luxurious. Not theatrical. *Good*. A roasted bird of some kind, a kind of grain Kester did not recognize but that smelled of butter and herbs, vegetables that had been dressed in something acidic and bright. A bread that steamed when broken. Wine, dark red, was poured into glasses by a staff member who did it without making the wine the subject of the moment.

Orrel raised his glass.

"To safe arrivals," he said. "To old friends. To honest conversation."

He drank.

Mouse drank from her own glass.

Kester saw her drink, and he saw the small precise movement of her wrist as she set the glass down again, and he understood, watching her, that she was *being* the thing the residence had been training her to be. She was not pretending. She had been programmed into the role over nineteen years and brought to its current calibration over the last several days, and the role was now sufficiently embedded that she could perform it without visible effort. She raised her glass. She drank. She set it down. The motions were fluid because the motions had been *practiced*, and the practicing had been done at a level her conscious mind no longer had to engage with.

Sable did not raise her glass.

Neither did Kester.

Davour, after a moment's hesitation, did. Davour drank a small swallow and set her glass down with the same neutral economy she did everything. Resh did not touch his glass.

Orrel did not seem to notice.

He looked at Mouse.

"Sweetheart," he said gently. "Why don't you greet our guests. I think they have been wanting to see you."

Mouse raised her eyes.

She looked at Sable first. Her face did not change. She inclined her head, very slightly — the same small bow Aide Veneya had given them in the hangar — and she said, in a voice that was clear and even and slightly higher than the voice Sable had known: *Hello, Sable. Welcome. I hope your journey was not too tiring.*

Sable did not respond.

Mouse turned her eyes to Kester. The same small inclination of the head. *Hello, Mr. Vaile. The Warden has spoken of you often. I am glad you have come.*

Kester felt, somewhere very far down in his chest, the small cold lurch that had been arriving on schedule for him for several days now. *The Warden has spoken of you often.* The girl across the table from him had been listening to Bastien Orrel speak of him *often*. The conversations had been happening for months. Years. Mouse had been sitting at this table — or one like it — and listening to Orrel speak about Kester Vaile, the logistics officer, the asset, the eventual recipient of an offer. The conversations had been preparing her for this moment.

He inclined his head in return. He did not trust his voice.

Mouse turned to Resh.

Her eyes lingered on Resh for perhaps half a second longer than they had lingered on Sable or Kester.

Something happened in her face.

It was very small. It was so small that Kester, watching closely, was not sure he had seen it. A muscle around her left eye tightened, very slightly. Her lips parted, briefly, on a word that did not come. Her right hand, which had been resting on the table beside her plate, moved perhaps a centimeter and stopped.

Then her face restored itself.

"Hello," she said, in the same clear even voice, to Resh. "I have not had the pleasure. I am Mouse. The Warden has not had occasion to mention you to me."

Resh did not speak.

Mouse waited the appropriate two seconds for a response, and when Resh did not provide one, she inclined her head a third time and turned her attention to her plate.

Sable was looking at her.

Sable's face was the carefully blank face it had been in the corridor, but Kester, who had been watching Sable for nine days and who knew her face by now in ways he had not earned the right to know it, saw what was happening in it. Sable had seen the half-second. Sable had seen the muscle tightening around the left eye. Sable had seen Mouse's hand twitch toward Resh and stop. Sable had registered that some compartment of Mouse, somewhere behind the dress and the clear voice and the practiced grace, had recognized the boy who had been her companion in the under-levels and who had carried her photograph in his jacket and who had not spoken since she was taken.

The compartment was very small.

The compartment was getting smaller every day.

But the compartment was, for the duration of half a second, *still there*.

Sable lifted her glass. She drank a small swallow of the dark red wine. She set the glass down. Her hand did not shake.

She was, Kester understood, recalibrating. The dinner was no longer a performance to be endured. The dinner was an *operation*. There was a Mouse in the room with them, somewhere behind the trained surface, and the operation was now to keep that Mouse alive for as long as possible, and to give her something to come back to when she was ready, and to do it without alerting Orrel that she was doing it.

The dinner began in earnest.

---

It went on for more than an hour.

Orrel made conversation. He was, Kester remembered with the small surprised admiration that had visited him intermittently over nine years of working for the man, a *good* dinner host. He told stories about the planet's terraforming, which had been done sixty years ago by an early Hegemony venture that had run out of funding and abandoned the work, and which had been quietly restarted by the Board fifteen years ago. He told stories about the gardens outside the windows, which had been planted by a former resident of this wing whose name he did not give, and which had matured in ways that surprised him each season. He told a story about a small bird that had nested in one of the garden's trees the previous year, and about Mouse coming to find him in his office to tell him about it, and Mouse — across the table — smiled at the right moment in the story, the small calibrated smile of a daughter who had been told the story by her father many times.

Sable ate.

Kester noticed this without commenting on it. Sable ate the food because eating the food was the thing her body needed, and Sable had stopped allowing her dignity to interfere with her body's needs. Davour ate. Resh ate small amounts in small bites. Kester ate. The food was as good as it had looked.

Orrel asked Davour, at one point, about her time in the Hegemony Military Police. He did this with the genuine interest of a man who liked soldiers and who had been one once, and Davour responded in short measured sentences that gave him nothing useful but that did not insult him by refusing the conversation entirely. Orrel asked Resh, gently, what his interests had been before his arrival in the under-levels. Resh did not answer. Orrel did not press.

He did not, Kester noticed, address Sable directly until they were perhaps forty minutes into the meal.

Then he said: *Madam Reyek. I want to thank you, formally, for the broadcast.*

Sable looked up.

Orrel's face was warm. His glass was in his hand. He was looking at Sable across the table the way a host looks at a guest he has been wanting to acknowledge for the duration of the dinner.

"I mean that sincerely," Orrel said. "The broadcast forced certain conversations within the Hegemony that had been deferred for too long. The reform commission is a real commission. It will produce real recommendations. Some of those recommendations will be implemented. The conditions in Wing Seven, and in similar wings at facilities I will not name, have been a moral burden on responsible administrators for many years, and the burden has become heavier than the institutions could carry. The broadcast lifted the burden. Forced its lifting. I am grateful. I want you to know that."

Sable was looking at him.

She did not respond.

"I understand," Orrel continued, "that you may not believe me. I understand that the conditions of your situation make it difficult to hear sincerity in the words of a man you regard as your enemy. I respect that. I am only telling you what is true. Your work mattered. It will continue to matter. The names you read on the broadcast will be on a list of acknowledged dead by the end of next year. Their families will receive compensation. The Federation will have observers at every site. The world your daughter — " he caught himself, smoothly " — *Mouse* will inherit will be a kinder world than the one your brother died in. That is partly your work."

Sable's hand was on the stem of her wineglass.

Kester saw her knuckles whiten.

Sable said, very quietly: "My brother was used as research material to develop the augmentation procedures you are using on her."

Orrel's face did not change.

"Yes," he said. "He was. I am sorry, Madam Reyek. Tomas's death was — it was not a clean death and it was not a meaningful death in the way we would all wish a death to be meaningful. He died early in the program, before we had refined our methods. The information his death produced was instrumental in saving the lives of many subsequent subjects, but I understand that this is a small comfort and probably no comfort at all. I am sorry. I want you to hear me say it. I am sorry your brother died as he did. I take responsibility for the conditions under which it happened."

Sable's hand on the wineglass did not relax.

"Do you," she said.

"Yes."

"Then return Mouse to me. Acknowledge that Mouse is not your daughter. Acknowledge that the implant in her neck is a violation. Allow her to come home."

Orrel was quiet for a long moment.

Mouse, on his right, did not look up.

"Madam Reyek," Orrel said, gently. "I cannot do that. Mouse's treatment is at a delicate phase. Reversing the implant's adjustments now would be — it would be medically dangerous. It would also be, frankly, an act of cruelty toward Mouse herself, who has spent the last several months adjusting to her current state and who would experience reversal as a kind of grief. She is, for the first time in her life, *settled*. I will not undo that for the sake of an emotional restoration that would not, in any case, return her to who she was. There is no version of Mouse that you are remembering. The Mouse who was your companion in the under-levels was a transitional figure, a phase of her development, no more her real self than her infant self was. The Mouse you see across this table is the real Mouse. The continued treatment is what allows her to be that. Removing it would not give you back what you lost. It would only break what she has become."

Sable did not speak.

Orrel's voice softened.

"I understand, Madam Reyek, that this will not be a satisfying answer. I do not offer it as a satisfying answer. I offer it as an honest one. I have been honest with you throughout this dinner, and I will continue to be honest with you. We will speak more, in the coming days, about what is possible and what is not. For now, please — eat. The chef has prepared dessert. I think you will enjoy it."

A staff member appeared with a covered dish.

The dish was uncovered, revealing a kind of small fruit tart that Kester did not recognize but that smelled of caramel and something warm and spiced. Plates were distributed. The ritual of the dessert continued.

Sable ate the dessert.

Mouse did not.

This was the first thing Mouse had not done at the table, and Kester noticed it. Mouse had eaten her main course in small, calibrated bites. She had drunk her wine in measured sips. She had performed, throughout the dinner, every small action that her training required of her. But when the dessert arrived in front of her, she set her fork beside her plate and folded her hands in her lap and did not eat.

Orrel did not press her.

Kester watched Mouse's face. There was nothing visible in it. Whatever was happening in her, it was happening below the surface. The dessert was sweet, perhaps. Mouse, in the under-levels, had not had access to sweet things for most of her life. The sweetness might be triggering something the implant was not configured to manage. Or perhaps not. Perhaps Mouse simply did not like fruit tart. There was no way to tell from across the table.

Orrel finished his dessert.

He set his fork down. He pressed the small linen napkin to his mouth, briefly. He looked around the table.

"Well," he said. "I think we have done very well for an evening of strangers becoming friends. I am grateful to all of you for coming. We have a great deal to discuss in the coming days, but those discussions will benefit from a night's sleep and perhaps a morning's reflection. Mouse — sweetheart — it is past your hour. Why don't you go up. I will see you in the morning. Aide Veneya will accompany you."

Mouse stood.

She inclined her head — to Orrel, to the table generally — and she said, in her clear even voice: *Goodnight, father. Goodnight, Mr. Vaile. Madam Reyek. Resh. Captain Davour.*

She used each of their names.

Sable's hand was still on the stem of her wineglass.

Mouse turned to leave.

And it was at this moment — as Mouse walked, with the practiced grace, behind Orrel's chair, toward the door at the room's far end — that Sable made the sound she made.

It was not loud. It was the small sound of a woman whose breath had been held for the duration of a meal and who had finally let it out, and the sound carried, in the quiet of the dining room, as clearly as a struck bell.

Mouse stopped walking.

She did not turn. She stood with her back to Sable, her hands clasped in front of her, perhaps two meters from Sable's chair.

She said, very quietly, without looking back: *Goodnight.*

The word was not the same word she had said a moment earlier.

The earlier *goodnight* had been a generic farewell to a table of guests.

This one was a goodbye, said specifically, by Mouse, to Sable, in a voice that was — for the duration of the single word — the voice Sable had known.

Then Mouse continued walking. She passed through the door. Aide Veneya followed. The door closed.

Sable rose from her chair.

Davour rose half a second after her. Resh rose with the lurching sluggishness of a teenager who had been holding himself still for too long and whose body did not entirely respond when he asked it to move. Kester rose last, because Kester had seen what Sable had seen and was still trying to understand what to do with it.

Orrel had not moved.

He was looking at Sable across the table with an expression that was not quite amused and not quite weary, the kind of expression a host wears when guests have been about to do an ill-advised thing and the host has been hoping, against the evidence, that they would not do it.

"Madam Reyek," Orrel said, mildly.

Sable was already moving.

She came around the table with the speed of a woman who had been preparing herself for the motion for the entire dinner. Her hand was reaching for the heavy candle holder at the table's center as she came. She closed her fingers around it. She lifted it. She brought it down.

She did not reach Orrel.

Three things happened in the space of perhaps one and a half seconds.

The silver-haired woman — who had not spoken once during the entire dinner, who had eaten her food in small precise bites, who Kester had cataloged as *senior staff possibly residence director possibly something else* — was suddenly out of her chair and between Sable and Orrel. The motion was so fast that Kester did not actually see her stand up. He registered that she had been seated. He registered that she was now not seated. He did not register the transition.

The candle holder hit the silver-haired woman's forearm.

The forearm did not break.

The silver-haired woman closed her hand around Sable's wrist with the calm professional efficiency of a nurse taking a pulse, and Sable — who had been a strong woman a moment before — was abruptly being held in place by a grip that her body's full weight could not break.

Davour had moved at the same time. Davour had gone for one of the staff members standing along the wall — Kester registered, in the chaos of the moment, that Davour had identified that staff member specifically, that Davour had picked her target out of the four staff members in the room based on some criterion Kester did not have time to parse — and Davour had her hands on the staff member's throat for perhaps one and a quarter seconds before the staff member, with an unhurried economical motion, removed Davour's hands and put Davour against the wall, face first, with her arms held behind her back.

Resh had moved toward Aide Veneya.

This was the thing Kester noticed afterward, the thing he sat with for a long time. Aide Veneya had gone with Mouse through the door. Aide Veneya was not in the room. But Resh, in his half-second of decision, had moved toward where Aide Veneya had been standing during the dinner, the corner of the room nearest the door — and now that Aide Veneya was not there, Resh found himself running toward an empty corner of a dining room, with no clear next action available to him, and the staff member nearest that corner intercepted him gently and turned him, with the practiced motion of a parent redirecting a confused child, and put him in a chair against the wall and held him there.

Kester had not moved.

He stood beside his chair with his hands at his sides and watched the three of them — Sable held by the silver-haired woman, Davour against the wall, Resh in the chair — and he registered that no one had touched him because no one needed to, because Kester had not moved.

The whole thing had taken perhaps ninety seconds. Possibly less.

The silver-haired woman released Sable's wrist.

Sable did not lunge again. The candle holder had fallen to the floor at some point during the exchange. Sable stood with her hand half-raised and her face — which had been carefully blank for the entire dinner — now wearing the expression of a woman who had just been shown a thing she had not believed was possible. The silver-haired woman had moved in a way that should not have been possible for a fifty-year-old residence administrator, and Sable was now understanding, all at once, what the residence's staff actually was.

Kester, watching her face, saw the understanding arrive.

He saw her look around the room.

He saw her look at the staff member holding Davour, at the staff member holding Resh, at the silver-haired woman who had returned to a calm standing posture three feet from Orrel's chair.

He saw her understand that the room had never been a room of guests. The room had been a room of *finished subjects*, with three guests inside it, and the guests had been observed throughout the meal by people who could move faster than the guests could move and who had been waiting, with professional patience, for the moment the guests would test the boundary so that the boundary could be demonstrated.

Sable's hand fell to her side.

The candle holder lay on the polished floor in a small puddle of wax.

Orrel had still not moved.

He had not even, Kester registered now, set down his glass.

Orrel raised the glass. He took a small swallow. He set it down. He looked at Sable across the table with the same expression he had worn when she had begun to move — not quite amused, not quite weary, the look of a man whose hospitality had been tested in a small predictable way and whose hospitality had answered.

"Well," Orrel said softly.

He looked at the three of them.

"Take them to their rooms, please," he said, to the staff. "Madam Reyek and the young man together, as arranged. Captain Davour to her own quarters. Ensure they are comfortable for the night. Mr. Vaile will stay. I would like to have a word with him."

The staff moved.

Sable was led from the room.

She did not look at Kester as she passed.

Davour was led from the room. She did look at Kester as she passed. Her look was — Kester registered, in the small administrative compartment that had been doing his thinking for him for hours — *appraising*. Davour was assessing him. Davour was deciding, in the second she had to do it, whether Kester was now, in her professional estimation, the resistance's last asset or the resistance's first betrayal. He did not know which conclusion she had reached. She did not give him time to read it.

Resh was led from the room. Resh looked at no one.

The door closed.

The dining room was quiet.

Kester stood beside his chair. The silver-haired woman stood three feet from Orrel, at perfect attention, her hands clasped in front of her. The two remaining staff members had taken positions against the walls. The candles on the table burned. The garden lights outside the windows were unchanged. The wine in Kester's glass had not moved.

Orrel raised his glass again. He took another small swallow.

He looked at Kester.

"Sit down, Kester," he said, gently. "We have so much to talk about."

## Chapter Eleven: The Half-Bottle

Orrel did not sit down.

He had not sat down since the others were led from the dining room. He had stood at the head of the long table with his glass in his hand, and he had drunk from it, slowly, while the staff cleared the dishes around him. The silver-haired woman remained at her position three feet from his chair. The two staff members at the walls had not moved. The candles continued to burn.

When the dishes were gone and the table held only the unfinished glasses of the guests who had been removed, Orrel set his own glass down on the polished wood, very precisely, and he turned to look at Kester.

His face was no longer warm.

It was not, Kester registered, the face Orrel had worn on the screen in the palace — the cold professional face of the station chief reporting to his board. It was something else, a third face, one Kester had not seen before. The face of a host whose hospitality had been refused, whose careful arrangements had been disrupted, whose dinner had been spat on by a guest he had given a chair at his table.

"Do you know," Orrel said, conversationally, "what is required to set a table for eight in this residence."

Kester did not respond.

"It is required that the cook be informed seven hours in advance, so the meal can be prepared from fresh ingredients rather than reheated from stocks. It is required that the linens be inspected for stains and replaced if any are found, and that the silver be polished, and that the candles be checked for wicks of sufficient length to last the duration of the meal. It is required that the room be cleaned. It is required that the wine be brought up from the cellar at a temperature appropriate to its vintage. It is required that staff be assigned, briefed, and rehearsed on the seating, the menu, and the protocol. The whole arrangement takes a day. I had a day's notice of your arrival, Kester. I spent the day on it. I gave it the attention I would give to the visit of a dignitary, because I considered it the visit of dignitaries. I considered Madam Reyek a dignitary. I considered her the leader of a community whose work had earned my respect, however much I have had to oppose it. I prepared the table accordingly."

He paused.

"And she swung a candle holder at my head," he said. "At her host's head. At the head of the man who had, that very afternoon, ordered the kitchen to prepare a fruit tart with the spice she had eaten as a child, because I had read in her file that she had eaten that spice as a child and I thought it would be a small kindness. She swung a candle holder. As though I had not been a host. As though the dinner had not been a dinner."

He shook his head, slightly. He almost smiled.

"A pest," he said softly, "will always be a pest. I should have remembered that. I had forgotten."

He sat down.

Not at the head of the table. He sat in the chair Sable had been sitting in — directly opposite Kester, at the far end — and he set his glass down in front of him and looked at Kester across the polished wood.

"Sit," he said.

Kester was already sitting. He did not move.

"Stay seated, then," Orrel said. He nodded to one of the staff. "Bring the brandy."

A staff member moved to a small sideboard Kester had not noticed. The sideboard contained a decanter and two small crystal glasses. The staff member brought all three to the table, placed them between Orrel and Kester, and withdrew.

Orrel poured.

He poured two glasses of the dark red brandy he had been carrying with him for as long as Kester had known him — the Martian vintage Kester had filed customs paperwork for in 2367 and 2368 and 2370, the bottle that had appeared on Orrel's desk every time a serious conversation was about to happen. The smell of it crossed the table to Kester immediately. It smelled of spice and old wood and the faint sweet rot of fruit that has been pressed and aged and pressed again. It smelled, Kester registered with a small involuntary surge of memory, of nine years of his life.

Orrel pushed one of the glasses toward him.

"No," Kester said.

Orrel paused.

"No?"

"I won't drink with you, Bastien."

It was, Kester realized as he said it, the first time he had used Orrel's first name in this conversation. He had not used it in years. He had used *Warden* or *Mr. Orrel* or *sir*, depending on the context, for most of nine years, and the first name had been reserved for moments of particular gravity. It came out of his mouth now without his having decided to use it, and Orrel registered the change. His face did not move. But he registered it.

"All right, Kester," Orrel said quietly. "You don't have to drink. But I am going to leave the glass here. In case you change your mind. The conversation is going to be long, and you may yet decide that a drink is appropriate before it ends. I will not press you."

He took a small swallow from his own glass.

He set it down.

He looked at Kester.

"Now," he said. "We talk."

---

He talked first about Calderon.

He did this, Kester registered, with the manner of a man telling a story he had told before — to himself, perhaps, or to a journal, or to the silver-haired woman who stood three feet behind him and who Kester suspected had heard the story enough times to know it by rote. Orrel did not embellish. He did not dramatize. He told the story the way an old soldier tells the story of a campaign that has shaped his life, which is to say, with a kind of weariness that did not pretend to be philosophical and did not pretend to be untraumatized.

Calderon Trench had been a forward observation objective on the third front of a war Kester knew by name and had not paid attention to. The war had been twenty-three years ago. Orrel had been a junior officer — twenty-six, lieutenant grade, in command of a small infantry unit attached to a larger formation. The objective had been a section of trench works approximately six kilometers in length on a ridge that the Hegemony command had identified as militarily significant. The trench had been held by Federation-allied forces. The Hegemony had been ordered to take it.

Sixteen thousand soldiers had been committed to the assault.

The assault had succeeded. The trench had been taken. It had been held for eleven days, after which a strategic reassessment by Hegemony command had concluded that the trench was no longer worth the resources required to hold it, and the trench had been abandoned. The Hegemony had withdrawn under fire. The Federation had reoccupied the position within a week. The eleven-day occupation had cost the Hegemony perhaps eleven thousand dead. The withdrawal had cost another four thousand. Of the sixteen thousand soldiers committed to the assault, fewer than nine hundred had survived the campaign intact. Of those nine hundred, perhaps two hundred had remained operationally deployable after the medical assessments were completed.

Bastien Orrel had been one of the two hundred.

He had been a junior officer of an infantry unit of forty men. Six of his men had survived the assault. Three had survived the occupation. One had survived the withdrawal. The man who had survived the withdrawal had taken his own life on a transport ship six days after the campaign ended, in a bathroom on the troop deck, with a piece of glass from a broken mirror.

Orrel had not taken his own life.

He had, instead, sat in his own bathroom on the same transport ship, with his own piece of mirror in his own hand, for approximately three hours. He had sat with the glass against his wrist for some portion of that time. He had decided, eventually, that he was not going to do it. The deciding had taken him six hours of additional sitting. By the time he stood up from the bathroom floor and walked out into the troop deck, the sun of whatever world they were transiting past had set and risen and set again, and Orrel had become a man with a single durable thesis about the operations of large institutions.

The thesis was simple.

Bodies were being spent.

The bodies were being spent because the institutions did not value them at their replacement cost, because the institutions had access to a supply that appeared to be infinite, and because the persons making the spending decisions did not ever themselves become the bodies that were spent. The asymmetry was the engine of the waste. It was also, in Orrel's estimate, the engine of every large-scale moral failure the Hegemony had committed in his lifetime. The persons who made the decisions about how many bodies to commit to a forward observation objective were not the persons who became the bodies. The bodies were drawn from a population that had no representation in the room where the decisions were made, and the population would continue to be drawn from for as long as the room continued to make decisions of that kind.

The thesis suggested two responses.

The first response was political. Adjust the composition of the room. Bring the population into the room. Give the persons who would become the bodies a voice in the spending decisions. This was the response Orrel had considered for some weeks after Calderon and had eventually rejected, because the political response required a generation to implement and would, even if implemented, be subject to gradual recapture by the same institutional pressures that had produced the original asymmetry. The political response was not, in Orrel's analysis, a response that addressed the underlying problem. It was a response that addressed the rhetoric of the problem.

The second response was operational.

If the bodies were being spent because the institutions did not value them at replacement cost, then the bodies needed to be made *more expensive to replace*. This could not be achieved by increasing wages or improving conditions or any of the conventional mechanisms of labor economics, because those mechanisms operated on the rhetoric of the problem rather than the underlying physics. The bodies needed to be made operationally more *valuable*. The bodies needed to embody, in their literal flesh, capabilities that the institutions could not easily acquire elsewhere. Augmented bodies. Conditioned bodies. Bodies that took twenty years to produce and that could not be replaced by a recruitment poster. Bodies that the institutions, when they considered spending them, would have to spend with the same care they spent ammunition or hardware or any other strategic resource that came with a real procurement cost.

This was the response Bastien Orrel had implemented.

Or rather — Orrel said, with a small wave of the glass — this was the response that the Architect, who had recruited Orrel some years after Calderon and whom Orrel was not at liberty to name, had implemented. Orrel had been a participant in the implementation. He had been a junior partner for several years, then a senior partner, then the operational lead for one of the program's seven research facilities. He had built the prototype on Tartaros. He had refined the procedures. He had survived the early failures, including the failures that had killed Sable Reyek's brother and many others, and he had presided over the successes, including the success that had emerged from the Successor — the child who had unlocked the substrate question — and the cohort that had grown from her example. He had been at the program for twenty-three years. He believed in it as completely as he believed in his own survival of Calderon, which was to say, he believed in it because it was the only ethical response to that survival that he had been able to construct.

"I am not asking you to admire this thesis, Kester," Orrel said.

He had been speaking for perhaps twenty minutes. His glass was nearly empty. He refilled it from the decanter, slowly, without taking his eyes off Kester's face.

"I am only asking you to understand it. I have observed, in the people I have explained this thesis to over the years, that they tend to mistake my conviction for fanaticism. It is not fanaticism. It is bookkeeping. The Hegemony has wars coming. The wars will be fought by bodies. I am building bodies that will not be wasted in those wars in the way my unit was wasted at Calderon. The bodies I am building will be expensive. Their loss will be felt. The persons making the decisions about their commitment will have to weigh the cost. They will commit fewer. They will commit them more carefully. Fewer mothers will receive the news that their son died for a forward observation objective that was abandoned eleven days after his death. That is the actual outcome of my work. That is what the Cleanse, in its bureaucratic ugliness, is *for*. It is for the mothers. The fact that it requires children to produce is a real cost. I do not minimize it. I will not minimize it to you. I am paying that cost on behalf of the mothers who will not have to lose their grown sons. The arithmetic is not mine to set. The arithmetic was set by Calderon Trench. I am only completing it."

He drank.

Kester sat across the table.

He did not respond. He could not respond. The thesis Orrel had described was monstrous and the thesis was also, Kester registered with a kind of horrified administrative clarity, *internally consistent*. Orrel was not lying. Orrel was not even rationalizing in the conventional sense. Orrel had constructed, over twenty-three years, a moral framework that took the worst experience of his life and converted it into a justification for the worst project of the Hegemony's history, and the framework did not have visible holes from inside the framework. From outside, the holes were everywhere. From inside, the framework was the only sane response a survivor of Calderon could have produced.

That was the thing that made Orrel terrifying.

Not the cruelty.

The *coherence*.

---

Orrel set his glass down.

"Now," he said. "The offer."

Kester braced himself, somewhere very far down in his chest, and he listened.

The offer was, as Orrel laid it out, almost exactly what Kester had expected. Senior administrator at the Harvester. Reporting to Orrel and to the Board. Compensation at a level that would make Kester wealthier than he had been at any point in his career. Accommodations comparable to the suite he had been placed in. Access to Mouse on a regular basis — *daily, if you wish, Kester, or as little as you wish, depending on her schedule and your own*. The work itself would be a continuation of what Kester had been doing on Tartaros, with the difference that he would now be aware of what the work was, and would be operating with full institutional awareness rather than as a compartmentalized administrator. The position included a five-year initial term, after which Kester could renegotiate or retire, with a pension that would allow him to live comfortably for the rest of his life.

The position also included one specific benefit that Orrel mentioned last, with the deliberation of a man who had been planning to mention it last from the beginning of the conversation.

"Your daughter," Orrel said.

Kester's hands, which had been resting on the table in front of him, did not move. The not-moving was a deliberate choice. He had been preparing himself, since the moment Orrel had begun the conversation, for the fact that Lira would be mentioned. He had decided, in the bath an hour ago, that he would not show what he felt when the name was spoken. He showed nothing now. His hands rested. His face was still.

"Your daughter is twenty-two years old," Orrel said. "She lives in the third terrace of Andra Prime, in a small apartment that she shares with two other women her age, both of them students at the Cresswell University philosophy department. She studies philosophy herself — comparative ethics, with a particular interest in the moral standing of artificial persons, which I find quietly amusing given the present conversation. She has a part-time job at a coffee house in the eastern quarter. She walks to work most mornings. She takes the underground transit when it rains. She has not seen you in eleven years. She has not received a letter from you in seven years, since you stopped writing because she stopped responding. I have a file on her. The file is not invasive — I have not bugged her apartment, I have not monitored her communications. The file is the kind of file the Hegemony keeps on the family of every senior administrator at every site, as a matter of routine procedure. Your daughter has been monitored at the same level as the daughters of every other administrator the Board has employed for the last twenty years. This is not personal. It is housekeeping."

Orrel paused.

"I am telling you this," he said, "because the Hegemony's reform commission will release its preliminary findings in approximately fifteen days. Your name will be in the findings. The findings will describe you as the senior logistics officer responsible for the abuses at Wing Seven. They will note that you have not been located and that a warrant has been issued for your arrest. The findings will be in the press. Your daughter will read them. She has been reading the news on her tablet at breakfast for several years now — the file notes the habit. She will read about her father at her breakfast, in a coffee house if she is at work that morning, and she will read about him as the press describes him, which will be as a man who supervised the medical murder of forty-two prisoners over a period of nine years."

Orrel's face was very gentle.

"The press will be mostly correct," he said. "The press will not be entirely correct. The complications and the contexts will not be in the press, because the press is not interested in complications and contexts. Your daughter will read the simplified version. She will sit in her coffee house and read the simplified version of her father, and she will understand, on her own, what she thinks of it, and the understanding will be the understanding she carries for the rest of her life unless something else is allowed to reach her first."

He looked at Kester.

"I am offering you the chance to write to her," he said. "Before the findings are released. You may write whatever you wish. I will not censor the letter. I will only ensure it is delivered. Your daughter will receive it on a morning before the findings are public, and she will read your words about yourself in your own handwriting, and she will know, before any other narrative reaches her, what you wish her to know. The letter is yours. The contents are yours. The reaching is what I am offering. That is all."

He paused.

"It is also, I should be honest with you, contingent on your acceptance of the position. I cannot send a letter from a man in custody. I can only send a letter from a man who is, by then, my colleague. The two are not separable. You will write to Lira from the Harvester. She will read your letter as the letter of a man who has, at the conclusion of a very difficult journey, joined the institution she will read about in the news. This is what I am offering. I will not pretend it is something else."

Orrel set his glass down.

"I will leave you with that," he said. "Take the night. We will speak again at breakfast. Mouse will be available to you tomorrow, regardless of your decision — I will not use her access as leverage. You may speak with her freely. Aide Veneya will arrange a private room. I do not want this offer to be entangled with your relationship to her. They are different questions. The position is the position. Mouse is Mouse. You may have access to her tomorrow whether you accept or decline."

He stood.

He pushed his chair back from the table, with the small economical motion of a man who had said what he came to say, and he turned toward the door.

He paused.

He turned back.

"Oh," he said. "Kester. I almost forgot. Between us — administrator to administrator — the civilian transit shuttle that left Pier Three on the morning you departed was a Class II frame. We had to write it off. The procurement office has been quite tedious about it for the last few days, asking me to justify the loss in operational terms when we both know that the operational terms are not the kind of thing one puts in a procurement report. I told them I would speak to you about it, on the chance that your perspective on the disposition would help frame the entry. We can discuss it tomorrow if you have any thoughts. Probably not important. I'll spare you the details."

Orrel inclined his head, very slightly.

"Goodnight, Kester."

He turned again.

He walked to the door.

The door opened for him. He passed through. The door closed.

The dining room was very quiet.

---

Kester sat at the table.

He sat for a long time.

The candles continued to burn. The two staff members along the walls had not moved. The silver-haired woman had withdrawn at some point during Orrel's monologue — Kester had not registered the withdrawal — and the room now contained Kester, the two staff, the candles, the half-empty decanter, and the two glasses, one of which was untouched and one of which was empty.

His hands were on the table.

He looked at his hands.

His hands were shaking.

This was, Kester registered with a small administrative observation that arrived from very far away, the first time his hands had shaken since the broadcast room. He had been steady through the chamber attack, through the expedition to the palace, through the descent, through the disposal of Ivo, through the dinner. His hands had not shaken. They were shaking now. The shaking had begun perhaps thirty seconds after Orrel's procurement aside, and they had not stopped.

He thought: *A Class II frame.*

He thought: *Write it off.*

He thought: *We had to.*

He thought: *We.*

He thought: *Yenna. Chayne. Pira.*

He thought: *Pira asked Sable will you remember me. Sable said yes. Sable said I will remember every day. Sable said I will remember your name and your face and the way you said goodbye to Tev. Sable kept the promise. Sable is keeping the promise tonight in her quarters in the west quarter of the residence wing, with Resh on the other bed, both of them not speaking. Sable is remembering Pira tonight. Sable believes that Pira is on Andra Prime. Sable does not know that Pira is no longer anywhere.*

He thought: *I am the only person in this universe who knows that Pira is no longer anywhere.*

He thought: *I do not know how Pira died. I do not know if Yenna or Chayne are alive. I do not know if the archive reached the surface of Andra Prime. I know only that the shuttle was a Class II frame and the procurement office is being tedious and the writing-off is a closed item.*

He reached for the untouched glass.

He drank from it.

The brandy was warm in his throat in the specific way Orrel's brandy had been warm in his throat across nine years of Friday afternoon meetings. The taste was familiar. The taste had been the texture of his career. He drank the rest of the glass in one long swallow. He set it down.

He picked up the decanter.

He poured. His hands were shaking. He spilled a small amount on the polished wood. He did not wipe it up. He drank the second glass. He poured a third.

He drank the third more slowly.

He sat with the third glass in his hand and he thought about Pira, and he thought about Yenna, and he thought about Chayne, and he thought about his daughter on Andra Prime in a coffee house at her breakfast in fifteen days. He thought about the letter Orrel had offered him. He thought about what he would write to Lira if he were going to write to her. He thought about the specific words he had been composing in his head for two weeks now, the unsent letter that began *if you hate me afterward, you will be right to hate me*, and he thought about how those words read differently now that they were words he could actually write and have delivered to her, in her hand, on a morning in fifteen days before the findings broke.

He could write the letter.

He could accept the position.

He could be sitting at his desk in this residence wing in three weeks, drinking this same brandy from a glass with his own initials on it, having watched Mouse in a private room in the morning and approved a budget for the Harvester's quarterly substrate procurement in the afternoon and dined with Orrel in the evening, and Lira on Andra Prime would have read his letter and would be carrying his words in her head when the findings broke, and she would understand — perhaps — that what he had become was the thing he had always been becoming, and the becoming was a long fall that he had been falling for eleven years before he had noticed he was falling.

He could do that.

He understood, sitting with the third glass, that he was not going to.

He did not know yet what he was going to do instead. He did not know whether there was a *what he was going to do instead* available to him. He understood only that whatever the answer was, the answer was not the position. He had known this since approximately the moment Orrel said *I will only ensure it is delivered*. The letter was the lever. The letter was the cleanest, most precisely calibrated lever Orrel had ever found to press on him. And Orrel had pressed it, and Kester had felt the pressing, and the pressing had clarified him.

He was not going to write the letter through Orrel.

If Lira learned the truth from the press release, she would learn it from the press release. Kester had given up the right to be the one who told her, eleven years ago. He could not now reclaim that right by writing through the man who had built the wing. Whatever Lira eventually learned of him, she would learn from a source that was not Orrel. It might be no source at all. She might never know the full picture. That was, possibly, what he had earned.

He drank the third glass.

He set it down.

He did not pour a fourth.

He put his hand to his face. He registered that his face was wet. He had been weeping for some portion of the time he had been sitting at the table — he did not know how long, he did not know when it had begun. He wiped his face on the sleeve of the cream tunic that the residence had provided him. The fabric was soft against his cheek. The softness produced, in him, a small sound that was perhaps a laugh and was perhaps not.

He thought about Pira.

He thought specifically about the moment in Sable's arms when Pira had asked *Will you remember me.* The voice. The clarity. The way the question had been a real question — a four-year-old's request for a real answer. He thought about Sable saying *I will remember every day*, and how the answer had been a real answer, made in the moment by a woman who was at that point already broken and already kind.

The promise was real.

The promise was being kept tonight, in the west quarter of the residence wing, by the woman who had made it.

The promise had been made about a child who was alive at the time the promise was made.

Kester sat at the table and understood, with the small clarity that the brandy had given him, that he was going to keep Sable's secret. He was not going to tell her about the shuttle. Not in the morning. Not before the rescue attempt. Not while the rescue attempt was running. Not until the rescue attempt was concluded, one way or the other. Sable's promise to Pira was the load-bearing structure of Sable's continued operation, and Kester was going to leave that structure intact for as long as the rescue required Sable to function.

He was going to lie by omission to a woman he loved, about a child she loved, in order to use her capacity to act on behalf of another child she loved.

He was going to do this.

He understood that he was going to do this and he understood that the doing of it was its own quiet betrayal, smaller than Ivo's and more deliberate and harder to forgive himself for, and he understood that he was going to do it anyway because there was nothing else to do.

He sat with this for some time.

The candles guttered.

Eventually he stood up.

He stood up slowly, because his legs did not want to take his weight. He held the edge of the table for a moment until his balance found him. He pushed his chair back. He turned toward the door.

He took two steps.

He stopped.

The augmented man in the doorway had not been there when Orrel left. Kester had watched the door close behind Orrel and the doorway had been empty. The augmented man was there now. He had appeared at some point during the time Kester had been sitting with the brandy, and he had taken his position in the doorway with the noiseless professional grace that Kester had come to recognize, in the last four hours, as the operational signature of the residence wing's staff.

The man was very tall.

He was perhaps six feet four. He was *built* — Kester registered the breadth of the shoulders, the thickness of the arms, the way the cream-colored uniform sat across his chest as though it had been tailored over earlier muscle and could no longer entirely contain what was beneath it. His face was clean. His hair was cropped short. He did not appear, on first inspection, dramatically different from the staff Kester had been observing all evening.

His eyes were wrong.

They were not black, exactly. Kester registered, with the small specific attention of a man who had learned, in the last hour, what augmented operatives looked like up close — that the irises were a very dark gray that read, in the dining room's candlelight, as black. The pupils inside the dark gray were not dilated and not contracted. They were *fixed*. They held one position regardless of the light.

The man was not looking at Kester.

He was looking at the empty space approximately one meter in front of him. He was at attention. He was waiting. He had been instructed to wait, by someone Kester did not know about, and he was waiting.

Kester took one more step toward the door.

The man did not move.

Kester took another step.

The man still did not move.

Kester stopped at perhaps a meter and a half from the doorway. He looked at the man. He looked at the doorway behind the man. He looked at the hallway visible through the doorway, beyond the man, the warm-lit corridor with its alcoves of pale flowers.

The man did not look at him.

Kester said, quietly: "I would like to go to my room."

The man did not respond. He continued to look at the empty space one meter in front of him. He continued to wait.

Kester registered, then, with the small clear administrative discipline he had been using all evening, that the man had not been instructed to *speak* to him. The man had been instructed only to *be* there. The man's instructions, whatever they were, did not include conversation. The man would respond to whatever stimulus his instructions specified, and he would not respond to anything else, and Kester's question had not been the stimulus.

Kester took a step backward.

The man did not move.

Kester took another step backward, into the dining room. He turned, slowly, to look at the two staff members against the walls. They were watching him. Their faces were, as they had been all evening, calm and untroubled and entirely professional.

He understood.

He stood in the dining room of the Harvester's residence wing, at the end of an evening that had begun with the warm welcome of an aide named Veneya and a beautiful corridor and a private suite with clothes laid out for him on the bed, and he understood — finally, completely, without any further compartmentalization being available to him — that he was not a guest. He had not been a guest at any point in the evening. The guest costume had been the dinner. The dinner was over. The costume was being removed. The augmented man in the doorway was the configuration the residence wing had always actually been operating in, and the warmth had been the temporary overlay the residence had performed for the duration of the meal.

He was in custody.

He had been in custody since the moment Aide Veneya had bowed in the hangar.

He looked at the man in the doorway one more time.

He looked at his hands. His hands were still shaking.

He put his hands in the pockets of the dark robe the residence had given him. He turned, slowly, and he walked back to the table. He sat down in the chair he had been sitting in for the last several hours. The candles had burned down by perhaps half an inch since Orrel had left. The decanter held perhaps a third of its original contents. The two glasses sat in front of him.

He looked at the doorway.

The man was still there. The man would, Kester now understood, remain there until someone else issued him a different instruction, and the instruction would not be issued by Kester. Kester was not the audience for the instructions. Kester was the *subject* of them.

He sat in the chair.

He did not pour a fourth glass.

He did not move.

He understood that he was going to sit in this chair, in this dining room, until a staff member came for him in the morning, because the augmented man in the doorway was not going to let him through and there was no version of the next several hours in which he did anything else. He understood that he would not sleep. He understood that he would think, for the duration of the night, about Pira and Yenna and Chayne and Lira and Mouse and Sable and Tev and Pell and Aleya and the seven sites and the fourteen thousand children and the unnamed Architect who had recruited Orrel some years after Calderon and whom Orrel was not at liberty to name, and he understood that the thinking would not produce any answer he did not already have.

He understood that whatever he was going to do, in the morning, he was going to do tired and grieving and hungover and watched, and the augmented man in the doorway would still be there, and the Harvester would still be the Harvester, and Mouse would still be on her schedule, and Sable would still be in her quarters with Resh in the other bed, and the universe would still contain the unanswered question of whether anyone on Andra Prime was alive.

He sat in the chair.

The candles burned.

Above the residence wing, beyond the green sky he could not see from the windowless dining room, two yellow suns wheeled through their slow shared orbit around a planet whose name appeared on no Hegemony chart, and the planet continued, and the conspiracy continued, and the Architect — whoever, wherever — continued, unnamed, unmet, unfound, the apex of a thing whose base was a forty-eight-meter shaft on a moon called Tartaros and whose middle had consumed everyone Kester loved.

He sat in the chair.

He waited for the morning.
