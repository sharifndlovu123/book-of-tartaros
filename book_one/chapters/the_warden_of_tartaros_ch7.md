# The Warden of Tartaros
## Chapter Seven: The Palace Beneath

Sable was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the chamber when Kester found her.

It was perhaps eight in the morning. Aleya's cot had been moved. The blanket had been folded. Tev had taken the body somewhere — to one of the deeper chambers, Kester thought, where the Undersiders kept their small dignities, where the dead were laid out and named and waited for whatever ceremony the community had cobbled together over the years to honor them. Kester had not asked. He understood, by now, that there were rooms in this place that belonged to other people, and that walking into them uninvited was the kind of thing he had spent his life doing and would now spend the rest of it learning not to do.

Sable was on the floor where the cot had been.

She was not crying. She was not, as far as Kester could tell, doing anything at all. She was sitting with her hands open on her knees, palms up, the way a person sits when they have decided to stop holding things.

He stood in the doorway for a long moment.

"Sable."

She did not turn around.

"Come in, Kester."

He came in. He sat down across from her, on the floor, because the chairs felt wrong and the table felt wrong and he understood, without being told, that this was not a conversation that was going to happen at furniture. He sat and he waited.

After a while she looked up.

He had been preparing himself, on the walk over, for what her face would look like. He had thought it would be hard. He had thought it would be the face of a woman who had decided to kill until she died — closed, narrowed, set. He had thought he would recognize the shape of Bastien Orrel after Calderon Trench, and he had been preparing himself to mourn the woman she had been before this morning.

Her face was not like that.

Her face was *gentle*.

She was looking at him with the kind of softness Kester had not seen on her before — had not, he realized, seen on anyone in a long time, perhaps not since his daughter Lira had been very small and had looked at him without yet knowing what he did for a living. It was the softness of a person who had nothing left to defend. It was the softness of a person who had stopped expecting the world to be different than it was.

It was the softness, Kester realized with a small cold lurch, that he had seen on Bastien Orrel for nine years.

It was not the same softness, exactly. Orrel's was performed. Sable's was real. But the *shape* of it was the same — the gentleness of a person who had decided what they were going to do, and who was now being kind to the people around them as a way of saying goodbye to the version of themselves that those people had loved. Orrel had been performing it for nine years because he had decided long ago, on a battlefield, that the world would not be changed by argument. Sable had arrived at the same conclusion overnight.

Kester wanted, very badly, to weep.

He did not.

"There's a deeper section," Sable said. Her voice was soft and level and entirely unhurried, the way a person speaks when they have stopped being in any kind of rush. "Beneath the under-levels. We've never gone down there. We always assumed it was structural — load-bearing concrete, foundation work, nothing built for human use. We were wrong. There's a door three levels below us that Ivo has been studying for two years. He says it's recent. He says it's not on any prison schematic. He says whoever built it put a great deal of care into making sure we would not look at it."

"You want to go through it."

"Yes."

"Today."

"Today."

Kester was quiet.

"Why today," he said.

Sable considered the question, the way a person considers a question that they have already answered to themselves but that they feel they owe the asker an answer to anyway. She tilted her head slightly. Her hands, on her knees, did not move.

"Because there is no point waiting anymore," she said. "We thought we had time. We thought the broadcast had bought us a window — a few weeks, maybe a month, while the Hegemony processed the spin and the Federation observers came and went. We were wrong about that. The Hegemony is not processing. The Hegemony is *moving*. They moved the cohort last night. They will move on us soon. If there is anything left to find on Tartaros, we have to find it now, today, before the next thing happens. After the next thing happens it will be too late." She paused. "Also Aleya is dead and I cannot sit in this chamber any longer."

"Sable — "

"Don't, Kester. I am not going to talk about her. Not today. Maybe not for a long time. There is a place she is, in me, and I am keeping her there, and the place is going to do its work without my supervision. I do not need to manage it. I need to *move*."

She stood up.

She did it with the easy gentleness of a woman who had not slept and had decided that not sleeping was no longer the kind of thing that would slow her down. She brushed her palms on her trousers. She looked down at Kester. Her face had not hardened. Her face was still soft.

She reached down and touched his shoulder.

"Kester," she said. "You don't have to come."

He looked up at her.

"I mean it," she said. The soft voice. The soft hand. "I am not asking you to come. I am telling you what we are doing, because you should know, but I am not asking you to come with me. There is no shame in staying in the chamber. Chayne could use you here. Tev could use you here. The records you have been making — somebody needs to keep making them, and you are good at it, and the work is real. You can stay. I will not blame you. I will not think less of you. I want you to understand this, before you decide. You have been carrying a great deal and you do not owe me the next thing. The next thing is mine. It is not yours."

Kester did not say anything for a long moment.

He was looking at her hand on his shoulder. The hand was warm and steady. It was the hand of a woman who was, as far as he could tell, no longer afraid of anything that could happen to her body.

He was thinking about Bastien Orrel, who had also stopped being afraid.

He was thinking about the shape they shared.

He was thinking that Sable did not yet know she had become this thing, and that telling her would not help, and that he was going to have to walk beside her for as long as he could in the hope that some of his own remaining fear might rub off on her like a blanket on a cold night.

"I'm coming," he said.

She nodded, once. She did not seem surprised.

"Thank you, Kester."

She walked out of the chamber.

He sat on the floor for another minute before he followed.

---

The expedition assembled at oh-nine-hundred, in the small staging area Ivo had been quietly fitting out for two years. Six of them. Sable. Kester. Kell Davour, who had been a Hegemony military police officer and who walked like one and who had not spoken to Kester directly in the three days since he had arrived in the under-levels because he was the kind of person who decided whether to trust a man over a long span of observation rather than a short span of conversation. Ivo, who had blown up the tram that had started this and who, now that Kester was meeting him properly, looked very tired in a way that had nothing to do with the last few days. Yenna Sorell, who was small and quiet and who carried a leather satchel of paper notebooks because she did not trust digital storage on principle. And Resh, the eighteen-year-old, who had not spoken since Mouse was taken and who was not going to start speaking now.

They were checking gear. They were not saying much.

Tev came over from the alcove where she had set up her medical station for the day. She had a small pouch in her hand. She walked up to Kester, who was the easiest of the six to approach because he was the only one not pretending to be busy, and she held the pouch out to him.

"What is it," he said.

"Field analgesic. Three doses. Pell carried it. It's yours now."

He took it.

He did not know what to say.

Tev looked at him. Her eyes were red but her hands were steady. She had been crying for hours, off and on, in the way a woman cries when she has lost a patient and knows she will lose more — quietly, while continuing to work, the tears arriving on their own schedule and dealt with as they came. She was looking at Kester now with an expression he could not read.

"Mr. Vaile," she said.

"Yes."

"I want to say something to you before you go."

"All right."

She was quiet for a moment, the way a person is quiet when they are choosing words they want to be precise.

"You were one of the bad ones," she said. "I want you to know I know that. I want you to know that I have not forgotten it. You signed the paperwork. You filed the vouchers. You moved the supplies that built the wing my friends died in. I will not pretend, to you or to myself, that those things did not happen. They happened. You did them."

Kester did not say anything. He was standing very still.

"But Mr. Vaile," Tev said, "we all do things with our eyes closed. Most of the world is built by people walking around with their eyes closed. The cruelty of it is that the world rewards us for keeping them closed. The work goes faster. The pay is better. The sleep is longer. We close our eyes and we get on with it and we tell ourselves that the things we cannot quite see are not really there." She paused. "And then sometimes a person opens their eyes. It happens for different reasons, to different people. Some open theirs because of love. Some because of grief. Some because they get old enough to be tired of the lie. You opened yours because an inspector asked you a question in a commissary and you could not bear it anymore. That is a stupid reason. That is also a real reason. The reason does not matter, in the end. What matters is that they are open."

Kester's eyes were burning. He did not blink. He was afraid that if he blinked he would cry, and he understood, with the small clear discipline he had been learning, that crying in front of Tev right now would be a thing he was doing for himself rather than for her.

"Now you have to choose," Tev said. "That is the part that comes after. The eyes are open and the choice is in front of you, and the world does not get easier from here — it gets harder, because now you can see, and seeing is heavier than not seeing. I am sorry. I wish it were the other way. It is not." She put her free hand briefly on his arm. "Make the choice well, Mr. Vaile. Make it for the right reasons. And whatever happens to you down there, whatever you find — remember that you opened your eyes. That is the thing you did. Nobody can take it back from you. Not Orrel. Not the Board. Not yourself, in your weak hours. You opened them. The rest is just the work of keeping them open."

She squeezed his arm, once, and walked back to her medical station.

Kester stood holding the pouch.

He did not weep. He did not have time to weep. He put the pouch in his jacket pocket and he turned to the rest of the expedition and he said, in a voice that was almost steady:

"I'm ready."

---

They went down through Ivo's shaft.

It took three hours. The deeper sections of the prison were not, as Sable had said, what the Undersiders had assumed. The first two levels below the under-levels chamber were structural, exactly as expected — load-bearing concrete, exposed rebar, the cold bones of the prison's foundation. The third level was different. The third level had a *finish* on it. The walls had been smoothed. The floor was not concrete but a kind of dark synthetic that absorbed sound. The air was cleaner. The temperature was warmer. The lighting, when Ivo activated the corridor's sensor strips, came up gentle and diffuse — not the harsh white of the prison's industrial fixtures, but something softer, almost domestic.

"This isn't a foundation," Davour said quietly. He had his sidearm out. He was speaking for the first time in the expedition, and his voice was lower and more careful than Kester had expected. "This is a residential level."

"It can't be," Yenna said. "Who would live three levels under a prison."

"Somebody who didn't want to be found," Davour said.

They moved on.

The corridor went for sixty meters before it ended in the door Ivo had been studying for two years.

The door was beautiful. Kester registered this with a small bewildered shock. He had been expecting an industrial blast door, a security lock, the kind of utilitarian barrier the prison used for its hardened sections. The door in front of them was made of a dark hardwood that he did not recognize, inlaid with brass in a pattern he eventually recognized as a stylized version of the Hegemony seal. The lock was not a security lock. It was a *handle*. A brass handle. It looked, to Kester, like the door of a private library on a wealthy man's estate.

Ivo studied it. He had a kit of small tools out. He did not use any of them. After a moment he reached out and tried the handle.

The door opened.

"It's not locked," Ivo said.

"That's worse," Davour said.

They went through.

---

The palace.

There was no other word for it. Kester stood in the doorway and looked at what was on the other side of the brass-handled door, and he understood that the language he had been using to describe his life for nine years had not contained the word for this place, because the existence of this place would have made the rest of his life impossible to continue.

The room beyond the door was a foyer. There was a chandelier — actual cut crystal, hanging from a domed ceiling that had been frescoed with a scene Kester could not parse from this angle but that involved figures in classical drapery and what might have been a bull. The floor was marble. Real marble, with veining that was not a print. The walls were paneled in the same dark wood as the door. There were two chairs against one wall, upholstered in a dark green silk. There was a small table between them with a porcelain bowl on it, the bowl empty but immaculate, the kind of bowl that had been chosen by someone who knew the names of porcelain manufacturers.

The air smelled faintly of cedar.

"What is this place," Yenna whispered.

Sable had walked into the foyer. She was looking at the chandelier with an expression Kester could not read. She walked across the marble floor — her boots, which had been mucky from the deeper levels, made small careful prints — and she stopped at the far doorway and looked through it.

"It keeps going," she said.

It kept going.

The foyer opened on a long gallery, hung with paintings — actual oil paintings, in gilded frames, of landscapes Kester did not recognize and portraits of men and women in formal dress. The portraits were old. The frames had small brass plates. The names on the plates meant nothing to Kester, but he registered, dimly, that they were *names* — that the people in the portraits had been named, that someone had cared enough to identify them, that this was not a generic decoration but a *collection*. Someone's family. Someone's lineage. Someone's pride.

The gallery led to a dining room. Long table. Sixteen chairs. Crystal on a sideboard. A second chandelier, smaller than the foyer's. A fireplace, and in the fireplace, a small neat stack of logs that had not been lit but had been arranged with the kind of care a butler arranges things when the master is expected.

"Somebody has been maintaining this," Davour said.

"Recently," Ivo agreed.

"Look at the dust," Yenna said.

There was almost no dust. The air recyclers were running — Kester could hear them, low and steady, behind the walls — and the surfaces had been cleaned within the last week or two. The dining room had not been used recently, Kester thought. But it had been *prepared* for use. The crystal had been polished. The chairs had been straightened.

They moved through the dining room and into a corridor that turned twice and ended at another door.

This door was different. It was heavier than the dining room's interior doors, and it had a small pad beside it — a security control, the only one Kester had seen so far in the palace, and even it was tasteful, brushed brass set into the wall paneling with the discretion of a thermostat.

Ivo studied the pad.

"This one's actually locked," he said.

He worked on it for ten minutes. Davour watched the corridor behind them. Sable stood very still, looking at the door. Yenna had taken one of her notebooks out and was sketching the corridor's layout with quick neat lines. Resh stood at Sable's shoulder and did not move and did not speak.

The door clicked.

Ivo pushed it open.

---

The room beyond the door was the board room.

Kester knew it the moment he saw it. He had spent nine years filing meeting notes for rooms that looked, in his imagination, almost exactly like this — long oval table, leather chairs with high backs, a sideboard with decanters, a screen on the wall opposite the head of the table, a low light that was warm without being intimate. The room was not lavish in the way the foyer and the dining room were lavish. The room was *functional*. It was the working room of a board that had been working in this palace for a long time, and the room's beauty was an afterthought to its purpose.

There were nine chairs around the table.

Nine.

Kester's mind cataloged this. Nine principals. Nine seats. The Board he had been assembling in his head was larger than he had thought.

There were papers on the table.

They were arranged neatly. Stacked at three of the nine seats. Folders. Notebooks. A bound volume that looked, from across the room, like a printed report. Whoever had last met in this room had left their working materials behind, and whoever maintained the palace had not removed them, because the materials had been left in a state of *paused work* — the meeting was not concluded. The Board would be coming back to this room. Soon, probably.

"Yenna," Sable said quietly.

"Yes."

"Read everything you can. Take it all. Take what you can carry and photograph the rest. We will not get a second chance."

"Yes."

Yenna went to the table.

She moved like a librarian into a fire. She was fast and gentle and she did not waste a motion. She opened the first folder and she began to read, and within twenty seconds her face had changed in a way Kester did not yet have the context to understand.

He went to the table beside her.

He looked over her shoulder.

The folder was a financial report. Quarterly. The header read: *Project Renewal — Q3 2371 — Tartaros Node — Substrate Yield.* Below the header was a chart. The chart showed numbers Kester recognized. They were the numbers he had been signing for nine years. Substrate Maintenance line items. Cohort assessment fees. Travel vouchers for Dr. Vorlith. They were here, in summary form, totaled and signed off on by — 

"There are signatures," Yenna whispered.

There were signatures.

There were nine signatures, on the report's final page. Most of them were illegible — careful, professional signatures of the kind that have been deliberately rendered unreadable by their owners — but two were clear enough to read. One read *S. Vorlith*. One read *B. Orrel*.

Orrel's signature was at the bottom of the second column. He was not at the head of the page. He was in the *middle*. He was — Kester counted — the sixth signature down.

Orrel was not the chair of this board.

Orrel was a *member*. A junior member. Possibly the most junior member. His signature came after five others.

Kester's mouth was dry.

"Yenna," he said. "What else."

She was already reading. She had moved to the second folder. Her notebook was open beside her and she was making notes in a tight quick hand.

"Personnel tracking," she said. "They — Kester, they have files on us. On me. There's a file with my name on it. There's — "

She pulled it out.

It was a thin file. It contained, Kester could see over her shoulder, a copy of an academic paper she had written, with marginalia in three different hands. It contained a printed photograph of her. It contained a memo dated 2370 — last year — that began *Subject Sorell continues to correspond with Federation auditor Adeyemi. Recommend continued surveillance with intervention pending if subject pursues formal investigation channels*.

Yenna sat down, slowly, in the chair nearest the file.

"They knew about me," she said. "They knew I was talking to her. They — they let me keep talking to her. They let her come to Tartaros. They — "

"Yenna."

"They let her come because she was my source. They wanted to see what I would tell her. They wanted to know how much I knew. They — Kester, they killed her because I — "

"Yenna. *No*."

"I told her about Wing Seven. I told her everything I had. She came to Tartaros because of what I told her. She died because of what I told her. They — "

"Yenna, listen to me." Kester crouched next to her chair. "They killed her because she got too close. Not because of you. Because of *her*. She did her job. She came here. She listened to me. She was going to file a report and they could not let her file it. That's why she died. You did not kill her. They killed her. *Listen to me.*"

Yenna was nodding. She was nodding and she was not listening. She was reading the next page of the file. Her hands were shaking now, finally, the way they had not shaken in the corridor.

Sable was standing on the other side of the table. She had not moved. She was watching Yenna read. Her face was still soft.

"Keep reading, Yenna," Sable said gently. "Read all of it. Take all of it. It's worse than you think but you have to keep going, sweetheart. We can grieve later. Right now we need to know."

Yenna nodded.

She kept reading.

---

The screen activated forty minutes later.

They had been working through the papers in silence — Yenna at the head of the table reading and noting, Kester moving through the financial reports beside her, Davour watching the door, Ivo studying the room's other systems for surveillance, Resh standing at Sable's elbow and Sable standing very still and watching. They had assembled, by then, a partial picture.

The Board had nine members. Vorlith was one. Orrel was one — a junior one. The other seven were referred to in the documents only by initial: *A.M., D.K., R.T., V.S., O.L., J.H.,* and one whose initial appeared in only one document, in a single line at the bottom of a memo, marked *for the Architect's review*.

The Board had been operating since 2349. Twenty-two years.

Tartaros was one of *seven* facilities. The others were named only by code: *Site Two, Site Three, Site Four, Site Five, Site Six, Site Seven*. Tartaros was Site One. The original. The prototype. Each of the other six sites had its own warden, its own scientist, its own administrator — its own Orrel and its own Vorlith and its own Kester. Kester had been one of seven men.

The Harvester was named in three documents.

It was named only by name. There were no coordinates in any document Yenna had so far read. The coordinates were held separately, presumably, by a smaller subset of the Board. The Architect, certainly. Probably one or two of the others.

The Harvester was where the cohorts went. All seven sites' cohorts. For decades. The number of children processed by the Harvester since 2349 was — Yenna read the figure twice, to make sure — *approximately fourteen thousand*.

Fourteen thousand.

Kester had stopped reading. He was sitting in one of the leather chairs. He was looking at a wall. He was thinking, with the dim clarity of a man whose moral universe had just expanded by an order of magnitude, that he had spent nine years on one of seven farms, and that the farms together had fed a single mill, and that the mill had been grinding for twenty-two years before he ever set foot on Tartaros.

The screen on the wall came on.

It was a soft chime first — the kind of chime an executive office uses to indicate an incoming call. Then the screen brightened. Then Bastien Orrel was on it.

He was sitting at his desk in the citadel. The desk Kester had sat across from a thousand times. The brandy decanter was on the corner. The lamp was on. Orrel was wearing his black dress uniform. His hands were folded on the desk in front of him.

He was not smiling.

This was the thing Kester noticed first, and the thing that disoriented him most. Orrel's face, on the screen, was *neutral*. Professional. The face of a station chief reporting to his superiors. There was no warmth in it and no cruelty in it. There was only the steady, slightly tired expression of a man at work.

This was the Orrel the Board saw.

This was the Orrel Kester had never seen.

"Mr. Vaile," Orrel said. His voice was different too — quieter, less performed. "Sable. Others. I will keep this brief, because I assume you are reading the materials and I do not wish to delay your work.

"You have reached the residence. This was always possible. I want you to understand that I am not surprised. I planned for this, though I did not plan for it to happen this week. The recent events have accelerated several timelines. I am adapting. You should adapt as well.

"I want to make you an offer, Mr. Vaile. I am making it because we have nine years of professional acquaintance and because I believe you are, at this moment, in a position to make a decision rather than a mistake. The offer is simple. I will tell you where Mouse is going. I will tell you when she will be there. I will tell you the name of the facility and I will tell you the schedule of her transit. I will tell you all of this, today, freely, with no immediate condition attached. You may use the information as you wish."

Orrel paused.

"In return, I am going to ask you to do nothing immediately. I am going to ask you to live with the information for a few days. I am going to ask you to consider, in those days, what you want for the girl. You have come to feel responsibility for her. I respect this. I share it. We have, between us, been the two men most responsible for what she has become, and I would like us to discuss her future as men who have raised a daughter together discuss her future. I would like you to come and see her, Mr. Vaile. I would like you to come to the Harvester. I will give you safe passage. You may bring whoever you trust. You may bring Sable, if she will come. You may bring all of them.

"Come and see what the next phase is. Come and see what Mouse is becoming. Then make your choice. If you choose to take her and leave, I will not stop you — though I will also not be able to protect you afterward, and others will. If you choose to stay and resume work, there is a position waiting for you that befits your skills. If you choose to do something else entirely, that is also your right. I am not here to coerce you. I am here to *show you*, because I believe you have not yet seen, and you cannot make a decision about Mouse's future without having seen.

"The information about her location and schedule will be transmitted to a drop point in the under-levels at twenty-two hundred local time tonight. Ivo will know how to retrieve it. If you do not retrieve it, no harm done — the offer stands and the information will be made available to you again at a later date.

"That is what I came to say. I understand this is a great deal. I understand you have, at this moment, a great deal else to absorb. I am sorry for the timing. The timing was not entirely mine to choose.

"Goodbye, Mr. Vaile. I look forward to our next conversation."

The screen went dark.

The board room was silent.

Kester was not breathing. He realized this and made himself breathe. He realized, at the same time, that he had been hearing the offer not as a threat but as a *gift*, that some part of him had been listening to Orrel's voice with the same attention he had given it for nine years across a desk, that he had been *agreeing* on some animal level before any of his rational faculties had caught up.

He looked at Sable.

She was looking at him.

Her face was still soft. The softness had not changed. But there was something behind her eyes now that he had not seen before, and he understood, with the cold lurch he had begun to recognize as the lurch of his own life rearranging itself, that she had watched him hear the offer. She had watched his face. She had seen what was there.

She did not say anything.

She turned, slowly, to Yenna.

"Keep reading," she said gently.

Yenna kept reading.

---

It was Yenna who found the Undersider files.

There was a folder, slim, indexed under *Tartaros Node — Surface Containment*. Inside it: surveillance summaries. Photographs taken from inside the chamber, somehow, at angles Kester could not source. A diagram of the chamber's layout that was almost correct but slightly outdated. A list of names, with annotations. *Sable Reyek. Chayne Olret. Tev Marn. Sten Haveric. Ivo Vens.* The names of the principal Undersiders, with brief biographical notes, with assessments of their likely behavior under various contingencies.

The list contained forty-three names of children.

The actual number, Sable had told Kester two nights ago, had been forty-seven.

The Board had been counting wrong.

Yenna read the file aloud, in the voice of a woman who had stopped feeling what she was reading, because feeling it would have stopped her from reading. She read the names. She read the assessments. She read the contingency plans.

There were three contingencies. *Contingency A: Broadcast event with international attention. Response protocol: managed exposure of named warden, public reform commission, internal cohort transfer to Harvester, surface team neutralization within seventy-two hours of broadcast.*

The broadcast had been seventy-two hours ago, almost exactly.

*Surface team neutralization* — that was the Undersiders.

"They're attacking the chamber," Davour said. His voice was flat. "Right now. This is happening right now."

"Yes," Sable said.

"How long have they been there."

Yenna scrolled through the file. "The transfer of the cohort was at oh-two hundred. The surface team neutralization is scheduled for — " She paused. " — for oh-nine-hundred. That's an hour ago. They've been in the chamber for an hour."

The board room was very quiet.

Sable closed her eyes.

She did not weep. She did not move. Her hands were at her sides. She was breathing, slowly, in through her nose and out through her mouth. The softness of her face had not left it. The softness had become something else — not harder, but *deeper*, the way a lake deepens when you step further out into it.

She opened her eyes.

"We have to go back," she said.

"There won't be anyone left," Davour said.

"We have to go back, Kell."

"Sable. There won't be anyone left. The team that hits the chamber is not Internal Security, it's a clearance unit. They don't take prisoners. They don't leave anyone alive. They've been there an hour. You know what an hour means."

"I know what an hour means," Sable said. "We are still going back."

Davour looked at her.

After a moment Davour nodded, once.

"Take everything," Sable said to Yenna. "Take everything you can carry. Photograph the rest. We have eight minutes and then we go."

Yenna started moving.

Kester stood up from the leather chair he had been sitting in. He had been thinking, for the last several minutes, about Orrel's offer. He had been thinking about Mouse. He had been thinking about the position waiting for him at the Harvester.

He had been thinking about all of these things and he had been ashamed of himself for thinking about them, and then he had been more ashamed of himself for noticing that he was ashamed, because the noticing was a kind of self-regard he could not afford right now. He had stood up to make himself stop thinking.

He went to help Yenna with the papers.

He did not look at Sable.

He was afraid, very specifically, that if he looked at her she would still be looking at him, and he did not yet know what he would do if she was.

---

They left the palace at oh-nine-fifty-two.

They climbed back up through Ivo's shaft as fast as their bodies would carry them. Davour led. Sable came second. Kester was third, with Yenna's satchel over one shoulder, the satchel heavy with paper and the small sealed device on which Yenna had photographed everything she could not carry. Ivo and Yenna and Resh came behind. Nobody spoke. The climb took them ninety minutes, and the ninety minutes felt like a year, because every meter of the climb was a meter closer to whatever was waiting for them in the chamber.

When they reached the under-levels' main corridor, they stopped to listen.

The chamber was silent.

Kester had been hoping, dimly and against all of his better judgment, that they would arrive in the middle of the attack — that there would still be guards in the chamber, that Davour would lead them into a fight, that the question of whether anyone was alive would still be open and they would close it with their hands. He had wanted, with a kind of awful child-want, for the situation to still be a situation he could affect.

The silence told him otherwise.

The clearance unit was already gone. They had finished. They had left. The chamber was — the chamber was whatever it was, now, on the other side of the corridor's last bend.

Davour went around the bend first.

She went with her sidearm raised. She went the way a soldier goes into a room she expects to clear. She had been a Hegemony military police officer for many years before her arrest, and her body had been trained, and her body did the trained thing now, because thinking would have been worse.

She stopped in the chamber's doorway.

Sable went past her.

Kester came next.

What he saw, when he came around the corner, was this:

The chamber's worklights were still on. The air recyclers were still running. The folding chairs and the small forest of his ration cards and the salvaged crates and Tev's medical station were all where they had been when the expedition left, three hours and a lifetime ago.

Tev was on the floor of the alcove. She had fallen between the children's cots and the door. Her body was where she would have placed it deliberately if she had had time to plan — between the door and the cots, between the threat and the children. She had not had time to plan. She had moved, when she heard the boots, and she had moved to the place she would have planned to move to, because the place was the same place either way. She had been hit twice. She was not breathing.

Sten was against the chamber's east wall. He was sitting up. He was bleeding from a wound in his thigh and a wound in his side and he was holding a salvaged welding torch in his right hand as if the welding torch were a weapon, which it was not, and which it had not been when the guards came through, and which he had presumably been using for something practical when the attack began. He was conscious. His eyes tracked Sable as she came into the chamber. He made a sound that was not a word. His mouth tried to make a word and could not.

Chayne was not in the chamber's main area.

Sable moved toward Sten. Davour moved with her, already pulling a field kit from his own pack, already kneeling. Yenna ran for Tev because Yenna had not yet absorbed that Tev was past help, and the running was Yenna's body refusing to absorb it for another few seconds. Resh stood in the doorway and did not move and did not make a sound.

Kester walked to the wall.

He walked to the section of wall, behind one of the salvaged crates, where Chayne had told him three nights ago that he kept his archive — the small false ventilation duct, hidden behind a panel that Chayne had built himself, where for years Chayne had been quietly storing every piece of stolen citadel paperwork the Undersiders had been able to acquire. Hard drives. Printed dossiers. The records of a records officer who had not stopped being one when he was arrested. Chayne's life's work, hidden behind a vent.

The panel had been moved.

Kester crouched down.

He pulled the panel aside, gently, with hands that he was making himself keep gentle.

Chayne was inside the duct.

He was alive. He was breathing in the small thin breaths of a man who had been crammed into a space too small for him for a long time. He was holding, in his arms, a child.

The child was perhaps four years old. A girl. Her face was pressed against Chayne's shoulder and her eyes were closed and her small hands were fisted in the fabric of Chayne's shirt. She was breathing too. She was alive.

She was the only child in the chamber.

Chayne saw Kester.

He did not say anything. His eyes were wet. He moved his head, very slightly, to indicate the child in his arms, and then he closed his eyes again.

"She's all right," Kester said. He did not know if he was saying it to Chayne or to himself or to the child or to no one. "It's all right. It's over. I'm going to get you out. Both of you. It's going to be all right."

He reached into the duct and took the child first.

She did not wake up.

He carried her over to the cot Tev had used for Aleya, the day before, the lifetime before, and he laid her down on the blanket Tev had folded that morning. The child was very small. Her hair was dark and it was wet at the temples with sweat. She had been hidden in that duct for an hour. She would not, Kester thought, remember it. He hoped she would not remember it.

He went back for Chayne.

It took him and Davour together to get the old man out. Chayne's knees, which had been bad before, were now worse. He could not stand. They carried him to a chair and they put him in it and Davour started checking him for injuries, and Chayne, in the chair, was finally able to speak.

"Tev," he said.

"Yes," Davour said gently. "I know."

"She — they came through the south passage. She was at the alcove. She — she didn't move, Kell. I told her to move. I told her to come with me. She did not move."

"I know."

"I had — the child was — I had her in my arms. I went for the duct. I had to choose. I had to — "

"You chose right, Chayne. You chose what Tev would have wanted. Don't talk now. Let me look at you."

Chayne let her look at him.

He was crying without sound. He was not making any noise at all. Tears were running down his face and into his beard and he was sitting in the chair and he was not making any sound.

Kester stood in the middle of the chamber and looked around it.

Tev was dead. Sten was alive but bleeding. Chayne was alive in his chair. The child was alive on the cot.

Of the rest of the chamber's population — the unnamed Undersiders, the older children who had been hidden here, the people who had received Sable's broadcast as a victory and Aleya's death as a wound — there were nine bodies on the floor, and there were no others. There had been perhaps thirty people in the chamber when the expedition left. There were thirteen alive now, including the six who had just returned. Seven Undersiders survived: Chayne. Sten. The child. And four others Kester would not learn the names of for another several hours, who had escaped through deeper passages when the attack began, and who would trickle back into the chamber over the course of the night.

Seven.

Seven Undersiders.

Kester counted them on his hands.

He still had three fingers left over.

---

Sable was kneeling beside Tev's body.

She had not, since they came into the chamber, made a sound. She had moved to Sten first, helped Davour stabilize him, accepted the field kit, applied the dressings. She had moved to Chayne next, listened to his account, said *thank you, Chayne, you did right*. She had moved to the child on the cot, looked at her, touched her hair very lightly, said nothing.

She had come to Tev last.

She knelt beside the body. Tev's eyes were open. Sable closed them, gently, the way Tev had closed Aleya's eyes the night before. Sable folded Tev's hands across Tev's chest, the way Tev had folded Aleya's. Sable pulled a clean cloth — there was one in Tev's medical station, and Sable knew where it was because Sable had watched Tev work for years — and she covered Tev's face.

She sat beside the body for a long minute.

She did not weep.

Then she stood up, slowly, and she turned, and she looked at the chamber, and she looked at the seven survivors, and her face was still soft.

She said, in a voice that was perfectly steady:

"We bury them tonight. We take care of Sten. We feed the child. Tomorrow we move. The chamber is no longer ours. The under-levels are no longer ours. The Board has known about this place for years and we have been living in their attic. We are going to find somewhere they have not yet thought of, and we are going to take what Yenna has, and we are going to figure out what to do next." She paused. Her eyes moved across the chamber. They moved across Kester's face. They did not linger. "Kester."

"Yes."

"At twenty-two hundred tonight, you and Ivo will go to whatever drop point Orrel has prepared. You will retrieve the information about Mouse. You will bring it back. We will read it together. We will decide together what to do with it. Do you understand."

"Yes."

"Good."

She turned away from him.

She crouched beside the child on the cot. The child was beginning to stir. Sable put her hand on the child's back, very lightly, and waited.

The child opened her eyes.

She was not afraid. She had been hidden in the duct for an hour and she had not made a sound. She was, Kester thought, the kind of child whose silence had been taught to her early. She looked up at Sable with the unsurprised expression of a child for whom adults appearing over her had stopped being events.

"Hello," Sable said.

"Hello," the child said.

"What is your name."

"Pira."

"Pira. That's a beautiful name. Pira, my name is Sable. Are you hurt anywhere."

Pira considered this. She moved her arms, then her legs. "No."

"That's good. Pira, I am going to take care of you now. Is that all right."

Pira looked at Sable for a long moment.

Then she said: "Where is Tev."

Sable did not answer immediately.

"Tev had to go," she said, finally. "She is somewhere else now. I am going to take care of you instead. Is that all right."

Pira nodded, slowly.

Sable lifted her, gently, and held her against her shoulder, the way Tev had held Aleya in the corridor of Building Twelve, the way Tev had held the broadcast deck against her chest in the comms array, the way Tev had held many things in her short life with the practiced gentleness of a woman who held things for a living. Pira put her arms around Sable's neck. She closed her eyes. She did not, as far as Kester could see, cry.

Sable stood in the ruined chamber with the child in her arms and looked at Kester across the bodies and the bloodstains and the small forest of his ration cards on the table, which had not been disturbed, which had been left exactly where the guards had found them, because the guards had not been here for paperwork.

She said, very softly:

"Twenty-two hundred. The drop. Don't be late."

He nodded.

She walked away from him, with Pira in her arms, into the deeper rooms of the chamber where the dead would be laid out and the living would be tended and the long quiet work of a community burying itself would begin.

Kester stood in the doorway.

He stood there for a long time.

Above him, six kilometers up, in a citadel that the Hegemony Information Office was telling the public was now under caretaker administration, Bastien Orrel was finishing his lunch. Across the desk from him, in the chair he kept for guests, Mouse sat with her hands folded in her lap. She had eaten very little. Her implant had been adjusted at oh-nine-hundred to manage the stress of the morning's operations, which she had observed through the citadel's internal monitoring system at Orrel's invitation. She had watched the clearance unit move through the chamber. She had watched Tev fall. She had watched Chayne crawl into the duct with the child.

She had watched it all with great attention.

She had not said anything.

Orrel set down his fork. He looked at her. He smiled, the warm paternal smile he had been smiling at her since she was four years old.

"How are you, sweetheart," he said.

"I'm fine."

"You watched the operation."

"Yes."

"Did you understand what you were watching."

Mouse considered the question, the way a child considers a question from a parent who has asked it deliberately, who wants a specific answer, who will be disappointed if the answer is not the one being expected. She had been considering questions of this shape for nineteen years. She knew what to do.

"I understood," she said.

"Tell me what you understood."

"I understood that the people in the chamber were going to die. I understood that Tev was going to be one of them. I understood that Sable was going to come back and find her, and that Sable would be different afterward. I understood that the work was necessary and that the work was hard and that the work would continue even though Tev was a person I had loved." Mouse paused. Her hands, on her lap, did not move. "I understood that I was supposed to feel something specific while I watched. I am trying to feel it. I am not sure I am feeling the right thing."

"What are you feeling, sweetheart."

"I am not sure."

"That's all right. That's all right, Mouse. You will understand the feeling in time. The next phase will help you understand it. Do you trust me."

Mouse looked at him.

"Yes," she said.

She was, in some compartment of herself that the implant could not reach, screaming.

The compartment was getting smaller. It had been getting smaller every day for nineteen years. She did not know if it would still be there next week.

She thought it might not be.

She thought, with the small clear discipline of a girl who had been taught from infancy to keep her thoughts from her face: *I have to remember Sable's name. I have to remember Tev's name. I have to remember Pira's name. I have to remember what they look like. Tomorrow I will not remember. I will remember today.*

She remembered.

She kept her hands folded in her lap.

She finished her lunch.
