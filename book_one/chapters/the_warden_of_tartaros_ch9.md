# The Warden of Tartaros
## Chapter Nine: The Long Quiet

Davour's briefing took nine minutes.

Pier Three had three perimeter checkpoints, two interior security stations, a control room overlooking the launch bays, and a flight crew quarters where the off-duty pilots slept. Total guard count, by Davour's estimate from the binoculars and from his seven years of Hegemony Military Police experience reading bases like this: twelve to fifteen active personnel, of whom no more than four would be combat-rated, the rest being base security with light training and slow reaction times. The civilian shuttle Yenna's group needed was in the east bay, scheduled for departure at oh-eight hundred under a routine logistics manifest. The military shuttle for Sable's group was in Bay Two, fueled for fourteen hours of transit, with the authorization codes Orrel had provided.

The plan was simple. Davour, Sable, and Resh would go in through the west service hatch — a maintenance entrance Davour had used during his MP years, which was not on the active patrol's primary attention list. They would clear the interior in stages: control room first, then the security stations, then the crew quarters. Kester and Ivo would stay with the transport at the perimeter and handle the loading of Yenna's group onto the civilian shuttle once the interior was clear. The whole operation was estimated at twenty-two minutes from breach to first launch.

"Kester," Davour said. "You won't fire."

"No."

"Good. You're not trained. Stay with the transport. Help Yenna's people load when they arrive. Don't approach the bays until I clear you on the comm."

"Understood."

"Ivo." Davour turned to him. "You're with Kester. Help with the loading. If anything goes wrong on our side, you and Kester get Yenna's group out on the civilian shuttle and you abandon the military shuttle. Their flight matters more than ours. Clear?"

"Clear," Ivo said.

Sable was checking her sidearm.

"Twenty-two minutes," she said. "Let's go."

---

It took nineteen.

Kester sat in the rear cabin of the tracked transport with Ivo and listened to the operation unfold over the suit comm. Davour's voice was flat and procedural — *west hatch breached, no contact* — *control room cleared, two down* — *security station east cleared, one down* — *crew quarters cleared, four down, all sleeping when we arrived* — and Sable's voice was less procedural, lower, the voice of a woman who was performing competently without quite being present in what she was performing. Resh's voice did not appear on the comm at all. Resh, Kester realized at some point during the operation, had been issued a sidearm but had not been given firing instructions. Resh was on the operation as a body, not as a fighter. Sable wanted him close. She did not want him to kill anyone.

When Davour's voice came over the comm at minute nineteen — *interior secured, civilian shuttle inbound from Yenna's transport, we are clear for loading* — Kester realized that he had been holding his breath in irregular, shallow patterns for nearly twenty minutes, and that his neck and shoulders ached as if he had been lifting something.

He had not been lifting anything.

He had been listening to his friends kill seven strangers, and his body had responded.

He exhaled.

He climbed out of the rear cabin.

The base was quiet. Pier Three's hangars opened to the surface above through enormous mag-sealed doors that rolled back on tracks when the launch sequence initiated; for now, the doors were closed, and the surface was cold and dim under Moloch's red light, and the bodies of the perimeter guards were small dark shapes on the concrete.

Davour came out of the western hatch with Sable and Resh. Sable's helmet was tucked under one arm. Her face was the same face it had been all morning — soft, contained, present. She had killed two people in the last nineteen minutes. Her face did not register this in any way Kester could read.

Yenna, Chayne, and Pira arrived in their own tracked transport ten minutes later. The civilian shuttle was already on its taxi rail. Davour walked Yenna through the manifest — *you are listed as Hegemony Education Authority returning from a corrections-system inspection, you will not be questioned at Andra Prime customs because the manifest is signed by an officer who does not exist but whose seal we have replicated, the shuttle's transponder will read clean for the duration of the transit* — and Yenna nodded and absorbed the information and asked exactly two clarifying questions, both of them sharp.

Pira held Chayne's hand at the foot of the loading ramp.

She was looking around the hangar with the wide quiet eyes of a four-year-old who had never seen a hangar before. The space was vast. The shuttle was huge. The mag-sealed doors above them were larger than any structure she had encountered. She held Chayne's hand and she looked, and she did not speak.

Sable did not approach her.

This was, Kester realized later, deliberate. Sable had said her goodbye at the chamber. Approaching Pira here would be walking it back. Sable was going to let the girl board the shuttle without a second goodbye, because a second goodbye would be for Sable, not for Pira, and Sable had decided several hours ago that nothing on the Harvester run would be done for herself.

Pira saw Sable across the hangar. Pira raised one small hand, briefly, in something that was not quite a wave. Sable raised her own hand, equally briefly. The moment was perhaps three seconds long.

Then Chayne and Pira and Yenna walked up the ramp.

The shuttle's hatch sealed.

The mag-sealed hangar doors above them began to roll back.

The civilian shuttle launched at oh-seven-fifty-two, eight minutes ahead of schedule, into the Tartaros morning sky.

It was tracked, because all shuttles were tracked, but it was tracked under a civilian transit code that had been laundered through three Hegemony bureaucratic layers and that would not produce an alert flag in the Federation customs system on Andra Prime. Yenna had eighteen hours before the Hegemony pieced together what had happened at Pier Three. By the time the alarm went up, she would be on the surface.

Kester watched the shuttle clear the doors and rise into the brown-red haze of the upper atmosphere. He watched it until it was a small bright point against the sky, and then he watched the sky where the point had been until even the afterimage faded.

He thought: *That is my daughter going home in a stranger's arms.*

He did not know what he meant by it. He thought it anyway. He stood in the cold of the hangar and watched the empty sky and let the thought sit in him, and after a moment he understood that what he meant by it was that some part of him had been hoping, perhaps without admitting it, that Yenna's group would somehow find Lira on Andra Prime — that Yenna would knock on a door and his daughter would be on the other side of it, and that the archive would reach her before the news did, and that she would understand what he had done and why he was at the Harvester and that she would not have to assemble it from a press release.

He understood, watching the empty sky, that this was not going to happen.

Lira would learn the truth from the news, like everyone else. He had given up the right to be the one who told her, eleven years ago when he took the Tartaros posting, and that right was not something he could now take back by sending an academic stranger to her door. The archive would reach the Federation press through whatever channels Yenna could open. Lira would read about her father in a newspaper. There would be no version of this in which she heard it from him first.

He had known this for hours.

He had only just allowed himself to feel it.

He turned away from the hangar doors.

He walked back to the military shuttle.

---

The military shuttle in Bay Two was a Class III Hegemony transport — a long-haul vessel, designed for fourteen-hour transits, with sleeping berths for eight and a small cabin for the flight crew. Davour took the pilot's seat. Sable sat beside him in the co-pilot's chair. Kester, Resh, and Ivo took the rear cabin.

The launch was uneventful. The authorization codes Orrel had provided were valid. The shuttle's transponder identified it as a Hegemony Internal Security transport on a manifest that Davour did not read aloud and that Kester chose not to ask about. The mag-sealed doors above Bay Two rolled back. The shuttle rose. Tartaros fell away beneath them, the great red eye of Moloch filled the upper viewport for several long minutes, and then the moon's gravity released them and they were in transit.

The Harvester was fourteen hours away.

The shuttle was very quiet.

---

Ivo went to sleep almost immediately.

This was, Kester thought at the time, sensible. Ivo had been on his feet for thirty-six hours. He had climbed shafts, planned operations, retrieved a drop, helped pack the chamber, and ridden across the Vorrath Range in a tracked transport. The man was older than the rest of them. He needed sleep. He took one of the rear berths, lay down in his suit liner with the helmet beside him on the floor, and was breathing slowly within three minutes.

Resh sat in the cabin's small fold-down seat and stared at the bulkhead.

Kester sat opposite him and tried, for a while, to do nothing. He had the photograph drives in a sealed pouch under his suit liner — Sable had insisted, before they left the chamber, that Kester carry a backup in case Yenna's group did not make it. The pouch was warm against his ribs. He could feel the slight angles of the drives through the fabric.

After perhaps an hour, Davour spoke from the cockpit, over the cabin comm: *Yenna's group is clear of Tartaros space. Their transit is nominal. We have a window.*

Kester sat up.

"What window."

"Comm window. The shuttle's long-range relay is up. We can talk to Yenna's shuttle on a tight beam if we do it quickly. They'll have a window of their own when they reach Andra Prime's near-orbit, but they won't be able to reach us once they enter the atmosphere. If you want to say anything to Chayne or Yenna, now is the time."

Sable said, from the co-pilot's seat: *Open it.*

The comm clicked.

Yenna's voice came through after a moment. "We're here. Chayne is here. Pira is asleep."

"Yenna," Sable said. "How is the transit."

"Clean. The transponder is reading exactly the way Davour said it would. We have not been challenged. Pira ate a small meal an hour ago and went down without protest. She has been very calm, Sable. She has not asked for you. I want you to know that, because I think you would want to know."

There was a pause from the co-pilot's seat.

Then Sable said: *Thank you, Yenna.*

"Chayne is here. He wants to speak."

Chayne's voice, when it came, was thin but steady. "Sable. Kester. I have been working on the archive during the transit. I have been cross-referencing the Board principals against the documents I had on the original drives. There is something I want to tell you both."

"Go ahead, Chayne," Sable said.

"There is a name that I had in my archive that I could never place. It appears in eleven separate documents over a span of six years, always in technical contexts — equipment authorization, personnel travel, medical authorization. The name is *Volner*. Doctor Henna Volner. I had never been able to find a public Hegemony identity to match it. The name was not one of the Board principals, as far as I could see, and it was not Vorlith or any of her known associates. I thought it was perhaps a clerical error. I thought it was perhaps a name that had been used as a placeholder in some early documentation system."

"And?"

"And in Yenna's palace papers, the name appears once. In a memo dated 2364. The memo is a personnel transfer authorization. It moves Doctor Henna Volner from Site Four — wherever Site Four is — to Tartaros, on a temporary rotation, for the purpose of *general medical support and asset cultivation*."

Kester's hand moved, very slightly, toward his pouch.

He stopped the motion.

He looked across the cabin at Ivo, who was still asleep, his breathing slow and even, his face turned slightly toward the bulkhead in the dim cabin light.

"*Asset cultivation*," Sable said, slowly, from the co-pilot's seat.

"Yes," Chayne said. "It is one of the keywords. I cross-referenced it. It appears, in Yenna's papers, in three other places. In each instance, it refers to the recruitment of an internal asset — someone inside a target population, selected for compromise, cultivated for information. The keyword is specific. It is not a general medical term. It is a recruitment term."

"Doctor Henna Volner," Sable said. "When was she on Tartaros."

"From 2364 to 2370, on rotation. She would come for stays of two to three weeks at a time, several times a year. She used the citadel medical wing for her rotations. Her name appears on the wing's duty rosters during her presence."

Sable was quiet for a long moment.

Behind Kester, in the rear cabin, Pira's small voice came through the comm.

She had not been asleep, after all. She had been listening, the way children listen when adults are speaking words they do not understand, the way a child stays awake when she has been told to sleep because something interesting is happening in the next room. Her voice, when it came, was bright and slightly muffled by what Kester guessed was a blanket pulled up to her chin, and it said:

"Volner. That's the doctor's name. Ivo says it sometimes."

The cabin went very still.

Kester did not move. He did not look at Ivo. He did not look at the comm panel. He sat in his fold-down seat with his hands on his knees and his eyes on the small porthole opposite him and he listened, with the kind of administrative clarity he had been trained for over nine years, to the silence that followed Pira's sentence.

In the cockpit, Davour said softly, on a closed channel that the rear cabin would not have heard had Kester not been wearing his suit comm in standby: *Sable.*

*Yes.*

*Did you hear what the child said.*

*I heard.*

*What do you want to do.*

A pause.

Then: *Kester.*

Kester pressed the activation rune on his suit comm. He spoke very quietly, on the same closed channel.

*Yes.*

*Did you hear.*

*Yes.*

*Are you sure.*

*Yes, Sable.*

Another pause.

Then: *Come to the cockpit, Kester. Resh stays in the rear cabin with Ivo. Tell Resh nothing. Tell him I need you up front. Walk normally.*

*Understood.*

He stood up. He stretched, casually, the way a man stretches who has been sitting too long. He turned to Resh and said, in the natural voice of a man making a small request: "Sable wants me up front. You're on watch back here. Don't wake Ivo, he needs the sleep."

Resh nodded. He did not speak.

Kester walked through the cabin toward the cockpit. He did not look at Ivo. He passed within three feet of the sleeping man's berth and he kept his eyes forward and his pace even and he reached the cockpit door and stepped through and closed it behind him.

In the cockpit Sable was looking at him.

Her face was no longer soft.

It was, Kester realized with a cold lurch of something close to relief, the face he had been waiting to see all morning. The face of a woman who had finally been given the thing she could move on. The softness — the dangerous gentleness that had frightened him since the morning of chapter seven — had not disappeared, but it had been *located*. It had a target. It had a name.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her.

He told her about Ivo's eight-second adjustment to the maintenance panel at the drop. He told her about how Ivo had known the location of the drop point in a way Kester had not at the time questioned. He told her about how the launch base's vulnerabilities had matched Orrel's letter exactly, and about how he had registered this as suspicious without yet knowing the shape of the suspicion. He told her about the small things — Ivo's habits with the chamber's access panels, Ivo's depth of knowledge about the citadel's systems, Ivo's particular history of having been a prisoner before the Undersiders rather than coming up from the chamber population, which was the standard recruitment path for the under-levels' technical staff.

"I did not see it," he said, finishing. "I should have seen it. I did not."

"Kester," Sable said.

"Yes."

"You filed paperwork for nine years and did not see what was happening in Wing Seven. Forgive yourself for missing one man over three days. We do not have the time."

He was silent.

"What is on the comm," Sable said to Davour.

"Yenna and Chayne are still there. They heard Pira's sentence."

"Patch us back."

The comm clicked.

"Yenna," Sable said. "Chayne. Are you still there."

"We are here."

"You heard."

"We heard."

"Volner is Ivo's recruiter. He has been a Board asset for at least seven years, possibly longer. The chamber attack was timed to his information. The launch base vulnerabilities were laid for us through him. The drop was retrieved with him as the second hand. Everything we have done since the broadcast has had his thumbprint."

Yenna made a small sound. She did not weep. The sound was the sound of a woman registering a fact.

Chayne said: "Sable. The four uncounted children."

"Yes."

"The Board's surveillance was forty-three. Ours was forty-seven. The discrepancy was not an information failure on the Board's part. It was Ivo's hedging. He did not give them the full count. He did not give them the locations of the four he chose to protect."

"He hedged," Sable said.

"He hedged," Chayne said. "I want you to know that, before you do whatever you are going to do. He was a traitor. He was also a man who held back. The four children we still have, the ones who are with Sten now — they are alive because Ivo did not give them up."

Sable was quiet for a long time.

"Thank you, Chayne. For knowing that. For telling me before I did this."

"I thought you should know."

"Yenna," Sable said. "Land your shuttle. Do your work. Be careful. We will see you when we see you."

"Sable — "

"Land the shuttle, Yenna. Go to Andra Prime. Get the archive home. Do not think about us until it is done. Do you understand."

There was a pause.

"I understand," Yenna said.

"Thank you. Goodbye."

"Goodbye, Sable."

The comm closed.

Sable sat for a moment in the co-pilot's chair.

Then she turned to Kester.

"Wake him," she said. "Bring him to the cockpit. Resh stays in the rear cabin and does not interfere. Tell Resh exactly that, and tell him exactly nothing else. I do not want Ivo making decisions in the rear with the boy alone. Bring him up here."

Kester went back through the cabin door.

He did what she asked.

---

Ivo did not fight when Kester woke him.

Kester had been preparing himself, on the short walk back through the cabin, for a struggle. He had been preparing himself to grab Ivo's wrists, to press him down on the berth, to call for Davour. He had been preparing himself for violence, in the small cold administrative way he prepared himself for things. None of it was necessary.

Ivo opened his eyes. He looked at Kester. He looked at Kester's face. He read whatever was on it.

He said, very quietly: "How."

"Pira."

Ivo closed his eyes for a moment.

"The child," he said.

"Yes."

"Yes. All right."

He sat up. He did not reach for his sidearm. He did not move quickly. He moved like a man who had been expecting this conversation for a long time and who had decided, at some point years ago, that when it came he would not make it harder than it had to be. He stood up. He looked at Resh, who had stood up at the cabin's far end and who was holding his sidearm now and who looked very young and very uncertain.

"Sit down, Resh," Ivo said gently. "I am not going to do anything. Sit down. This is not yours."

Resh sat down.

Ivo walked toward the cockpit. He did not look back. Kester walked behind him.

In the cockpit Sable was waiting in the co-pilot's chair, turned to face the cockpit door. Davour was at the controls, half-turned, watching. The cockpit was small and the four of them filled it, and the door behind them was closed, and the porthole showed the long dark of transit and the small white smear of a star that was not the Hegemony's home sun and that Kester did not know the name of.

"Sit," Sable said.

Ivo sat in the small jump seat behind the pilot's chair.

Sable looked at him.

She did not speak immediately. She was, Kester realized, *studying* him, the way a person studies a face they think they know and are now seeing for the first time. Her own face was no longer soft. Her own face had in it, finally, the thing Kester had been waiting to see — anger. Pure anger, clean and undiluted, without the dangerous gentleness she had been carrying since the morning of Aleya's death.

"Talk," she said.

Ivo talked.

He talked for perhaps eleven minutes. He did not embellish. He did not justify. He told them about his prison sentence — fourteen years for industrial sabotage, a charge that had been technically accurate, a strike action gone wrong on Andra Prime that had killed three workers in a refinery accident and that had been prosecuted not as labor activism but as terrorism. He told them about his wife, Maela, and his son, Theron, who had been six when he was sentenced. He told them about the medical issue that had brought him to the citadel in 2364 — a kidney condition, treatable, but requiring equipment the prison's general infirmary did not have. He told them about Doctor Volner. He told them what she had said about Maela and Theron. He told them about the photographs she had shown him. He told them how she had let him hold the photographs for several minutes before taking them back.

"I did not say yes," he said. "Not that day. I did not say yes for many weeks. I told myself I would refuse. Then she came back during my next medical visit, and I did not refuse, and she explained what would be expected of me, and she sent me back to general population, and a month later I was transferred to a labor rotation where the Undersiders found me. I did not know they would find me. I learned later that the transfer had been arranged. I had been delivered to them. I joined them. I worked. I told myself, for many years, that I was hedging — that I gave Volner enough to keep the home alive but not enough to compromise the Undersiders. I told myself this. I do not know if it was true. I think parts of it were true. I think parts of it were what I needed to think to keep doing the work. The two are not always the same."

Sable was listening without expression.

"I did not see Volner after 2370," Ivo said. "She was rotated out. My handler became a procedure. Dead drops. Maintenance panels. I have not spoken to a person about my work for the Board in five years. The information has been flowing into a system, and the system has been keeping its end of the agreement, in that the allowance payments have continued and the boy has been at school and Maela has been — Maela has been told I died on Tartaros, ten years ago. She remarried. The boy is fourteen. He thinks his stepfather is his father. I have known these things for some time. I have not allowed myself to fully think about them. Thinking about them would have stopped me from being able to continue."

He paused.

"Two days ago I made an adjustment to a maintenance panel during the drop," he said. "I want to confirm this for you. The adjustment was a check-in. It told my handlers that the drop had been received and that the team was reading it. I do not know precisely what they did with the information. I assume they passed it to Orrel. I assume the launch base was thinned for us in part because of the check-in. I assume the timing of the chamber attack was informed by what I had previously reported about the expedition."

"Did you give them the cohort count," Sable said.

"I gave them forty-three."

"There were forty-seven."

"There were forty-seven. I gave them the four I would not give. The four I — " He stopped. He swallowed. "The four I told myself were my line. The line I would not cross. The line that meant I was not entirely what I was."

"Their names," Sable said.

Ivo gave her four names. They were, Kester noticed, the four youngest children Sable had been hiding. Two of them were the children Sten was now sheltering in the deeper passages.

"Anything else," Sable said.

"I gave them everything else, Sable. Everything. The chamber's layout. The shaft system. The cipher Chayne was working with — though that was one I gave reluctantly, because I did not understand at the time what they would do with it. I gave them the names of the Undersiders. I gave them assessments of the principal members. I gave them — I gave them a profile of you, Sable. Years of profile. Your habits, your weaknesses, the people you trusted, the people you did not. I gave them what I knew about Mouse. About Tev. About Pell, before he died — I gave them Pell's role in the broadcast plan, before the broadcast went out. They did not stop the broadcast because they did not want to stop it. They wanted it to happen. I helped them arrange for it to happen the way they wanted it to happen."

"Pell," Sable said.

"Yes."

Sable's hands, on her knees in the co-pilot's chair, did not move.

"All right," she said.

Ivo waited.

"Do you have anything to ask of me, Ivo."

Ivo looked at her.

"No," he said. "I am not going to ask you for anything. I do not deserve to ask you for anything. I will tell you what is true and you will do what you need to do, and that is the entire transaction. I am sorry, Sable. I am sorry, Kester. I am sorry."

"You have not said *Tev*," Sable said.

Ivo closed his eyes.

"Tev," he said. "I am sorry, Tev."

Sable nodded, slowly.

She turned to Davour.

"How long until the Harvester."

"Six hours, fourteen minutes."

"How long until atmospheric entry."

"Five hours, forty-eight minutes, give or take. The descent profile is a long approach — they want shuttles down at low altitude for a stretch before the receiving bay so the customs sweep has time to read the manifest. We will be in atmosphere for about ten minutes before we land."

"All right." Sable stood up. She did this slowly, with the deliberation of a woman who had been making a decision throughout Ivo's talk and who was now ready to execute it. "We do not throw him out into space. We do not waste a body inside the shuttle. We do not give the Hegemony a corpse to find at the bay."

She turned to Ivo.

"You go off in atmosphere. Once we are low and the air is thick enough that opening the hatch will not vacuum the cabin, we will open the hatch. You will be at the hatch. You will jump."

Ivo did not respond immediately.

"That is the kindness," Sable said. "It is the only one I have. If you jump, you choose the angle. You roll when you hit. You give yourself a chance. If you do not jump, I will shoot you in the leg and I will kick you out. The leg is what you get either way. The choice is whether the leg is the only injury or whether you are bleeding when you go off the platform. Do you understand."

"I understand."

"Good."

Sable looked at Davour.

"Find a window. Somewhere remote. The night side if we cross it, otherwise a stretch of terrain without facility traffic. I want him on the ground at low altitude with no surveillance witnessing the drop. We take the leg in atmosphere, drop him, close the hatch, continue to the bay. The shuttle's transponder reads clean for the whole operation. The customs station does not see anything."

"Copy. I'll route us through the southern continent on the inbound. There's a stretch of grassland that doesn't have any monitoring relays for about four hundred kilometers. We can take him low over the middle of it."

"Good."

Sable turned back to Ivo.

"Sit in the rear cabin. Do not speak to Resh. Do not approach the controls. Kester will sit with you. We will tie your hands when we begin the descent so that there is no ambiguity at the hatch. Do you have any final thing to say."

Ivo looked at her.

"No," he said. "Nothing that will help."

"All right."

He stood up. He walked back through the cockpit door, into the rear cabin, with the steady careful walk of a man who had not yet been killed and who did not know how to feel about the fact.

Kester followed him.

In the rear cabin, Resh was sitting in his fold-down seat with his sidearm in his lap. He looked up at Kester, and at Ivo behind Kester, and his face did not change.

"What's happening," Resh said quietly.

"Ivo is going to sit," Kester said. "He is going to sit until we begin the descent. I am going to sit with him. You can sleep, Resh. I will keep watch."

"Why does he need to sit."

"Resh."

"Why does he need to sit, Kester."

Kester looked at the boy. Resh was eighteen. He had been silent for three days. He was holding a sidearm that he had not yet been authorized to fire.

"He fed information to the Board," Kester said. "For years. The chamber attack was because of him. Pell was because of him. Tev was because of him. Sable will explain the rest when she is ready. For now he sits. We do not hurt him. We do not yell. We sit."

Resh looked at Ivo for a long time.

His face did not change.

After a moment he set his sidearm down on the small fold-out table beside his seat and he turned his face to the wall and he did not look at Ivo again for the rest of the transit.

Kester sat across from Ivo in the rear cabin.

The cabin was very quiet.

After a long time — perhaps an hour, perhaps two — Ivo said, in a voice so quiet Kester almost did not hear it:

"I am sorry, Mr. Vaile. For what it is worth. I am sorry. I liked you. I liked all of you. I want you to know that. The liking was real. The other thing was also real. Both were real. I do not know how to explain it better than that."

Kester thought about it.

He thought about Tev, and Aleya, and Pell, and the eight unnamed dead in the chamber. He thought about Pira's small bright voice on the comm, saying *Volner. That's the doctor's name. Ivo says it sometimes.* He thought about Mouse, six hours ahead of them in another shuttle, sedated, on a transport that the Board had arranged.

He thought about the man across from him.

He said: "I believe you."

Ivo closed his eyes.

"Thank you," he said.

He did not say anything else for hours.

---

The Harvester planet was visible through the cockpit porthole at hour eleven of the transit.

Kester saw it briefly, when he went forward to deliver Davour a ration pack. The planet was small and green-blue from this distance — *terraformed*, Davour said, *or at least atmospherically processed, the surface readings are within human-tolerable range*. The system around it had two suns, both of them small and yellow, neither of them on Hegemony charts. The planet was the only inhabited body in the system. There were three orbital stations, none of which appeared to be transmitting on civilian frequencies. The Harvester's traffic was all encrypted or silent.

The shuttle's transponder, broadcasting under the codes Orrel had provided, was met by a customs hail at hour twelve. Davour responded with the manifest. The customs station accepted it without query. *Welcome to the facility, Hegemony Internal Security transport,* the customs voice said, in a flat synthesized tone. *Proceed to receiving bay seven on the southern approach. Please ensure all weapons are secured for offload review.*

Davour acknowledged.

The shuttle began its descent.

The descent profile, as Davour had described, was a long one. The shuttle would skim the upper atmosphere for several minutes, drop through the lower thermosphere over the planet's southern continent, and then run a low-altitude approach across roughly six hundred kilometers of terrain before reaching the receiving bay's pad. The whole approach was a designed-in vulnerability of Hegemony shuttle traffic — the sustained low-altitude segment was a customs measure, intended to give surface monitoring time to verify the manifest before the shuttle reached a sensitive facility — and it was the segment Sable was going to use.

Davour brought them in.

The first eight minutes of the descent were unremarkable. The shuttle bucked gently as it crossed the troposphere boundary; the porthole filled with the green-tinged orange of atmospheric entry; the cabin's deck heated through the suit liners and then cooled again as the shuttle's thermal systems caught up. The porthole cleared. The shuttle was below the cloud layer.

Below them, the southern continent was a vast unbroken plain — long grass, low scrub, the pale yellow of a biome Kester did not recognize, lit by the late afternoon angle of the system's smaller sun. The terrain was empty. No facilities. No roads. No relays. Davour, in the cockpit, said: *Coming up on the window. Five minutes.*

Sable came through the cockpit door.

She looked at Ivo. Ivo was sitting in the same seat he had been sitting in for ten hours. His face was very tired. His hands were resting on his knees.

"Stand up," Sable said.

Ivo stood up.

"Hands."

He held them out. Davour, who had come back through the cockpit door behind Sable, produced a length of suit-liner cord and tied his wrists in front of him with the precise economical knots of a woman who had been trained to restrain prisoners and who had not forgotten the training. The cord was cinched tight. Ivo did not flinch.

"Walk to the hatch," Sable said.

Ivo walked.

The hatch was on the rear cabin's port side — a standard atmospheric egress hatch, sealed with a manual override that could be opened in atmosphere without venting the cabin once the pressure differential equalized. Davour cycled the equalization. The hatch's status indicator went from red to amber. The shuttle's altitude was approximately four hundred meters above the grassland — low, but not low enough that a fall would be survivable without a roll. Sable had given Ivo a chance, but the chance was narrow.

The shuttle's airspeed was the larger problem. They were moving at perhaps four hundred kilometers per hour. Hitting the ground at that speed, even with a perfect roll, would be catastrophic.

Davour, at the cockpit door, said: *Reducing airspeed. Coming down to one-fifty. We have ninety seconds before the customs sweep gets curious about the speed deviation. Make it count.*

The shuttle's deceleration registered as a brief lurch. Kester braced himself against the bulkhead. Ivo did not brace. Ivo stood at the hatch with his hands tied and his eyes closed.

Sable was beside him.

"Open it," she said to Davour.

The hatch opened.

The atmosphere of the Harvester planet rushed in — warm air, smelling faintly of grass and ozone, the particular air of a world that had been terraformed and had grown into the terraforming. The cabin filled with the noise of the shuttle's passage. The grassland scrolled past below them, a green-yellow blur four hundred meters down, slowing as Davour bled off speed.

"Ivo," Sable said.

He opened his eyes.

"Jump."

Ivo looked at her. He looked past her, at the open hatch. He looked at the grassland. He looked at the distance.

He hesitated.

It was, Kester thought afterward, perhaps a full second. Maybe a second and a half. Long enough that another person in another mood might have given him another moment, might have understood the hesitation as the natural response of a man who had been given a kindness and was trying to use it. Sable was not in another mood. Sable had been preparing herself for the hesitation since the moment she made the offer. She had decided, in advance, that the hesitation would cost him.

She raised her sidearm and shot him in the right thigh.

The shot was loud in the cabin. Ivo grunted — the same small involuntary sound he might have made on the platform in a different version of this morning — and his leg buckled. He did not fall. He sagged against the hatch frame, his bound hands coming up reflexively, his mouth opening on something that was perhaps the beginning of a word and that did not finish becoming one.

Sable kicked him.

The kick was professional and economical, the side of her boot against the small of his back, and Ivo went through the hatch sideways and down, with his hands tied and his leg bleeding and the wind taking his face away from Kester's view in less than a heartbeat. Kester saw him for one moment as a small twisting shape against the green-yellow blur of the grassland below, and then Ivo was gone, and the cabin was full of the noise of the wind, and Sable was already turning back.

"Close it," she said.

Davour cycled the hatch.

The hatch sealed.

The cabin's noise dropped, abruptly, to the ordinary hum of the shuttle's atmospheric flight. The pressure stabilized. The smell of the grassland faded.

Davour, at the cockpit door, said: *Bringing us back up to nominal speed. Customs sweep is not flagging the deviation. We are clean. Receiving bay in twelve minutes.*

Sable walked back to the cockpit without speaking.

Kester stood by the closed hatch and looked at the small smear of blood on the deck where Ivo had sagged against the frame. The smear was not large. The shot had been a clean one. Most of the blood had gone with the body, into the four-hundred-meter fall, into the grassland.

He thought about the grassland.

He thought about a man with bound hands and a wounded leg falling four hundred meters and hitting tall grass. He thought about whether the grass would be tall enough to slow the impact. He thought about whether four hundred meters and a hundred-fifty kilometers per hour and a wounded leg and bound hands could produce a survivor.

He understood that the answer was almost certainly no.

He understood that *almost certainly* was the gift Sable had given Ivo, and that the gift was small but not nothing, and that the fact that Sable had bothered to give it at all was a piece of who she still was that he would have to remember when the rest of her had become harder to see.

He turned away from the hatch.

He went back to his fold-down seat.

Resh was looking at him.

"Did he jump," Resh said quietly.

"He hesitated," Kester said. "Sable shot him and kicked him. He went out the hatch."

Resh nodded, slowly.

"Was he alive when he went out."

"Yes."

"Will he survive."

"No."

Resh was quiet for a long moment.

Then he said: "Good."

Kester looked at him. Resh was eighteen. He had been silent for three days. He had just said *good* about a man Kester had liked, who had held doors for him, who had taught him how to read a maintenance shaft, who had been a member of the resistance for fifteen years and a Board asset for at least seven.

Kester did not correct him.

He sat in his fold-down seat and he closed his eyes and he understood, with the small administrative clarity that was now more or less the only mode of thought he had access to, that the resistance was no longer a community. It was a small group of people on a shuttle, bound for a meeting with a man who had been preparing them, and they were each of them carrying their own version of what the last week had cost, and the versions did not match. Sable was carrying one thing. Davour was carrying another. Resh was carrying a third. He himself was carrying a fourth, which was the weight of a man whose paperwork had built the room they were now flying toward and whose silence at an open hatch had just helped close another transaction.

The shuttle continued its descent.

---

The shuttle landed at receiving bay seven at hour fourteen, eleven minutes — a few minutes behind schedule, on a strip of polished landing pad in a hangar that opened to a green sky and that contained, Kester saw through the porthole, the silhouettes of perhaps twenty other shuttles in adjacent bays.

The hangar was warm.

The shuttle's exterior hatch released.

Davour, in the cockpit, said quietly: *We are clean. Customs reads us as a standard arrival. There is a reception waiting at the door.*

Sable came back through the cockpit door. She had cleaned her sidearm during the final approach. The smear of blood by the hatch had been wiped down with a suit liner cloth. The cabin was, to a casual inspection, a normal arriving Hegemony military shuttle with four passengers and no incidents.

She nodded to Kester.

Kester stood up.

The four of them — Sable, Kester, Davour, and Resh — moved to the exterior hatch. Davour cycled it. The hatch opened onto the small extruded landing platform, and beyond the platform, the hangar's polished floor, and beyond the floor, the figure waiting at the hangar's interior door.

The figure was small and wore a neat uniform Kester did not recognize. The figure raised one hand in greeting and walked toward them, and as the figure approached Kester saw that it was a young woman — perhaps twenty-five, perhaps younger — with a face that was calm and professional and that bore no insignia identifying her institution.

She stopped a respectful distance from the foot of the loading ramp, which had already extended.

She bowed, very slightly, in the formal Hegemony manner.

"Mr. Vaile," she said. "Welcome to the Harvester. I am Aide Veneya. The Warden has been expecting you. He apologizes for not meeting you personally; he is currently in a session with a subject and will join you in the residence wing within the hour. If you will follow me, I will escort you to your quarters and arrange refreshments. The journey has been long. We have prepared rooms."

She turned. She began to walk, with the easy professional pace of a person who had escorted many guests through this hangar and who expected, without question, that her guests would follow.

Sable looked at Kester.

Kester looked at Sable.

Behind them, four hundred kilometers and several hours back along the descent path, in a stretch of grassland that did not appear on any monitoring relay's map, the body of a man with bound hands was lying somewhere in the tall yellow grass, and nothing in the universe was attending to it, and nothing was going to.

They walked down the loading ramp.

They followed Aide Veneya through the hangar's interior door.

The door was already opening, and the residence wing was already waiting.
