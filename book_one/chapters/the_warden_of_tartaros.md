# The Warden of Tartaros

The tram car smelled like rust and old blood, and Kester Vaile was handcuffed to the bench across from a man he used to call sir.

"You're quiet, Kester."

Warden Bastien Orrel sat with his legs crossed, one polished boot swinging lazily in the low gravity. He was fifty-eight, silver-haired, and wore the black dress uniform of the Hegemony Corrections Authority like a priest wore his vestments — with reverence. Behind him, through the tram's reinforced window, the surface of Tartaros scrolled past: a rust-red moon pockmarked with mining pits and the grey concrete scars of Facility Nine. Somewhere up above, past the haze, the gas giant Moloch filled half the sky like a bruised eye.

"I've got nothing to say to you," Kester said.

"You had plenty to say three days ago. To the Federation inspector. On an encrypted channel. Which I owned." Orrel smiled. It was a warm smile, the kind he used at the annual officers' dinner when he handed out commendations. "I was disappointed, Kester. I thought we were family."

"You run a death camp."

"I run a *correctional facility*." The smile didn't move. "The distinction matters."

Kester looked away. He'd been Orrel's right hand for nine years. Logistics officer. The man who signed off on the ore quotas, who rerouted the oxygen rations, who filed the incident reports that said *prisoner expired due to pre-existing condition* when the truth was the prisoner had been worked to death in a sulfur vein with a cracked suit. He'd told himself, for nine years, that he was keeping it humane. That without him it would be worse.

Then the Federation inspector had come. A woman named Adeyemi, with tired eyes and a briefcase full of subpoenas, and she'd asked him one question in the commissary that he couldn't stop hearing:

*Do you sleep, Mr. Vaile?*

He didn't. He hadn't in months.

So he'd talked. He'd given her everything — the off-books prisoners, the medical trials, the children of political dissidents listed as *administrative wards*. And now here he was, in a tram car bound for Sub-Level Four, with the warden himself as his escort.

"I'm going to offer you something," Orrel said. "Because I loved you like a son. Are you listening?"

Kester said nothing.

"I'm going to let you walk out of this tram. I'm going to give you a suit, a sidearm, and a twelve-hour head start. You run. You hide. You find one of the old survey shelters in the Ridge, you live out your days eating reclaimed protein and drinking your own piss. And I never see you again."

"And the catch."

"You tell me where the inspector's data core is." Orrel leaned forward. "That's it. One location. One sentence. And this — " he gestured at the cuffs, the tram, the descending red planet below, " — all goes away."

Kester studied him. Nine years. He knew every tell the man had. The slight tightening at the jaw when he lied. The way his left hand drifted toward his cuff when he was about to have someone killed.

His left hand was resting on his cuff.

"Bastien," Kester said quietly. "You were going to kill me either way."

The smile finally moved. It widened.

"Well," Orrel said. "Yes."

---

The tram hit the charge.

Kester had no idea who planted it — later he'd learn it was a maintenance tech named Ivo who'd been funneling scrap to the Undersiders for two years — but the explosion peeled the tram car open like a ration tin, and suddenly there was red dust and screaming atmosphere and Orrel's guards tumbling into the thin cold air.

Kester's cuffs were magnetic. They released when the tram's power died.

He didn't think. He grabbed a guard's emergency mask, clamped it over his face, and ran.

Behind him, through the dust and the sirens and the high thin wail of decompression, he heard Orrel laughing. Actually laughing. Delighted.

"RUN, KESTER!" the warden bellowed, somewhere in the red haze. "RUN, YOU BEAUTIFUL TRAITOR! OH, WE'RE GOING TO HAVE *FUN* NOW!"

---

The Undersiders found him first.

He woke up in a converted ore-processing chamber three levels below the official map of Facility Nine, with a woman pointing a rivet gun at his head. She was maybe thirty, shaved scalp tattooed with prisoner serials that weren't hers — she wore the dead's numbers like armor — and she did not look friendly.

"You're Vaile."

"Yes."

"Logistics officer Vaile. The one who signed the Wing Seven transfers."

Kester swallowed. Wing Seven had been the medical trial block. Forty-two prisoners. None of them had come back.

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she lowered the rivet gun, and said, conversationally: "My brother was in Wing Seven."

"I know," Kester said. "Tomas Reyek. Prisoner 8841-C. He was a welder. He had a daughter named Mira on Sephara. He made it forty-one days."

Her face didn't change. But something behind her eyes did.

"How do you know that."

"Because I read every file," Kester said. "Every single one. I told myself if I remembered them, it meant something. I told myself a lot of things."

She stared at him. The rivet gun didn't come back up. But it didn't go down either.

"My name is Sable," she said finally. "And I don't forgive you. I want that clear."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Good." She holstered the gun. "Because we don't need your forgiveness. We need your access codes."

---

There's a thing that happens on prison moons, when the regime gets too comfortable. The mines go deeper than the maps. The prisoners outnumber the guards ten to one. And somewhere, in the dark arteries of the facility, a second society grows — not free, exactly, but unadministered. The Undersiders had been building theirs for a decade. Smuggling oxygen. Hiding children born to prisoners the regime had forgotten to sterilize. Stockpiling weapons made from mining equipment and salvaged guard gear.

What they didn't have was a way into the warden's citadel.

What Kester had, burned into his memory from nine years of signing off on everything, was every code, every patrol rotation, every blind spot in the security grid.

"You understand what you're asking," he told Sable, on the third night, in a chamber lit by stolen emergency lamps. "If we take the citadel, we don't just kill Orrel. We trigger the dead-man protocols. The whole facility goes into lockdown. Every prisoner in every wing, sealed in. No oxygen rotation. No food. They'll suffocate in four days."

"We know."

"Then you also know Orrel has a secondary protocol. If the citadel falls and he can't trigger lockdown himself, the reactor vents. The whole moon becomes uninhabitable for a century."

"We know that too."

"So what's the plan."

Sable smiled for the first time since he'd met her. It wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of someone who had done the math a long time ago and come out the other side of it.

"The plan," she said, "is that we don't take the citadel."

She unrolled a schematic across the table. Not of the citadel. Of the comms array on the north pole — the one that broadcast the warden's weekly address to the Hegemony, the one piece of infrastructure Orrel *personally* controlled, the one thing he loved more than the prison itself.

"We take his voice," Sable said. "We put you on that broadcast. The logistics officer, the inside man, with the files, the names, the serials. Live. Uncut. Hegemony-wide."

"He'll kill every prisoner in the facility before he lets me finish."

"Probably."

"Sable — "

"Kester." She looked at him levelly. "There are sixty thousand of us in this facility. There are four million in the Hegemony's other facilities. If we die and they hear us die, maybe the other four million live. Maybe they don't. But right now nobody out there even knows we exist. Right now, in the official record, we already died years ago."

She tapped the schematic.

"You helped make the record. Now you help unmake it."

---

He thought about Orrel's offer, in the tram, for a long time after that.

*I'm going to let you walk out.*

He could have taken it. Could have given up the inspector's data core, run to the Ridge, lived on reclaimed protein and the thin cold comfort of being alive. Orrel would have killed him eventually, sure — but *eventually* was a long word, and Kester Vaile had made a career out of stretching it.

That was the offer, really. Not freedom. Just *eventually*.

He looked at Sable, at the tattooed serials on her scalp, at the schematic of the comms array, at the low red light of Tartaros pulsing through the vents like a dying heart.

"When do we move," he said.

---

Orrel's voice came over the facility PA the next morning, warm as ever, calling Kester by name. Offering terms. Offering forgiveness. Offering the Ridge, the suit, the twelve-hour head start, *all of it still on the table, my boy, all of it still yours.*

Kester listened from a maintenance shaft beneath the comms array, with sixty pounds of improvised charges strapped to his back and a stolen guard's sidearm at his hip, and he thought: *You know what, Bastien. I think I'd rather not run.*

He started climbing.

Above him, through the grating, the red eye of Moloch watched everything.
