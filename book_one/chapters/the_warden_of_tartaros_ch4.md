# The Warden of Tartaros
## Chapter Four: The Things He Signed

The under-levels of Facility Nine were colder than the upper wings, and quieter, and they smelled of mineral water and old machine oil, and Kester Vaile sat on a folding chair beneath a hanging worklight and tried to remember every piece of paperwork he had signed in nine years.

It was not going well.

He had a stub of pencil and a stack of salvaged ration cards — the Undersiders' substitute for paper, harvested from decades of waste — and he was trying to build a list. Names. Dates. Document categories. Anything he could pull from memory that might mean something to Sable when she came back from wherever she had gone to be alone.

He had written *Wing Seven*. He had written *external consultants*. He had written *Calderon*, three times, in three different places on three different cards, as if writing it more would make him understand it more. It had not.

He had also written a name. *Dr. Sennae Vorlith.* He had stared at the name for a long time before writing it down, because he had not thought of it in seven years and he was not entirely sure he had not invented it. But the more he held it in his mind, the more solid it became. She had come to Tartaros twice a year for six years. She had stopped coming sometime around 2369. He had filed her travel vouchers personally. He had once shared an elevator with her and she had said *good morning, Mr. Vaile*, and her voice had been soft and academic and entirely unmemorable, and he had filed her vouchers for three years afterward without ever once asking what a Dr. Sennae Vorlith was a doctor *of*.

He wrote her name on the card.

He underlined it.

He did not yet know why.

---

Sable was somewhere in the chamber's east wing, doing whatever a person does when they have lost a brother and a friend and a girl they had raised in five years instead of a mother. Kester had not gone after her. He had understood, with the dim clarity of a man who had spent his life avoiding rooms where grief was being conducted, that there was nothing he could offer her except the absence of his face.

So he sat with the pencil and the ration cards and the worklight and tried to remember.

It came back the way memory always came back to him — sideways, in fragments, prompted by the wrong things. The smell of the under-levels' machine oil reminded him of a service report he had filed in 2367 about a malfunctioning autoclave in the medical wing. The autoclave had been running hot for months. The report had recommended replacement. Replacement had been denied. He had refiled the report three times before the request was approved, by which point the autoclave had been running hot for two years, and Kester had not asked why an autoclave running hot was acceptable for two years in a medical wing that handled human tissue.

Now, in the cold of the under-level, he asked.

*Because they didn't want it sterile,* his mind answered, in a voice that was perhaps his own and perhaps not. *Because whatever they were running through it, sterilization would have damaged.*

He wrote *autoclave 2367* on a ration card.

He underlined it.

---

Pell's body was still in the broadcast room.

This was a fact Kester could not stop returning to. The Hegemony would have collected it by now — would have photographed it, catalogued it, filed it under *insurgent killed in lawful response to terrorist action*, and incinerated it in one of the facility's ten thousand efficient procedures. Pell had a daughter on the home world. Kester knew this because Pell had mentioned her, once, in the maintenance crawl, and Kester had nodded and said nothing because he had not wanted to be the kind of man who knew Pell's daughter's name. The man had bought him forty seconds of broadcast with his life. The least Kester owed him was the discomfort of remembering that he had not asked.

He wrote *Pell — daughter — name?* on a card.

He set the card aside.

---

There were people in the chamber with him, though he had been ignoring them. Three Undersiders Sable had introduced him to before she walked away — a woman called Tev who had been a nurse before her arrest, a man called Roon who had been nothing in particular before his arrest and was now apparently the one who managed the chamber's air recyclers, and a thin elderly man called Chayne who Kester suspected had been a teacher of some kind in his previous life and who was watching Kester with an expression of mild, patient curiosity that Kester found unbearable.

"Mr. Vaile," Chayne said, eventually.

Kester looked up from his cards.

"You're trying to remember things."

"Yes."

"Would you like help."

Kester opened his mouth to say no, and then he stopped, and he thought about it.

"Yes," he said. "I would."

Chayne pulled a folding chair over and sat down. He moved like a man who was in pain and had been for a long time, and who had decided years ago not to mention it. He set his hands on his knees. His knuckles were swollen.

"I was a records officer," Chayne said. "Before. On Korya. I worked in municipal archives for thirty-one years."

"Why are you here."

"Because in 2358 I refused to redact a death certificate." Chayne smiled, very slightly. "It was a small thing. A boy had drowned in a canal. The cause of death was negligence — there was a broken safety rail, the city had been notified, the city had not repaired it. I was asked to change the cause of death to *misadventure*. I refused. I was given three opportunities to reconsider. On the third opportunity I was arrested."

"For refusing to redact a death certificate."

"For sedition, technically. The official record is more colorful than the truth usually is." Chayne folded his hands. "Tell me about the documents you signed."

Kester looked at him for a long moment.

Then he started talking.

---

It took him three hours.

He talked about the medical wing's autoclave. He talked about the consultants whose vouchers he filed. He talked about Wing Seven and the forty-two prisoners who went in and the zero who came out. He talked about Dr. Sennae Vorlith, who had said *good morning* in an elevator. He talked about a budget line item called *Substrate Maintenance* that had appeared in the wing's quarterly reports for nine years and that he had assumed meant something about food preparation and had never once verified.

He talked about Calderon. He told Chayne what little he knew — that Orrel had been there, that it had been a battle or a campaign or a meatgrinder, that sixteen thousand men had died there. That Orrel had said the word, once, in the corridor, when Mouse was walking back. *Calderon*. As if it explained something. As if it were a justification.

Chayne listened.

Chayne wrote nothing down. He simply listened, with his hands folded on his knees, and his expression did not change, and when Kester finally ran out of words Chayne sat in silence for a long time before speaking.

"Mr. Vaile," Chayne said. "I would like to tell you what I think you have just described. You may correct me if I am wrong."

"All right."

"You have described a research program. A long one. At least nine years, more likely longer — you came in partway through it, and the autoclave was already running hot before you arrived, which suggests the program predates your tenure. The research has had at least one named principal — your Dr. Vorlith — and probably others you didn't notice because they were better at being unnoticed. The program has consumed prisoners. Specifically, the program has consumed adult prisoners, in batches, with high mortality. You have signed paperwork that supported the program's logistics — supply routing, personnel travel, budget allocations under euphemistic line items — for nine years. You believed, for nine of those years, that the paperwork was administrative."

"Yes."

"It was not administrative. It was procurement."

Kester closed his eyes.

"And the word your warden used in the corridor," Chayne continued, gently, "*Calderon*. I do not know the word. But I know what it sounds like. It sounds like a place a man survived. It sounds like a place a man came back from with a particular set of conclusions about the value of human bodies in the operations of large institutions. I think your warden has been working, for a long time, on a project that he believes will repay his survival with interest. I think the prisoners in Wing Seven were not being killed. I think they were being *spent*."

"On what."

"On the question," Chayne said, "of how to make a body that the next Calderon cannot kill."

Kester sat very still.

"Weapons," he said, after a long time.

"Or something close to it. Soldiers. Tools. Augmented infantry. The terminology will be technical and the technology will be ugly, but the substance, I suspect, is that simple. The Hegemony has wars coming. Your warden is building the answer. Your prisoners are the iterations." Chayne paused. "I am sorry, Mr. Vaile. I think this is what you have been part of."

Kester did not say anything for a long time.

When he did, he said: "I want to be sure."

"I would not be sure on the strength of one conversation in a cold room. But you have the records. Or you can get to them. The Undersiders have been pulling administrative documents for years — most of them are useless to us, but if you know what you are looking for, the documents will tell you. Look for the consultants. Follow Dr. Vorlith's vouchers. She came twice a year for six years; she went somewhere on Tartaros and she went home again. Where did she go. Who did she see. What was her clearance. What was her department of origin." Chayne's hands were still folded. His eyes were very tired. "The answer to those questions will tell you what was in Wing Seven. And once you know what was in Wing Seven, you will know what your warden has been building."

"And then?"

"And then," Chayne said, "you will know what to do. Or you will know that there is nothing to do. Both are possible. Both are answers."

He stood up, slowly, with the care of a man whose joints had been negotiating with him for a decade.

"I will leave you to your cards, Mr. Vaile. If you would like my help again, you have only to ask. I am not going anywhere."

He walked away.

Kester sat with his pencil and his ration cards and his worklight and his understanding, fragile and provisional and almost certainly correct, of what he had spent nine years doing.

He did not weep. He had wept earlier, on the tram, when nobody could see him, and he had decided that further weeping was a luxury he could not yet afford. There was work to do. Chayne had told him what the work was. The work was to verify, and once he had verified, the work would be to expose — or to act, or to abandon, or whichever of the three turned out to be possible. The work was simple in shape and would be very hard in execution.

He picked up his pencil.

He wrote, on a fresh ration card, in capital letters: *VORLITH — DEPT OF ORIGIN.*

He underlined it.

He kept writing.

---

Sable came back to the chamber four hours later.

She looked, Kester thought, like a person who had walked a great distance through cold weather and was now indoors but not yet warm. She sat down across from him without speaking, and she looked at the small forest of ration cards on the table between them — names, dates, line items, underlines — and she said, quietly:

"What do you have."

Kester did not lie. He did not minimize. He did not soften.

He told her what Chayne had told him. He told her he believed it. He told her he was going to verify it, and then he was going to find Dr. Sennae Vorlith, and then he was going to figure out what came after.

Sable listened.

When he was finished, she said: "Soldiers."

"Yes."

"He's making soldiers out of the people he kills."

"Out of their bodies. Or — I don't know yet. Augmenting. Iterating. I don't know the technology. But yes. Something like that."

She nodded, slowly. She did not seem surprised. She seemed, in fact, like a person who had suspected something like this for a long time without permitting herself to name it.

"My brother," she said.

"Sable — "

"He was a welder. He had calloused hands. He had a back that hurt him in cold weather because he'd broken a vertebra in a fall when he was twenty-two. He was not — " Her voice did not break. She was not crying. But the words were coming out very carefully, like a person setting down glass. "He was not the kind of man you would build a soldier out of. He was forty-three. His body was already failing. Why him."

Kester thought about it.

"I don't know," he said. "But I think it matters that you asked. I think — Sable, I think when I figure out what the program is, I will also figure out why your brother. And I think the answer will not be what we expect."

Sable looked at him for a long time.

Then she said: "We need a next move."

"I know."

"The broadcast went out. The Hegemony is going to respond. We have maybe seventy-two hours before they hit the under-levels — they know we're down here, they've always known, they'll be moving units now. Mouse is in the citadel and she is — " Her voice caught, just for a moment, on the name. She kept going. "Mouse is alive. I have to believe that. Which means we have a window, and the window is closing, and we need to know what we are doing before it closes."

"What do you want to do."

"I want," Sable said, "to know what is in Wing Seven."

"That's where I want to start."

"Then we start there. Tonight. Chayne can help with the records. Tev knows the medical wing's old layout — she nursed there for two years before she was transferred. Roon can get us power and air for as deep as we need to go." Sable rubbed her face. "We have two objectives now, Kester. Mouse and the truth. If we can get one we can probably get both. If we can't get either we are going to die down here in the dark."

"Yes."

"You understand."

"I understand."

She nodded.

She stood up, slowly, the way Chayne had stood up — like a person whose body was now a tool she was using rather than a body she was inhabiting — and she walked to the chamber's far wall, where the broadcast deck was charging on a salvaged power coupler. She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she said, without turning around:

"Pell's daughter is named Iyani. She's eleven. She lives on the home world with his sister. He never told you because he didn't trust you yet."

Kester closed his eyes.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Just remember her name. That's the price of him."

"Iyani."

"Iyani."

He wrote it on a ration card.

He underlined it.

---

That night — though night was an abstraction in the under-levels, where the only light was electric and the only clock was the rotation of the air recyclers — Kester lay on a thin pallet in a converted storage chamber and could not sleep.

He kept thinking about Mouse.

Not about Mouse in the corridor, with her hand on the override and Orrel through the glass. He had stopped allowing himself to think about that, because thinking about it produced nothing but a kind of cold sick paralysis that helped no one. He was thinking, instead, about Mouse in the broadcast room. The way she had pointed her stolen rifle at the technician and told him, calmly, to sit down. The way she had moved through the maintenance crawl in the dark like she had been born underground, which, he realized now, she had been.

He was thinking about how she was nineteen.

He was thinking about how Bastien Orrel had said *sweetheart* in a voice that had not been the voice of a captor.

There was something in the shape of that, something he could not yet put words to, something that did not fit cleanly with what Chayne had said about adult prisoners and augmented infantry. Mouse was not an adult prisoner. Mouse had been *born* in the prison. Orrel had known her — *known her well*, known her by name, known her with a familiarity that a warden does not have for an ordinary inmate — for a long time. Years. Maybe her whole life.

*Sweetheart.*

There was a piece of the picture he had not yet placed. He could feel it, sitting somewhere in the back of his mind, the way you can feel the absence of a tooth with your tongue. It was small and it was wrong-shaped and it did not match anything else he had assembled.

He tried to make it fit Chayne's theory. *Augmented infantry. Weapons made of men.* Mouse was not a man. Mouse was a girl. Mouse was a girl who had been born in this prison and had grown up calm and competent and — 

He stopped.

He did not finish the thought.

He turned over on his pallet and faced the wall and told himself, with the discipline of a man who had spent nine years not finishing thoughts, that he would think about it tomorrow. Or next week. Or when the rest of the picture was clearer. There was no point chasing a single piece when the whole was still fragmentary. He would come back to it.

He almost believed himself.

Above him, six kilometers away in the citadel's medical wing, a nineteen-year-old girl was being prepared for something he did not yet have the language to describe. Her implant was being adjusted. Her vital signs were being logged. A woman whose name he had written on a ration card four hours earlier — *Dr. Sennae Vorlith*, who had been said to have stopped coming to Tartaros in 2369 — was reviewing Mouse's file and making small annotations in a hand that had not changed in twenty years.

The annotations used keywords. Not sentences. *Cohort retention. Substrate viability. Threshold response.* The keywords had been chosen carefully, decades ago, by people who had understood that the right phrase, repeated often enough in the right documents, becomes invisible to anyone without the cipher.

Kester had filed those phrases for nine years.

He had not yet realized that he had been carrying the cipher in his hands the entire time.

He fell asleep, finally, just before the air recyclers cycled into their dawn rotation. He dreamed of his daughter on Europa, walking on water-skids across the under-ice canals, holding a strawberry up to the sodium lamps to see the light through it. In the dream she turned to him and smiled, and her smile was Mouse's smile, and he woke up before he could decide what that meant.
