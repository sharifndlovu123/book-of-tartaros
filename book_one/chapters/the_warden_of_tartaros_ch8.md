# The Warden of Tartaros
## Chapter Eight: The Combination

They buried Tev in the deeper chamber the Undersiders had used for their dead since the beginning — a small room three levels below the main chamber, accessed through a narrow shaft, which had been a storage bay for ore-handling equipment in the prison's earliest years and which had been converted, ten years ago, into something that the Undersiders did not have a word for and that an outsider would have recognized as a chapel.

There were small alcoves in the walls. Some of the alcoves held photographs. Some held small objects — a comb, a medal, a folded letter. Some were empty. The empty ones were waiting.

Tev was laid out on the central plinth with her hands folded across her chest in the same configuration she had folded Aleya's hands the night before. Someone had brushed her hair. Someone — Kester suspected Davour — had placed a small dried flower from the chamber's hydroponic stock between her fingers. The flower was white and waxy and the kind of flower that did not need much light to grow. The Undersiders had been growing it for fifteen years, Chayne told Kester later, for exactly this purpose.

The whole community was there. The seven survivors. The four who had escaped through the deeper passages and trickled back overnight. Ten of them counting Kester. Sten was carried in on a litter by two of the unnamed survivors — a man called Dren and a woman called Hess, whose names Kester learned that morning and whose faces he would carry forward without yet knowing them — and Sten was placed on the chamber's only chair. He could not stand. He sat with his bandaged side and his bandaged thigh and his eyes very far away, and he did not speak.

Chayne stood at the head of the plinth.

He spoke for perhaps two minutes. He did not say much. He said Tev had been a nurse. He said she had nursed in the medical wing of Building Twelve for two years before her transfer to general population, and she had nursed in the under-levels for fifteen years after, and that in those years she had cared for ninety-three Undersiders that he could name and probably more that he could not. He said she had held the hands of the dying when there was no one else to hold them, and she had held the hands of the living when the living had needed it, and the difference between the two had not always been clear to her in the moment but it had not mattered, because Tev had been a woman who held hands and who did not require the situation to have been clarified before she did so. He said she had sung to a child the night before. He said the child had been a stranger to her but that Tev had treated the child as a granddaughter for the six hours she had with her, because that was the kind of work Tev did, and the work did not require relationship, only attention. He said *thank you, Tev*, and his voice did not break, and he stepped back.

Sable did not speak.

Kester did not speak.

The community stood in silence for a long moment. Then, one by one, the survivors approached the plinth and placed something — a small object, a hand on Tev's shoulder, a whispered word. Pira was carried up by Sable. The child looked at Tev for a long time. She did not seem afraid. Sable whispered something to her, and Pira reached out one small hand and touched Tev's hand, the one with the flower, and Pira said, in her clear small voice: *Goodbye*.

Then Sable carried her back.

The community sealed the alcove. Tev's photograph — there had been one in her medical kit, Davour found it during the cleanup — was placed in the wall. The unnamed dead, eight of them, were placed in alcoves of their own. The community dispersed, slowly, and went back to the main chamber.

The whole ceremony took less than an hour.

It was, Kester thought, the most carefully sufficient ritual he had ever witnessed. There was nothing he could have added to it. There was nothing he could have taken away.

---

He went to the drop with Ivo at twenty-two hundred.

The drop point was three levels above the chamber, in a maintenance recess Ivo had identified that morning — *a place I've used for tool storage*, he said, *the kind of place an Internal Security operator would leave something if they were trying to be casual about it*. Kester accepted the explanation without questioning it. Ivo had been opening locks and finding shafts for fifteen years. His judgment about drop locations was not something Kester had reason to doubt.

They went in silence. Ivo led. Kester followed.

The maintenance recess was a small alcove behind a service panel that Ivo opened in seven seconds with a tool he kept in his belt. Inside the alcove, on a small shelf, was a data slate.

Ivo pulled it down. He handed it to Kester.

"It's yours," Ivo said.

Kester took the slate.

He turned it on. He read what was on it. He read it twice. He did not speak.

Ivo, behind him in the corridor, was doing something with a maintenance panel ten meters down the way. Kester registered this peripherally — Ivo was always doing something with maintenance panels, it was the man's natural state — and did not turn around to look. He was reading.

The slate contained a transport schedule. A single passenger of interest, manifest designation *Subject M-19* — Mouse, he understood, with the small cold lurch that had become familiar — would depart Tartaros at oh-four hundred local time on the morning of the fifth day from now. Departure point: the surface launch facility designated *Pier Three*, located approximately forty kilometers north of Facility Nine, accessible by the maintained vehicle road over the Vorrath Range. Destination: redacted in the slate's display, but the transit duration was given as *approximately fourteen hours*, which placed the destination — Kester ran the math against what he knew of Hegemony shuttle drives — far around the curve of the world, at a site whose coordinates had been struck from every chart he had ever filed.

The Harvester.

Below the schedule, in a small text field, was a brief note. The handwriting was Orrel's — Kester recognized it from nine years of administrative paperwork — and the note read:

*Mr. Vaile,*

*The schedule above is accurate. Pier Three is sparsely garrisoned at this time; resource demands at the other sites have thinned the security presence considerably. A Hegemony military shuttle will be available in Bay Two and is fueled for the transit duration. Authorization codes for ignition are appended below.*

*Mouse's transit will be on the same shuttle, arriving at Pier Three from the citadel approximately thirty minutes before launch. She will be sedated for the journey but stable. Her presence on the shuttle should not be a complication for your party.*

*I have arranged for your arrival at the Harvester to be uncontested. Customs processing will accept the manifest you carry. You will be met at the receiving bay by an aide who will escort you to the residence wing. I will see you there.*

*Bring whoever you like. I would ask, only, that whatever weapons you bring be left at the receiving bay's secure storage. This is courtesy, not coercion. You will be given them back when you depart, regardless of what you decide.*

*— B.O.*

*P.S. I look forward to it.*

Kester stood in the corridor and read the note three times.

The hand was Orrel's. The tone was Orrel's. The offer was Orrel's. There was nothing in any of it that surprised him.

What surprised him was the *generosity*. The shuttle would be in the bay. Authorization codes were appended. The launch facility was thinly staffed. Mouse would be on board. The arrival would be uncontested. They would even be allowed to bring weapons, only to surrender them at receiving — *courtesy, not coercion* — which Kester recognized, as a man who had filed Orrel's correspondence for nine years, as Orrel's particular way of saying *we both know how this works*.

The whole thing was an open door.

The door was held open by a man who knew, with a confidence Kester could not yet match, what would happen when they walked through it.

Behind him, Ivo finished whatever he had been doing with the maintenance panel — Kester heard the small click of a panel being closed — and Ivo said, in his level quiet voice: *Got what you needed?*

"Yes," Kester said.

"Then let's go."

They went back down to the chamber.

Kester did not know, then, that Ivo's adjustment to the maintenance panel had been a check-in. He would not know it for another day.

---

The reading took place in the main chamber at midnight.

The survivors gathered around the long salvaged table where Kester's ration cards still lay in their underlined forest. Yenna brought her satchel from the palace. Chayne brought his drives, retrieved from the ventilation duct, the panels still bearing the sticky smudges where his hands had pulled them open in the night of the attack. Sten was carried over. Pira was asleep in a folding cot beside Sable's chair. Resh stood at the back of the chamber and did not sit down. Davour stood at the door and did not sit down. Ivo sat at the table.

The reading lasted six hours.

Yenna led. She had the documents organized — she had spent the day cross-indexing what she had photographed in the palace against what was in her notebooks, and she had a working hypothesis about the keyword cipher. She presented the hypothesis. Chayne tested it against his archive. Kester tested it against his memory of the paperwork he had signed. Sable tested it against the names of the dead.

It worked.

The cipher was elegant in the way bureaucratic ciphers are elegant — not difficult, just *consistent*. *Substrate* meant child. *Cohort* meant the collected children of a single research generation. *Retention* meant survival. *Yield* meant transferred to the Harvester. *Recovery* meant death and biological material extracted. *Threshold* meant the developmental stage at which a child became viable for the next phase. *Augmentation* meant the procedure itself. *Integration* meant the implantation of the behavioral compliance system Mouse had carried in her neck since infancy. *Programming* meant — and here Kester read the word three times to be sure — meant the doctrinal preparation, the years of carefully calibrated exposure to violence and grief and tested loyalties, that produced a finished subject.

Mouse had been *programmed*. The word was on her file. She had been programmed for nineteen years. The forced witnessing of the chamber attack, two nights ago, had been logged in her file as *terminal phase orientation, witnessing protocol, satisfactory*.

Sable read this. She did not respond. She continued reading.

Chayne's archive contained, the four of them now realized, the *low-value* version of the same operation. The Board had been allowing certain kinds of documents to leak into circulation for years, and Chayne had been faithfully collecting them, and the documents had been chosen by the Board's information officers specifically because they would mislead a determined reader into the wrong conclusions. Chayne's reading of the archive over fifteen years had produced a picture of a corrupt prison administration with brutal medical practices and political dissident persecution. That picture was true as far as it went. It did not contain the Cleanse. It did not contain the Harvester. It did not contain the seven sites or the cohort transport or the fourteen thousand children.

But Chayne's archive *did* contain — and this was the realization that took the four of them an hour of cross-referencing to reach — the names of the principals. Not under their initials. Under their *cover identities*. The Board members, when they were referred to in low-classification documents, were referred to by the public roles they held in the Hegemony's official structure. *A.M.* in the palace papers was, in Chayne's archive, *Senior Director Adran Marse, Hegemony Penal Affairs*. *D.K.* was *Senior Auditor Davreen Khoul, Hegemony Office of Federation Liaison*. *R.T.* was *Director Rion Tavers, Cresswell University Research Council* — Yenna's old institution, the source of Yenna's grant, the office that had signed off on her arrest. Yenna read the name. She closed her eyes for a long moment. She did not say anything. She kept reading.

The cover identities all checked out. The Board members were not shadow figures. They were *senior public officials*, every one of them, holding positions that gave them legitimate access to the institutional levers the Cleanse required — penal administration, oversight management, academic legitimization, financial routing, military procurement. The Board did not need to operate in secret because the Board *was* the system. They were doing the work in the open. The work simply did not look like what it was.

Eight names. The ninth was the Architect.

The Architect's cover identity was not in Chayne's archive.

The Architect had been careful.

By oh-three hundred the four of them — Kester, Yenna, Chayne, and now Sable, who had stopped contributing names and had started contributing *patterns* — had assembled a working portrait of the Cleanse from the operational level upward. Seven sites, each producing a cohort of children. The cohorts converging at the Harvester on a roughly biennial schedule. The Harvester's purpose: producing a class of augmented operatives — not soldiers in the conventional sense, but *finished subjects*, individuals with embedded behavioral compliance, conditioned loyalty, and physical augmentations that made them deployable in environments and operational categories ordinary infantry could not survive. Approximately fourteen thousand subjects produced over twenty-two years. The Hegemony had been quietly absorbing these subjects into specialized military, intelligence, and security roles for over a decade. They were already in the field. They were already operating. Some of them, by now, were probably commanding others.

The next war, when it came, would be fought partly by them.

Kester sat in his chair and looked at what they had assembled and understood, with a clarity that arrived too late and too completely to be of any use, that the broadcast had been a small thing.

The broadcast had revealed Wing Seven. Wing Seven was one wing, on one site, of seven sites, run by one Board, that had been operating for twenty-two years. The broadcast had been a single drop of water thrown at a fire that had been burning since before Kester had children of his own.

He looked at Sable.

She was looking at the documents.

She was nodding, slowly, the way a person nods who has finally been given the picture they have been demanding for years and who is not surprised by the picture, only tired by it.

She said, very quietly: "The archive has to go home."

"Yes," Yenna said.

"Andra Prime. Federation observers. Opposition press. Adeyemi's family. Whoever you can reach, Yenna. The picture has to be assembled there. They cannot spin a fourteen-thousand-children dataset. They can spin Wing Seven. They cannot spin this."

"I know."

"You will go. Chayne will go with you. Pira will go with you."

Yenna looked up. "Pira — "

"The Harvester is no place for her. Andra Prime is no place for her either, but it is more of a place for her than the Harvester. She will go with you. Chayne can carry her when she gets tired. You will be a family of three traveling. The Hegemony's surveillance net is degraded — Yenna, the file the Board has on you is two years old, there are paths in the system you can use. You know the paths. You know the names." Sable was not looking at Yenna directly. She was looking at the papers. "You will go on the morning shuttle from the launch base, after we take the base. You will fly under a civilian transit code we will manufacture for you. You will have eighteen hours of invisibility before the Hegemony pieces together what we did at the base. Use them. Get to the surface of Andra Prime. Get to your contacts. Begin the work."

"Sable, the rest of you — "

"We will go to the Harvester. We will get Mouse. We will get whatever evidence we can carry. We will come back." Sable paused. "Or we will not. Either way, the archive has to be on Andra Prime when the Hegemony figures out what has happened. The archive does not depend on our return. The archive *cannot* depend on our return. Do you understand."

Yenna was crying, silently, the way she had cried in the palace.

"I understand," she said.

"Good."

Sable stood up.

"We move at first light," she said. "Sleep if you can. Pack what you need. Yenna, write down your contacts on Andra Prime tonight, in case — in case the journey is harder than we hope. Make sure two people know the names. Chayne should have them. So should Sten, if he is staying."

"Sten is staying," Sten said, from his litter. His voice was thin but clear. "I cannot travel and I would slow you down. Dren and Hess will stay with me. We will hold what we can hold of the chamber for as long as we can hold it, and if the Board comes back we will move deeper. There are passages I have not shown you, Sable. There are passages even Ivo does not know about. I have been keeping them in reserve. I will keep them in reserve."

Sable looked at Sten for a long moment.

"Thank you, Sten."

"Get the girl, Sable. Get the truth home. The rest of us will be here, or we will not, and either way you will have done what you went to do."

Sable nodded.

She walked away from the table.

The reading was over.

---

The hours before dawn were quiet.

Yenna sat with Chayne in a corner of the chamber and made her list of contacts on Andra Prime. There were six names. Two of them were academics who had survived after Yenna's arrest. One was a journalist who had written about disappeared persons under a pen name and who Yenna believed was Adeyemi's primary source in the Federation press. One was Adeyemi's husband — a man Yenna had never met but whose name she had known for years from the dedications in his wife's published reports, a man who would now be in mourning and would also be, perhaps, capable of acting on what they brought him. One was a sympathetic lawyer in the Federation oversight apparatus. One was a former colleague of Chayne's from the municipal archives on Sephara, who had escaped his arrest and was rumored to be living quietly under a different name on Andra Prime's outer settlements.

Six names. Six possible doors. Yenna wrote them down and gave one copy to Chayne and one copy to Sable and one copy to Sten, and Sten hid his copy in a place even Dren and Hess did not know about, because Sten was a man whose paranoia had now been validated by the chamber attack and who was no longer willing to assume the chamber was a safe place for paper.

Kester did not sleep.

He sat at his table with his ration cards and he wrote, for the last time, what he had learned. He did not know if the cards would survive. He did not know if anything in the chamber would survive. He wrote anyway, because writing was the work he could do, and the work was the only thing that had not yet been taken from him.

He wrote about the seven sites. He wrote about the Harvester. He wrote the cover identities of the Board principals. He wrote the keyword cipher in full, two columns, code on the left and meaning on the right. He wrote Mouse's transport schedule. He wrote Pier Three. He wrote what was in Orrel's note, word for word, because his memory was good and because the note itself was now the slate that Sable had taken into her own keeping, and Kester did not know whether he would see it again.

He wrote, at the bottom of the last card, in capital letters: *THE BOARD HAS BEEN CHANNELING US. EVERY MOVE WE HAVE MADE SINCE THE BROADCAST WAS ON RAILS THEY LAID. THE HARVESTER IS THE END OF THE RAIL. WE ARE GOING ANYWAY.*

He underlined the last sentence.

He set the pencil down.

He looked at Sable across the chamber.

She was sitting beside Pira's cot. She was not sleeping. She was watching the child sleep, the way a person watches a child sleep when they are trying to memorize the face. Pira's small mouth was slightly open. Pira's hand was curled against her cheek. Sable was sitting with her hands open on her knees, palms up, the way she had sat on the chamber floor the morning Kester had found her.

Kester understood, watching her, that Sable had been spending the last few hours saying goodbye to Pira in the way she could — silently, by sitting beside her, by not waking her, by being present for the hours she would never have again. Sable would do the verbal goodbye in the morning, briefly, because Pira was four and would not benefit from a long farewell. The real goodbye was happening now, in the quiet, in the chamber where everyone else was busy with maps and lists and lasts.

Kester turned away.

He thought about his own daughter, Lira, on Andra Prime. He thought about her being twenty-two years old and not knowing whether her father was alive. He thought about the strawberries under sodium lamps. He thought, with the small cold administrative clarity he had been using for years to avoid feeling things, that if Yenna succeeded in her mission, then within perhaps a month Lira would learn that her father was the man whose paperwork had built Wing Seven, and that the learning would be public, and that it would happen to her in a city of sixty million people most of whom would be reading the same news at the same time.

He did not know what she would do with it.

He did not know what he wanted her to do with it.

He closed his eyes.

He thought: *If you hate me afterward, you will be right to hate me. If you understand, eventually, why I am at the Harvester rather than coming home to you, you will be a kinder daughter than I deserved. Either way, Lira. Either way, I love you. I have always loved you. I just spent nine years not letting it matter, and now it is too late to make it matter in any way that will reach you in time.*

He kept his eyes closed for a long time.

---

Dawn was an abstraction in the under-levels, but the chamber's clock said oh-six-hundred when Sable said *we go*, and the survivors moved.

Sten, on his litter, was carried back to the deeper rooms by Dren and Hess. Sable knelt beside the litter for a moment. She did not speak. She put her hand on Sten's hand, and he closed his fingers around hers, and they stayed like that for perhaps thirty seconds. Then she stood up and walked away.

The split happened at the chamber's door.

Yenna was carrying her satchel of palace papers and a sealed case of the photograph drives, copied from the originals, with a backup buried in a panel Sten had prepared in the deeper rooms. Chayne was carrying a smaller bag of his own — his archive's original drives, his notebooks, a few personal items Kester did not see. Pira was held in Sable's arms. She had been awake for ten minutes. She was eating a small piece of dried fruit Tev had set aside two days ago, which Davour had retrieved from Tev's medical station that morning without saying why she was retrieving it.

Sable carried Pira to Chayne. She knelt down so the child was at Chayne's eye level. Chayne, in his folding chair, opened his arms.

"Pira," Sable said, very softly.

"Yes."

"This is Chayne. You know Chayne. You are going to go with Chayne for a while. Chayne is going to take care of you. He is going to take you to a place that is warm and that has food and that has people who will be kind to you. Yenna is going with you. Yenna is going to help."

Pira looked at Chayne. She had known him her whole life. She did not seem confused.

"Where are you going," she said to Sable.

"I am going somewhere else, sweetheart. I have to do something. I will come find you when I am done."

"When."

Sable did not answer immediately. She was looking at Pira's face. Her own face was, Kester saw, very still — not soft anymore, not exactly, but *held*, in the way a person holds their face when they are determined not to lie to a child but also cannot afford to tell the truth.

"As soon as I can," Sable said. "I do not know how long. But as soon as I can. I promise."

Pira considered this.

"Will you remember me," Pira said.

Sable made a sound. It was not quite a sob. It was the sound a person makes when they have been hit very hard and are trying not to make the louder sound that comes after.

"Yes," she said. "Yes. I will remember you. I will remember you every day. I will remember your name and your face and the way you said *goodbye* to Tev yesterday, and I will remember everything, Pira. I will remember everything."

"Okay."

Pira turned in Sable's arms and reached for Chayne. Chayne took her. The transfer was quick and gentle and the kind of transfer Pira had clearly experienced many times in her short life — handed from one set of arms to another by adults who had decided things she was too small to know about. She curled against Chayne's shoulder. She did not cry. She put one hand into Chayne's beard and held on.

Sable stood up.

She did not look at Pira again.

She turned to Yenna. "Go. The civilian transit shuttle leaves at oh-eight hundred from the base's east bay. Davour will brief you on the timing while we are en route. Once you are in the air you do not stop until you are on Andra Prime. Do you understand."

"Yes."

"I am sorry, Yenna. I am sorry to send you with this much. I am sorry I am not coming with you."

"Sable."

"Yes."

"You are coming back. Tell me you are coming back."

Sable looked at her.

"I will try," she said.

It was not what Yenna had asked for. Yenna's face registered that. Yenna nodded anyway, because Yenna was a woman who had stopped requiring the people she loved to lie to her.

Sable turned to Chayne. She put her hand on his shoulder, lightly. She did not speak. He did not speak. He nodded to her once, and she nodded once back, and that was the goodbye between them — the shortest one, between two people who had read each other's archives and who did not need words to mark the parting.

Chayne carried Pira out, with Yenna walking beside him, and Davour following with their small cases.

The remaining party — Sable, Kester, Resh, Ivo, and Davour, who would return after seeing Yenna's group to the shuttle — assembled at the chamber's south exit and began the trek to the surface.

Kester looked back, once, before they left.

The chamber was quiet. Sten's litter was no longer visible. The salvaged table where the reading had happened was still strewn with papers — Yenna had taken what she needed, Kester had taken his ration cards, but the bulk of the working materials remained, abandoned, the surface of an investigation no one would be coming back to. The ceiling lights hummed. The air recyclers cycled. The chamber had been a society for ten years and was now a room.

He turned away.

He kept walking.

---

The surface of Tartaros was cold in a way Kester's body had forgotten.

He had not been on the surface in eleven years. He had forgotten the feeling of the suit — the slight pressure imbalance against the skin, the close hum of the rebreather, the way the helmet's faceplate fogged at the edges if you breathed too hard. He had forgotten the *light*. The light on Tartaros was not light. It was a kind of brown-red haze, the sun a small white smear behind the moon's perpetual atmospheric dust, and the shadows fell long and ambiguous across the surface in a way that made distances impossible to judge.

Above him, Moloch hung in its place.

It was the first time Kester had seen Moloch in years.

The gas giant filled half the visible sky. It was so large and so close that the edge of it curved away beyond the horizon in both directions, and the great red eye — the perpetual storm Kester had seen from the tram window what felt like a lifetime ago — was directly overhead, watching him from above with the slow indifference of weather. Kester stood on the surface of Tartaros in his pressure suit and looked up at Moloch and thought, with the small clear discipline of a man who had spent his life filing things, *my daughter has seen this from her window every evening of her life. She has been looking at the front of this. I have been working on the back of it. We have been on the same side of the same star for twenty-two years and we have never seen it together.*

The vehicle was a tracked transport — the kind the Hegemony used for surface maintenance work, the kind Kester had filed maintenance reports for during his early years on Tartaros. Davour drove. Sable sat in the passenger seat. Kester, Resh, and Ivo sat in the rear cabin with the gear.

The road over the Vorrath Range was a maintained route — graded, marked, swept by automated rovers — and the transport could make Pier Three in approximately three hours. They had time. They were not yet behind a clock.

Kester sat in the rear cabin and watched the surface scroll past the small armored window. He thought about Lira. He thought about the strawberries. He thought about the version of his life in which he had stayed on Andra Prime, in which he had refused the Tartaros posting eleven years ago, in which he had been a junior administrator in some municipal office and had eaten dinner with his daughter every night. The version of his life would have been small. The version would have been ordinary. The version would have been, he understood now, the only version worth wanting.

He had not wanted it at the time. He had wanted the senior posting and the better salary and the chance to advance. He had wanted, as he understood now, the specific thing Orrel had wanted after Calderon Trench: to stop being a body in someone else's accounting. He had not realized that the alternative to being a body was being the man who counted them.

He sat with this for the duration of the drive.

He did not say anything.

Sable, in the front, did not say anything either. Davour drove. Resh stared out the window. Ivo, beside Kester, occasionally checked the transport's cabin systems with the small pre-occupied attention of a man who was used to checking things.

Pier Three rose on the horizon two hours and forty minutes after they left.

It was, as Orrel had described, sparsely garrisoned. Kester counted, through the binoculars Davour passed him, perhaps fifteen guard silhouettes patrolling the perimeter — about a third of what a properly staffed launch facility this size would have. The Board's resource demands at the other sites were real. The base was running thin.

*Or it had been thinned for them*, Kester thought.

He looked at Sable.

Sable had the binoculars now. She was studying the base. Her face under the helmet visor was unreadable.

"It looks like he said it would," she said, over the suit comm.

"Yes," Davour said.

"That makes me trust it less, not more."

"Yes."

"We go anyway."

"Yes."

She lowered the binoculars.

She turned her head, slightly, and looked at Kester. He met her eyes through the visor. She did not say anything. She did not have to. He understood, with the kind of mute administrative clarity he had been using for hours now, that she was telling him: *I know this is a road they laid. I know we are going down it. I am going down it anyway. So are you. We will see each other on the other side or we will not. Either way, this is what we are doing.*

He nodded, once.

She turned back to the base.

"Davour," she said. "Brief us."

Davour began the briefing.

The chapter was over and the next thing was beginning, and Kester sat in the rear of a tracked transport on the surface of Tartaros under the great red eye of Moloch and listened to Kell Davour, formerly of the Hegemony Military Police, walk them through the assault on a launch base whose vulnerabilities had been written specifically for them.
